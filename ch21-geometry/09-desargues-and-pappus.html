21.9  Desargues and Pappus

Two theorems from the drawing board, both of them older than coordinates. Both speak only of points, lines and incidence: no lengths, no angles, nothing a projective transformation could disturb. Both are proved here by one algebraic move, the one that makes projective arguments short — choosing, out of the whole line of vectors that represents a point, the single vector that makes the hypothesis into an equation.

21.9.1. Perspective from a point, perspective from a line

Draw a triangle on a sheet of glass and let a lamp cast its shadow on the floor. The two triangles that result have their corresponding vertices joined by three lines through the lamp. That is the first of the two relations below. The second is what the same picture looks like when you attend to the sides instead of the vertices: corresponding sides, extended, meet the ground in three points, and those three points lie on one line.

Both relations need the words “the line through two points”, and there is exactly one: distinct points A=[a] and B=[b] of P(V) have independent representatives and lie on the unique line P(span(a,b)), by Proposition 21.6.8. We write AB for it, and we use that description of it, as P(span(a,b)), in every proof below.

Definition 21.9.1 (Triangles, and the Two Perspectives)

Let dim⁡V=3, so that P(V) is a projective plane (Definition 21.6.3). A triangle in P(V) is an ordered triple (A,B,C) of non-collinear points, called its vertices; its sides are the three lines AB, BC, CA.

Let (A,B,C) and (A′,B′,C′) be triangles with A≠A′, B≠B′ and C≠C′. They are:

  • (a) perspective from a point if some point O lies on all three of the lines AA′, BB′, CC′; such an O is a center;
  • (b) perspective from a line if the three pairs of corresponding sides {AB,A′B′}, {BC,B′C′}, {CA,C′A′} consist of distinct lines and the three intersection points AB∩A′B′,BC∩B′C′,CA∩C′A′ are collinear; a line through all three is an axis.

In (b) each intersection is a single point: two distinct lines in a projective plane meet in exactly one point (Corollary 21.6.7). That is why the clause “distinct in each pair” is part of the definition and not an afterthought; without it the phrase “the point AB∩A′B′” names nothing.

The picture shows both relations at once: the three gray rays make the triangles perspective from O, and the three marked points R=AB∩A′B′, P=BC∩B′C′, Q=CA∩C′A′ lie on the thick line. Desargues’s theorem says that this coincidence is no coincidence.

Warning. Two corresponding sides may be the same line, and then part (b) is not even a statement. Take a line ℓ, four distinct points A,B,A′,B′ on it and a point O on it, and any C∉ℓ. Fix representatives o of O and c of C and put C′=[o+c], a point of the line OC other than O and C; it does not lie on ℓ, since OC≠ℓ and the two meet only at O (Corollary 21.6.7). The triples (A,B,C) and (A′,B′,C′) are triangles, the six vertices are distinct, and the three lines AA′, BB′, CC′ all pass through O. But AB=ℓ=A′B′, so “the point AB∩A′B′” does not exist. Exercise (C3: Why the side hypothesis is there) writes this out in coordinates. The hypothesis that rules it out is the one in the theorem below: no vertex of either triangle lies on a side of the other.

21.9.2. Choosing the representatives

A point of P(V) is a whole line of vectors, and every proof has to pick one vector per point. The pick is free, and freedom used well is the whole art. Here the hypothesis is three collinearities, and each of them becomes a clean equation as soon as the two representatives involved are scaled against each other.

Lemma 21.9.1 (Scaling Three Collinear Points)

Let W⊆V be a 2-dimensional subspace and let O,A,A′ be three distinct points of the line P(W). Let o be any non-zero vector representing O. Then there are vectors a representing A and a′ representing A′ with a′=a+o.

Idea. Two distinct points of a line give a basis of W, so the third is a combination of them. Both coefficients are non-zero, precisely because the third point is neither of the first two; and scaling the two representatives by the right amounts turns that combination into a sum with coefficients 1 and 1.

Proof. Pick any non-zero a0 representing A. Since O≠A, the pair (o,a0) is independent, and it lies in W with dim⁡W=2, so it is a basis of W. As A′∈P(W), a representative a0′ of A′ can be written a0′=μa0+νo,μ,ν∈F. Here ν≠0: otherwise a0′=μa0 with μ≠0, giving A′=A. And μ≠0: otherwise a0′=νo with ν≠0, giving A′=O. Both are excluded because the three points are distinct.

Put a:=(μ/ν)a0 and a′:=(1/ν)a0′, which is legitimate since μ/ν and 1/ν are non-zero scalars, so a still represents A and a′ still represents A′. Then a′=1ν(μa0+νo)=μνa0+o=a+o. This proves the lemma.

It is Lemma 21.7.4 for n=1, with the one common scalar spent on fixing o first. The lemma is used three times in a row, always with the same o. That is the point: o is chosen once and never rescaled again, while each pair (a,a′), (b,b′), (c,c′) is scaled to match it.

Quick check. In the lemma, which conclusions survive if we drop the hypothesis A′≠O? And if we drop A′≠A?

Solution. Neither survives. If A′=O we may take a0′=o, so μ=0; and the conclusion fails, because every representative a′ of A′=O is a multiple of o, so a=a′−o would lie in span(o), forcing a=0 or A=[a]=O, and both are impossible. If A′=A we may take a0′=a0, so ν=0, and a′=a+o would say that two representatives of the same point A differ by o; subtracting, o would be a multiple of a, making O=A, again excluded. Each of the two scalars μ,ν is guarded by exactly one of the two hypotheses.

21.9.3. Desargues’s theorem

The forward half of Desargues’s theorem needs less than the full configuration, and stating it with its minimal hypotheses is what makes it reusable: the converse will apply it to a different pair of triangles built from the same picture.

Lemma 21.9.2 (Perspective from a Point Gives an Axis)

Let dim⁡V=3. Let (A,B,C) and (A′,B′,C′) be triangles in P(V) with A≠A′, B≠B′, C≠C′, and suppose:

  • (i) some point O, distinct from all six vertices, lies on each of AA′, BB′, CC′;
  • (ii) AB≠A′B′, BC≠B′C′ and CA≠C′A′.

Then the three points AB∩A′B′, BC∩B′C′, CA∩C′A′ are collinear.

Idea. Fix one representative o of O. By Lemma 21.9.1 the three collinearities can be written as a′=a+o, b′=b+o, c′=c+o for suitable representatives. Subtract two of them and o cancels: a−b=a′−b′. One vector, lying in span(a,b) and in span(a′,b′) at once, so its point lies on both AB and A′B′. The three such vectors add to 0, so they span at most a plane; the triangles being genuine keeps them from collapsing to a line, so they span exactly a plane, and a plane of V is a line of P(V).

Proof. Fix a non-zero o representing O. The points O,A,A′ are distinct, by (i) and by A≠A′, and they are collinear, since O∈AA′ and AA′=P(W) with dim⁡W=2. So Lemma 21.9.1 gives representatives a of A and a′ of A′ with a′=a+o. The same lemma, with the same o, gives b′=b+o and c′=c+o.

Subtracting, a−b=a′−b′. Now a−b≠0, since a=b would give A=B, contradicting that (A,B,C) is a triangle. Since a−b∈span(a,b) and AB=P(span(a,b)), the point [a−b] lies on AB; since a−b=a′−b′∈span(a′,b′), it lies on A′B′ as well. By (ii) these two lines are distinct, so by Corollary 21.6.7 they meet in exactly one point, and therefore AB∩A′B′=[a−b]. The same argument, with the letters advanced cyclically, gives BC∩B′C′=[b−c] and CA∩C′A′=[c−a].

Let U=span(a−b, b−c, c−a). The three vectors sum to 0, so U=span(a−b, b−c) and dim⁡U≤2. Moreover dim⁡U=2: if a−b=t(b−c) for some t∈F, then a=(1+t)b−tc∈span(b,c), so A would lie on BC, contradicting that (A,B,C) is a triangle; and b−c≠0 for the same reason as before. So P(U) is a line, and it contains all three points. This proves the lemma.

Now the configuration of Definition 21.9.1, with the one extra hypothesis that kept the warning above at bay.

Theorem 21.9.3 (Desargues’s Theorem)

Let dim⁡V=3 and let (A,B,C) and (A′,B′,C′) be triangles in P(V) such that

  • (i) the six vertices are distinct, and
  • (ii) no vertex of either triangle lies on a side of the other.

Then the two triangles are perspective from a point if and only if they are perspective from a line.

Idea. ① The hypotheses (i) and (ii) are exactly what is needed to feed Lemma 21.9.2: (ii) forces corresponding sides apart, and — less obviously — it also forces a center to miss all six vertices, because a center sitting on a vertex would drag a vertex of one triangle onto a side of the other. ② For the converse there is no second theorem to prove: the same lemma is applied to the auxiliary triangles (A,A′,Q) and (B,B′,P), which are perspective from R because the axis passes through R. Their corresponding sides meet in AA′∩BB′, in C′ and in C; collinearity of those three puts AA′∩BB′ on the line CC′, which is concurrency.

Proof. Throughout, (ii) is used in the form: none of A,B,C lies on A′B′, B′C′ or C′A′, and none of A′,B′,C′ lies on AB, BC or CA.

First, the three pairs of corresponding sides are distinct. If AB=A′B′ then A′∈AB, contrary to (ii); the other two pairs are the same argument with the letters advanced.

(⇒) Suppose O lies on AA′, BB′, CC′. We check that O is none of the six vertices. If O=A, then A∈BB′; since A≠B, the line through A and B is AB, and it contains B′, so B′∈AB, contrary to (ii). If O=B, then B∈AA′ gives A′∈AB in the same way, and if O=C then C∈AA′ gives A′∈CA; both contradict (ii). If O=A′, then A′∈BB′; since A′≠B′, the line through them is A′B′, and it contains B, so B∈A′B′, contrary to (ii). If O=B′ then B′∈AA′ gives A∈A′B′, and if O=C′ then C′∈AA′ gives A∈C′A′; both contradict (ii). So Lemma 21.9.2 applies, and its conclusion is that the two triangles are perspective from a line.

(⇐) Suppose the three points R=AB∩A′B′,P=BC∩B′C′,Q=CA∩C′A′ are collinear. We apply Lemma 21.9.2 to the triangles (A,A′,Q) and (B,B′,P), with center R, and verify its hypotheses one at a time.

The points Q and P are not vertices. If Q=A then A∈C′A′, and if Q=A′ then A′∈CA; both contradict (ii). Likewise P≠B and P≠B′.

Both triples are triangles. Suppose A,A′,Q were collinear. Since Q∈CA and Q≠A, the line QA is CA; so AA′=QA=CA, whence A′∈CA, contrary to (ii). The triple (B,B′,P) is handled the same way.

Corresponding vertices are distinct. A≠B and A′≠B′ by (i). If Q=P, that point lies on CA and on CB; these two lines are distinct (else A,B,C would be collinear) and both contain C, so by Corollary 21.6.7 they meet only at C, giving Q=C and hence C∈C′A′, contrary to (ii).

The center R is none of the six vertices A,A′,Q,B,B′,P. If R=A then A∈A′B′; if R=B then B∈A′B′; if R=A′ then A′∈AB; if R=B′ then B′∈AB. All four contradict (ii). If R=Q, then this point lies on AB and on CA, two distinct lines through A, so R=A, already excluded; if R=P, the same argument with AB and BC gives R=B, also excluded.

R lies on the three joins. It lies on AB and on A′B′ by definition. Since Q≠P, the line QP is defined, and it contains R because P,Q,R are collinear.

Corresponding sides of the two auxiliary triangles are distinct. If AA′=BB′, this one line contains A and B, hence equals AB, and then A′∈AB, contrary to (ii). Next, Q∈C′A′ and Q≠A′ give A′Q=C′A′, and P∈B′C′ with P≠B′ give B′P=B′C′; these are distinct, since C′A′=B′C′ would make A′,B′,C′ collinear. Finally QA=CA and PB=CB, distinct for the same reason.

By Lemma 21.9.2 the three points X=AA′∩BB′,A′Q∩B′P=C′A′∩B′C′=C′,QA∩PB=CA∩CB=C are collinear, the last two evaluations being Corollary 21.6.7 applied to two distinct lines with an evident common point. Since C≠C′ by (i), the line through C and C′ is CC′, and collinearity puts X on it. So X lies on AA′, on BB′ and on CC′: the triangles are perspective from the point X. This proves the theorem.

Two routes to Desargues’s theorem are standard. One embeds the plane in P3, moves one triangle off the plane, and uses that two distinct planes of P3 meet in a line; Exercise (C1: Desargues in space) carries that route out. The proof above is the other, purely two-dimensional route, and the step that decides whether it works is the scaling of Lemma 21.9.1 — one representative o fixed first, every other representative matched to it afterwards.

Remark. The two halves of the theorem are dual statements: interchange “point” with “line”, “lies on” with “passes through”, and “perspective from a point” becomes “perspective from a line”. Read through the correspondence of Theorem 21.8.3, the converse should therefore be the dual of the direct half rather than an independent fact; we do not carry that dualization out here, and nothing depends on it. The proof above does not use it; it derives the converse from the direct half by applying it to a second pair of triangles, so that nothing rests on how far the duality principle of Theorem 21.8.6 can be pushed as a general statement.

Example (Desargues with exact coordinates)

In P2(Q) take O=[1:1:1] and the triangle A=[1:0:0], B=[0:1:0], C=[0:0:1], and on the three lines through O take A′=[3:1:1],B′=[1:4:1],C′=[1:1:2]. Verify the hypotheses, scale the representatives as in the proof, and find the axis.

Solution. Each primed point does lie on the corresponding line: (3,1,1)=2(1,0,0)+(1,1,1), (1,4,1)=3(0,1,0)+(1,1,1), and (1,1,2)=(0,0,1)+(1,1,1). Both triples are triangles, since (e0,e1,e2) is a basis and det(311141112)=17≠0. The six vertices are distinct. No vertex of either lies on a side of the other: the sides of ABC are the coordinate lines {x2=0}, {x0=0}, {x1=0}, and each primed point has all three coordinates non-zero; in the other direction the primed sides are A′B′={3x0+2x1−11x2=0},B′C′={7x0−x1−3x2=0},C′A′={−x0+5x1−2x2=0}, and no coefficient in any of them is zero, so none of e0,e1,e2 satisfies any of the three equations.

Now scale. With o=(1,1,1) the relation a0′=2a0+o has μ=2, ν=1, so the lemma takes a=2(1,0,0)=(2,0,0) and a′=(3,1,1); similarly b=(0,3,0), b′=(1,4,1) and c=(0,0,1), c′=(1,1,2). Each check is one subtraction: a′−a=b′−b=c′−c=(1,1,1)=o. The three intersection points are therefore R=[a−b]=[2:−3:0],P=[b−c]=[0:3:−1],Q=[c−a]=[−2:0:1], and indeed (2,−3,0)+(0,3,−1)+(−2,0,1)=0. A linear form vanishing on the first two is 3x0+2x1+6x2, and it vanishes on the third as well, so the axis is the line {3x0+2x1+6x2=0}.

21.9.4. Pappus’s theorem

Desargues’s theorem uses two triangles. Pappus’s uses two lines, with three points on each, and concludes that three points built by cross-joining them lie on a line. Nothing is assumed about the two triples beyond being distinct and avoiding the one point the two lines share.

The six thin lines are the cross-joins; they meet in pairs at R, Q and P, and the theorem says those three points always lie on one line, the thick one.

Theorem 21.9.4 (Pappus’s Theorem)

Let dim⁡V=3, let ℓ and m be distinct lines of P(V) and let O be their common point. Let A,B,C be three distinct points of ℓ, none equal to O, and let A′,B′,C′ be three distinct points of m, none equal to O. Then the three points R=AB′∩A′B,Q=AC′∩A′C,P=BC′∩B′C exist and are collinear.

Idea. Coordinates, in the one basis the data offers: the common point O and one direction along each line. Every point of the configuration then costs a single parameter, each cross-join is a 2×2 system, and the three answers come out as explicit vectors. What finishes the proof is not a determinant but an identity visible on the page: the vector for Q is the sum of the vectors for P and R.

Proof. Write ℓ=P(U) and m=P(W) with dim⁡U=dim⁡W=2. Since ℓ≠m we have U≠W (Lemma 21.6.4), and two distinct subspaces of the same dimension satisfy U⊈W, so U+W strictly contains W and has dimension 3=dim⁡V. By the dimension formula Theorem 1.7.3, dim⁡(U∩W)=2+2−3=1. Thus U∩W=span(o) for a non-zero o, and O=[o] is the unique common point of ℓ and m.

Choose u∈U∖span(o) and w∈W∖span(o). Then (o,u) is a basis of U and (o,w) is a basis of W, and (u,w,o) is a basis of V: it has three members and spans U+W=V. Every point of ℓ other than O is represented by a vector su+to with s≠0, hence, after dividing by s, by exactly one vector of the form u+to; and likewise on m. So there are scalars with A=[u+ao],B=[u+bo],C=[u+co],A′=[w+a′o],B′=[w+b′o],C′=[w+c′o], where a,b,c are distinct and a′,b′,c′ are distinct, because the representatives above are uniquely determined by the points.

The three intersections exist. The lines AB′ and A′B are defined, since A≠B′ and A′≠B: a point of ℓ other than O is not a point of m, as ℓ∩m={O}. They are distinct: if AB′=A′B, this line would contain A and B, two distinct points of ℓ, hence be ℓ; then A′∈ℓ∩m={O}, contradicting A′≠O. So R=AB′∩A′B is a single point (Corollary 21.6.7), and the same argument gives Q and P.

Computing R. A vector in span(u+ao, w+b′o) has the form λu+μw+(λa+μb′)o, and a vector in span(w+a′o, u+bo) has the form λ′u+μ′w+(λ′b+μ′a′)o. A non-zero vector lying in both must have λ=λ′ and μ=μ′, the triple (u,w,o) being a basis, and then λa+μb′=λb+μa′⟺λ(a−b)=μ(a′−b′). Taking λ=a′−b′ and μ=a−b solves it. The resulting vector is the same read in either span: in the first the coefficient of o is (a′−b′)a+(a−b)b′=aa′−bb′, and in the second it is (a′−b′)b+(a−b)a′=aa′−bb′ as well. So r=(a′−b′)u+(a−b)w+(aa′−bb′)o lies on both lines, and it is non-zero since a≠b. The two lines being distinct, Corollary 21.6.7 makes [r] their unique common point: R=[r]. Advancing the letters, Q=[q] and P=[p] with q=(a′−c′)u+(a−c)w+(aa′−cc′)o,p=(b′−c′)u+(b−c)w+(bb′−cc′)o.

The finish. Comparing the three displayed vectors coordinate by coordinate in the basis (u,w,o), p+r−q=0. So span(p,q,r)=span(p,r) has dimension at most 2, and it has dimension at least 1 since r≠0. Any subspace of dimension 1 or 2 is contained in some 2-dimensional subspace Z of V, and then P(Z) is a line containing P, Q and R. This proves the theorem.

The hypotheses did exactly three jobs, and it is worth saying which. That ℓ≠m gave the basis. That none of the six points is O let every one of them be written as u+to or w+to with the leading coefficient 1; a point equal to O has no such representative, and the parametrization would break down. That a,b,c are distinct and a′,b′,c′ are distinct made the six cross-joins exist and the three vectors non-zero. Nothing else was needed — in particular the six points may interleave along their two lines in any order, and no further non-degeneracy hypothesis is required.

Remark. Over F2 the theorem is vacuous: a projective line over F2 has 2+1=3 points (Proposition 21.6.9), so after removing O only two remain and three distinct points A,B,C cannot be chosen. Over F3 the hypotheses can just be met, and Exercise (C2: Pappus over the two smallest fields) checks the conclusion there by hand.

Example (Pappus with exact coordinates)

In P2(Q) let ℓ={x1=0} and m={x0=0}, and take A=[1:0:0],B=[1:0:1],C=[1:0:2],A′=[0:1:0],B′=[0:1:1],C′=[0:1:3]. Find R, Q, P and the line through them.

Solution. Here o=e2, u=e0, w=e1, and the parameters are a=0, b=1, c=2, a′=0, b′=1, c′=3. The formulas of the proof give r=(0−1)e0+(0−1)e1+(0−1)e2=−(1,1,1),q=(0−3)e0+(0−2)e1+(0−6)e2=−(3,2,6),p=(1−3)e0+(1−2)e1+(1−6)e2=−(2,1,5), so R=[1:1:1], Q=[3:2:6], P=[2:1:5], and p+r−q=0 as it must be. A linear form vanishing at R and P is 4x0−3x1−x2, and 4⋅3−3⋅2−6=0, so all three lie on the line {4x0−3x1−x2=0}.

As a check on the formulas, compute R directly: AB′ is spanned by (1,0,0) and (0,1,1), so it is {x1=x2}; A′B is spanned by (0,1,0) and (1,0,1), so it is {x0=x2}. Their common point is [1:1:1].

21.9.5. What the two theorems measure

Both theorems have been proved here inside P(V) for a vector space V over a field F, and the proofs used the field only through its arithmetic. It is worth recording what happens if either assumption is loosened, because the answers are among the most quoted facts in the subject — and because this book has built neither the tools to prove them nor the language to state them carefully.

Remark. An axiomatic projective plane is a set of points and lines satisfying incidence axioms, with no vector space given in advance. Hilbert’s coordinatization theory attaches an algebraic system to such a plane and reads geometric axioms off it. Two results of that theory: Desargues’s theorem holds in a plane exactly when the plane comes from a division ring — a field except that multiplication need not commute — and Pappus’s theorem holds exactly when that division ring is commutative, hence a field. Consequently Pappus implies Desargues, a purely geometric implication first proved by Hessenberg; and there are non-Desarguesian planes, the Moulton plane being the standard example. None of this is proved here, and nothing in this book depends on it: we have not constructed division rings, and “projective plane” has meant P(V) throughout. The honest summary is that our proof of Pappus’s theorem multiplied scalars in whatever order was convenient — and that commuting them is exactly what the general theory says cannot be dispensed with.

21.9.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) Define “perspective from a point” and “perspective from a line” for two triangles in a projective plane.
  • (b) In Lemma 21.9.1, which hypothesis makes μ≠0 and which makes ν≠0?
  • (c) State Theorem 21.9.3, with both of its hypotheses on the configuration.
  • (d) True or false, with a reason: in Theorem 21.9.4 the six points are required to be pairwise distinct.
  • (e) Name the one place in the proof of Theorem 21.9.4 where the hypothesis ℓ≠m is used.

Solution.

  • (a) Definition 21.9.1: perspective from a point means some point lies on all three lines AA′, BB′, CC′; perspective from a line means the three pairs of corresponding sides are pairs of distinct lines and their three intersection points are collinear.

  • (b) The hypothesis A′≠O makes μ≠0, and A′≠A makes ν≠0.

  • (c) Two triangles whose six vertices are distinct and with no vertex of either lying on a side of the other are perspective from a point if and only if they are perspective from a line.

  • (d) True, though it is stated in two pieces: A,B,C are required distinct and A′,B′,C′ are required distinct, and no point of ℓ∖{O} can equal a point of m∖{O}, since ℓ∩m={O}. So all six are distinct.

  • (e) In the first paragraph, to get dim⁡(U∩W)=1 and hence the basis (u,w,o). If ℓ=m then U=W and there is no such basis.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: A Desargues configuration)

In P2(Q) let O=[1:1:1], A=[1:0:0], B=[0:1:0], C=[0:0:1], and A′=[0:1:1],B′=[1:0:1],C′=[1:1:0]. Check that each primed point lies on the corresponding line through O, scale the representatives as in Lemma 21.9.2, and compute the axis.

Solution. (0,1,1)=−(1,0,0)+(1,1,1), and similarly (1,0,1)=−(0,1,0)+(1,1,1) and (1,1,0)=−(0,0,1)+(1,1,1). In each case μ=−1 and ν=1, so Lemma 21.9.1 takes a=−e0, a′=(0,1,1), and likewise b=−e1, b′=(1,0,1), c=−e2, c′=(1,1,0). Each difference a′−a is (1,1,1), as required.

Hence AB∩A′B′=[a−b]=[−1:1:0],BC∩B′C′=[b−c]=[0:−1:1],CA∩C′A′=[c−a]=[1:0:−1], and the three vectors sum to 0. A linear form vanishing at the first two is x0+x1+x2, which also vanishes at the third, so the axis is {x0+x1+x2=0}. (Both triples are triangles, since det(a′∣b′∣c′)=2≠0.)

Exercise (B2: A Pappus configuration)

In P2(Q) let ℓ={x1=0} and m={x0=0}, and take A=[1:0:1],B=[1:0:2],C=[1:0:4],A′=[0:1:1],B′=[0:1:3],C′=[0:1:4]. Compute R, Q, P and the line through them, and verify p+r−q=0.

Solution. With u=e0, w=e1, o=e2 the parameters are a=1, b=2, c=4 and a′=1, b′=3, c′=4. By the formulas in the proof of Theorem 21.9.4, r=(1−3)e0+(1−2)e1+(1−6)e2=(−2,−1,−5),q=(1−4)e0+(1−4)e1+(1−16)e2=(−3,−3,−15),p=(3−4)e0+(2−4)e1+(6−16)e2=(−1,−2,−10). Then p+r=(−3,−3,−15)=q, so p+r−q=0. The points are R=[2:1:5], Q=[1:1:5], P=[1:2:10]. A linear form vanishing at R and Q is 5x1−x2, and 5⋅2−10=0, so the three lie on {5x1=x2}.

Exercise (B3: Concurrency from collinearity)

In P2(Q) let A=[1:0:0], B=[0:1:0], C=[0:0:1] and A′=[1:2:2],B′=[2:1:2],C′=[2:2:1]. Verify the hypotheses of Theorem 21.9.3, then decide whether AA′, BB′, CC′ are concurrent by computing the three points AB∩A′B′, BC∩B′C′, CA∩C′A′ and testing them for collinearity. Do not compute the three lines AA′, BB′, CC′.

Solution. Both triples are triangles: (e0,e1,e2) is a basis, and the determinant of the matrix with rows (1,2,2), (2,1,2), (2,2,1) equals 5≠0. The six vertices are distinct. No vertex of ABC lies on a side of A′B′C′, and conversely: the sides of ABC are the three coordinate lines and every primed point has all coordinates non-zero, while A′B′={2x0+2x1−3x2=0}, B′C′={−3x0+2x1+2x2=0}, C′A′={2x0−3x1+2x2=0}, none of which is satisfied by e0, e1 or e2.

Now AB={x2=0}, and a point of A′B′ with x2=0 satisfies 2x0+2x1=0, so AB∩A′B′=[1:−1:0]. Cyclically, BC∩B′C′=[0:1:−1] and CA∩C′A′=[−1:0:1]. The three vectors sum to 0, so they are dependent and the points are collinear, on {x0+x1+x2=0}. By the (⇐) half of Theorem 21.9.3 the three lines AA′, BB′, CC′ are therefore concurrent.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Desargues in space)

Let dim⁡V=4, so P(V)=P3. Let (A,B,C) and (A′,B′,C′) be triangles spanning distinct planes Π and Π′ of P3, with the six vertices distinct, and suppose a point O∉Π∪Π′ lies on each of AA′, BB′, CC′.

  • (a) Prove that A, B, A′, B′ lie in one plane of P3, and deduce that the lines AB and A′B′ meet.
  • (b) Prove that Π∩Π′ is a line.
  • (c) Deduce that the three points AB∩A′B′, BC∩B′C′, CA∩C′A′ lie on that line, and so are collinear.

Hint: for (a), all four points lie on lines through O; for (b), use the dimension formula for subspaces.

Solution.

  • (a) Since O∉Π∪Π′ and all six vertices lie in Π∪Π′, the point O is none of them; and A≠A′, B≠B′ because the six are distinct. Let o represent O. The points O,A,A′ are distinct and collinear, so Lemma 21.9.1 gives representatives with a′=a+o, and the same o gives b′=b+o. All four of a,b,a′,b′ then lie in Z=span(o,a,b), of dimension at most 3, so the four points lie in the projective subspace P(Z), of dimension at most 2 — a plane or less. Subtracting, a−b=a′−b′, a vector that is non-zero because A≠B and that lies in span(a,b)∩span(a′,b′); so [a−b] lies on both AB and A′B′, and the two lines meet.

  • (b) Π=P(S) and Π′=P(S′) with dim⁡S=dim⁡S′=3 and S≠S′, inside a 4-dimensional V. Then S+S′=V, so by Theorem 1.7.3, dim⁡(S∩S′)=3+3−4=2, and P(S∩S′) is a line.

  • (c) First, AB≠A′B′: otherwise A and A′ would both lie in Π′, hence so would the line AA′ and the point O on it, contradicting O∉Π∪Π′. Two distinct lines have at most one common point, since two distinct points determine their line (Proposition 21.6.8), and by (a) these two lines do meet; so AB∩A′B′ is a single point. It lies on AB⊆Π and on A′B′⊆Π′, hence in Π∩Π′, which is the line of (b). The same holds for the other two points. Three points on one line are collinear.

Exercise (C2: Pappus over the two smallest fields)
  • (a) Prove that the hypotheses of Theorem 21.9.4 cannot be satisfied when F=F2.
  • (b) Over F=F3, take ℓ, m, o, u, w as in the proof of Theorem 21.9.4 and the parameters a=a′=0, b=b′=1, c=c′=2. Compute r,q,p and exhibit a line containing all three points.
  • (c) Explain why the choice of parameters in (b) is the only one available over F3, up to renaming the points.

Solution.

  • (a) A line of P2(Fq) is P(U) with dim⁡U=2, and U∖{0} has q2−1 vectors falling into (q2−1)/(q−1)=q+1 points (Proposition 21.6.9). For q=2 a line has 3 points, so ℓ∖{O} has only two, and three distinct points A,B,C of ℓ, none equal to O, do not exist.

  • (b) With a=a′=0, b=b′=1, c=c′=2 the formulas give r=(0−1)u+(0−1)w+(0−1)o=(2,2,2),q=(0−2)u+(0−2)w+(0−4)o=(1,1,2),p=(1−2)u+(1−2)w+(1−4)o=(2,2,0), all read in F3 and in the basis (u,w,o). So R=[1:1:1], Q=[1:1:2], P=[1:1:0], and every one of them satisfies x0−x1=0. That linear form defines a line, and the three points lie on it. As a check, p+r−q=(2+2−1, 2+2−1, 0+2−2)=(0,0,0) in F3.

  • (c) A line of P2(F3) has 3+1=4 points, so ℓ∖{O} has exactly three, namely the ones with parameters 0,1,2; the same on m. The three points A,B,C must therefore be those three in some order, and likewise A′,B′,C′. Renaming amounts to permuting the parameters, which permutes P, Q, R and changes nothing about their collinearity.

Exercise (C3: Why the side hypothesis is there)

In P2(Q) let A=[1:0:0],B=[0:1:0],C=[0:0:1],A′=[1:1:0],B′=[1:2:0],C′=[1:3:1], and let O=[1:3:0].

  • (a) Prove that (A,B,C) and (A′,B′,C′) are triangles with six distinct vertices, and that O lies on each of AA′, BB′, CC′.
  • (b) Prove that the two triangles are not perspective from a line, in the strict sense of Definition 21.9.1.
  • (c) Say which clause of hypothesis (ii) of Theorem 21.9.3 fails here.

Solution.

  • (a) (e0,e1,e2) is a basis, so (A,B,C) is a triangle, and det(110120131)=1≠0, so (A′,B′,C′) is one too. The six coordinate vectors are pairwise non-proportional, so the vertices are distinct. Now A,A′,O all have last coordinate 0, hence lie on the line {x2=0}, and so are collinear; the same for B,B′,O. Finally (1,3,1)=(0,0,1)+(1,3,0), so C′ lies on the line through C and O.

  • (b) The line AB is {x2=0}, since e0 and e1 span its underlying plane. The line A′B′ is spanned by (1,1,0) and (1,2,0), an independent pair inside the same plane, so A′B′={x2=0}=AB as well. The pair {AB,A′B′} therefore does not consist of distinct lines, and clause (b) of Definition 21.9.1 fails at its first requirement: there is no point “AB∩A′B′” to speak of, the intersection being a whole line.

  • (c) The clause that no vertex of one triangle lies on a side of the other. Here A′=[1:1:0] lies on AB={x2=0}, and so do B′ and O.
