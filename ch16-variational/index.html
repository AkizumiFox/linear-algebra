Variational Principles and Interlacing

Since Chapter 12 the book has written λ1(A)≥⋯≥λn(A) for the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix without ever saying what the middle ones are. The two at the ends have an answer. Chapter 10 observed that ⟨Tv,v⟩ is real for a self-adjoint operator, and Chapter 12 §05 turned that into Lemma 12.5.2: on the unit sphere, ⟨Ax,x⟩ runs from λn(A) up to λ1(A), and both ends are attained at eigenvectors. Everything between the ends was left blank, twice and deliberately. Chapter 12 §05 said it in one sentence: the intermediate eigenvalues “have no description as a plain maximum over the whole unit sphere”. Chapter 12 §12 said where the blank would be filled: “Chapter 16 characterizes the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix as maxima and minima of ⟨Ax,x⟩ — at which point the test becomes the theory.”

This is that chapter, and the device that fills the blank is a min–max. One maximum over the unit sphere is not enough information to see λ2; a maximum over a subspace, followed by a minimum over all subspaces of the right dimension, is exactly enough. That is the Courant–Fischer theorem, and its proof is one dimension count repeated: two subspaces of Fn whose dimensions add to more than n must share a non-zero vector. Once every eigenvalue is a maximum and a minimum of a quantity that does not mention an eigenvector, eigenvalues of different matrices become comparable, and the rest of the chapter is the harvest. How far the eigenvalues move when the matrix moves (Weyl). How they move when a row and its column are deleted, or a rank-one matrix is added (Cauchy interlacing, and the secular equation). What sums of the largest eigenvalues optimize (Poincaré, Ky Fan), and what arbitrary index sets do (Wielandt, Lidskii). What the diagonal of a Hermitian matrix can be, given its spectrum (Schur). The same theory for singular values, which finally proves the approximation theorem Chapter 12 §10 could only state. Inertia, re-proved without a basis and then made additive across a Schur complement. And a first look at which scalar functions respect the Loewner order.

Every matrix whose eigenvalues are indexed in this chapter is Hermitian, and the field is R or C. This is not a convenience. The index i in λi(A) presupposes that the eigenvalues are real and can be sorted, which is Theorem 11.2.1, and every eigenvalue inequality below is false without it — §04 exhibits a non-Hermitian matrix with a 1×1 principal submatrix lying outside the convex hull of the whole matrix’s spectrum. Each section states the hypothesis once and keeps it.

Eigenvalues are indexed decreasingly, λ1(A)≥λ2(A)≥⋯≥λn(A), repeated according to multiplicity, as Chapter 12 §05 fixed it. Many sources index the min–max theorem the other way, and the two statements differ by i↔n+1−i; §02 gives the increasing form once, as a remark, so that a reader who meets it elsewhere is not lost.

The analysis this chapter imports

None that is new. Chapter 15 named everything it borrowed from analysis in a numbered list (A1)–(A6) in its introduction, and this chapter uses that list and nothing else, citing an item by the same name at the point of use — “by the extreme value theorem, fact (A4) of Chapter 15’s introduction”. Compactness of the unit sphere (A3) and the extreme value theorem (A4) are the two that a reader will expect to see, since a min–max is an optimization and optimizations need their extrema to exist.

It is worth saying in advance that the expectation is not always met. In §§01–02, where the maxima and minima are first written down, no analytic fact is invoked: the spectral theorem is already available, and it hands over a maximizer explicitly, so the extreme value theorem is never called on to prove that one exists. That is not the same as saying no analysis lies underneath. The spectral theorem rests on the fundamental theorem of algebra, and Chapter 5 §05 proves that with the extreme value theorem on a closed disc; the analysis was paid for there, once, and is not paid again here. Chapter 11 §02 avoided that theorem on purpose and said so; §§01–02 below return to the remark and settle the account. Where a later section does need an item of the list, it names it. Nothing is added to the list.

Roadmap

  • The Rayleigh quotient. RA(x)=⟨Ax,x⟩/⟨x,x⟩ promoted from a test to an object: its range is exactly [λn,λ1], its critical points on the unit sphere are exactly the unit eigenvectors, and the first description of λk — maximize after deflating by the top eigenvectors — is correct but circular.
  • The min–max theorem. Courant–Fischer: λk is a minimum over subspaces of a maximum over vectors, and equally a maximum over subspaces of a minimum. No eigenvector is named, so two matrices can now be compared eigenvalue by eigenvalue.
  • How far eigenvalues move. Weyl’s inequalities, and the Lipschitz bound |λi(A+E)−λi(A)|≤‖E‖2 that Chapter 15 §07 promised.
  • Deleting a row and a column. Cauchy interlacing: the spectrum of a principal submatrix is caught between consecutive eigenvalues of the whole matrix. When the interlacing is strict, which settles a promise of Chapter 10: the zeros of consecutive orthogonal polynomials interlace strictly. And why none of it survives without symmetry.
  • Adding a rank-one matrix. Interlacing again, the secular equation that locates the new eigenvalues between the old, and the shifts that add up to τ‖v‖2.
  • Subspaces and sums. Poincaré’s separation theorem, and Ky Fan’s: the sum of the k largest eigenvalues is a maximum of a trace over orthonormal k-frames, hence a convex function of the matrix. Chapter 12’s capture bound turns out to be one case of it.
  • Arbitrary index sets. The Wielandt min–max over a flag of subspaces, and Lidskii’s inequality. It contains Ky Fan’s subadditivity and the Weyl inequalities with one index at an end — but not all of Weyl’s, which is worth seeing.
  • The diagonal and the spectrum. Majorization; Schur’s theorem and Horn’s converse, which together say that the diagonals of the Hermitian matrices with a given spectrum are exactly the vectors that spectrum majorizes.
  • The same theory for singular values. The min–max for σk, perturbation with no hypothesis on the perturbation at all, the spectral-norm form of Eckart–Young, interlacing when a row or column is deleted, and the distance from an invertible matrix to the nearest singular one — which is what Chapter 15’s condition number was measuring all along.
  • Inertia, again and additively. A second proof of Sylvester’s law with no basis in it, now over C as well; how far a rank-r perturbation can move the inertia; and Haynsworth’s additivity across a Schur complement.
  • Which functions respect the order. Operator monotone and operator convex functions, why the square root behaves and the square does not, and a statement of the classification that Chapter 20 proves.

Named moves

  • Count dimensions to force an intersection. Two subspaces of an n-dimensional space whose dimensions add to more than n meet in a non-zero vector. This one line proves Courant–Fischer and Weyl directly, and Cauchy interlacing and Poincaré through Courant–Fischer. It is the chapter’s single technique, and everything else is bookkeeping around it.
  • Prove a min–max by two inequalities of different characters. One direction exhibits a witness subspace — usually a span of eigenvectors — and computes on it. The other takes an arbitrary subspace and intersects it with a span of eigenvectors from the other end. Neither half resembles the other, and recognizing which half is which is most of the work in reading any proof below.
  • Apply the result to −A. The two halves of Courant–Fischer, the two families of Weyl inequalities and the top- and bottom-k trace theorems are each other’s images under A↦−A, which reverses the order and sends λi to −λn+1−i. Deriving the second statement this way is a genuine argument, not a wave of the hand, and it is written out each time.
  • Compress, then interlace. Restricting the quadratic form to a subspace produces a smaller Hermitian matrix whose eigenvalues are trapped by the original’s. Deleting rows and columns, adding a rank-one matrix and cutting a singular value decomposition short are all this move.
  • Perturb and compare, eigenvalue by eigenvalue. Chapter 15’s move, now applied one index at a time rather than to the spectrum as a whole.
  • Reduce singular values to eigenvalues. Either through A∗A, which is shortest, or through the Hermitian dilation, which is symmetric in A and A∗. Both appear, and each section says which it uses and why.
  • Majorize. When the right comparison is between sums of the largest entries rather than individual ones, compare the partial sums of the decreasing rearrangements. The diagonal of a Hermitian matrix against its spectrum (§08), and the Ky Fan and Lidskii sums (§§06–07), are all of this type.
