13.12  A First Look at Clifford Algebras

The chapter ends with a construction rather than a classification. Everything so far has taken a quadratic form apart; this section asks instead for an algebra in which the form becomes a square, so that q(v) is literally v times v for a multiplication we invent. The answer is a Clifford algebra, and two of the most familiar algebras in mathematics are its first two examples. This is an introduction only: we define the object, prove what the definition alone gives, work three cases out completely, and stop.

13.12.1. Making a form into a square

Take V=R2 with the negative definite form q(x)=−(x12+x22), and suppose we had an associative multiplication on some larger space containing V, with an identity 1, in which every vector satisfies v2=q(v)⋅1. Write ϵ1,ϵ2 for the two standard basis vectors sitting inside that algebra. Expanding v=x1ϵ1+x2ϵ2 and using only bilinearity of the product, v2=x12ϵ12+x1x2(ϵ1ϵ2+ϵ2ϵ1)+x22ϵ22. We want this to equal −(x12+x22)⋅1 for every x1,x2. Taking x2=0 forces ϵ12=−1, and taking x1=0 forces ϵ22=−1. What is left is x1x2(ϵ1ϵ2+ϵ2ϵ1)=0 for all x1,x2, which forces ϵ1ϵ2=−ϵ2ϵ1. So the two basis vectors must square to −1 and must anticommute. That last relation hands us a third square root of −1 we did not ask for, since (ϵ1ϵ2)2=ϵ1ϵ2ϵ1ϵ2=−ϵ12ϵ22=−1. Three anticommuting square roots of −1: those are Hamilton’s quaternions. Run the same argument on a one-dimensional V with q(x)=−x2 and the single relation ϵ12=−1 produces C.

A Clifford algebra is the smallest place where the vectors of V can be multiplied and each one squares to its own q-value.

13.12.2. Algebras

The objects above are algebras, a word the book has used informally and now needs precisely.

Definition 13.12.1 (Algebra over a Field)

An algebra over F, or an F-algebra, is a vector space A over F together with a map A×A→A, written (x,y)↦xy, which is

  • (A1) bilinear: linear in each argument with the other fixed;
  • (A2) associative: (xy)z=x(yz) for all x,y,z∈A;
  • (A3) unital: there is an element 1A∈A with 1Ax=x1A=x for every x∈A.

A homomorphism of F-algebras is an F-linear map φ:A→B with φ(xy)=φ(x)φ(y) for all x,y and φ(1A)=1B. An isomorphism is a bijective homomorphism.

Every algebra here is associative and unital, and we stop saying so. The examples are old friends: F itself; Mn(F); F[x]; L(V) under composition; and C over R, of dimension 2. A non-example by minimal change: R3 with the cross product is bilinear but fails (A2) and (A3), since (e1×e1)×e2=0 while e1×(e1×e2)=−e2, and no vector acts as an identity.

One consequence of (A1) is used constantly below: a bilinear map is determined by its values on a basis, so two products agree, and a linear map is multiplicative, as soon as this is checked on basis elements.

13.12.3. The definition

Definition 13.12.2 (Clifford Algebra)

Let F be a field with charF≠2, let V be a finite-dimensional F-vector space, let β be a symmetric bilinear form on V and let q(v)=β(v,v) be its quadratic form (Definition 13.3.2). A Clifford algebra of (V,q) is a pair (C,ι), where C is an F-algebra and ι:V→C is a linear map, such that

  • (C1) ι(v)2=q(v)1C for every v∈V;
  • (C2) (universal property) for every F-algebra A and every linear map f:V→A with f(v)2=q(v)1A for every v∈V, there is a unique algebra homomorphism f¯:C→A with f¯∘ι=f.

We write Cl(q), or Cl(V,q), for a Clifford algebra of (V,q), and Clp,m(R) when V is the space Rp,m of Definition 13.11.1, that is, Rp+m with the real form of signature (p,m).

Clause by clause. (C1) is the demand we started from: inside C every vector has a square, and that square is a number times the identity, not a vector. (C2) says C is the most economical algebra with that property. Any other algebra where the vectors square correctly receives a map from C, so C has no relations beyond those (C1) forces; and the map is unique, so C has no room to spare either. What is defined is the pair (C,ι), not C alone.

Remark. The subscripts in Clp,m(R) are usually written p,q, but q already names the quadratic form here, so the second index is m.

Existence is deferred. The definition says what a Clifford algebra would be; it does not produce one. The standard construction is not available yet: one takes the tensor algebra of V — the infinite-dimensional algebra whose elements are formal sums of strings of vectors, multiplied by concatenation — and divides out by the relations v⊗v−q(v)1. Chapter 14 builds tensor algebras and carries this out, for every V and q at once. So everything below is of one of two kinds: a consequence of the definition, valid whenever a Clifford algebra exists, or an explicit algebra written down by hand, which proves existence in that case. We say which each time.

The first consequence is that “the” Clifford algebra is legitimate language.

Proposition 13.12.1 (Uniqueness of the Clifford Algebra)

Let (C,ι) and (C′,ι′) both be Clifford algebras of (V,q). Then there is a unique algebra isomorphism φ:C→C′ with φ∘ι=ι′.

Idea. Each one’s universal property produces a map to the other. The two composites are self-maps that do nothing to ι, and the uniqueness clause says the only such self-map is the identity.

Proof. Since (C′,ι′) satisfies (C1), the universal property (C2) of C, applied with A=C′ and f=ι′, gives an algebra homomorphism φ:C→C′ with φι=ι′. Symmetrically, (C2) for C′ gives ψ:C′→C with ψι′=ι. Then ψφ:C→C is an algebra homomorphism with ψφι=ψι′=ι. The identity idC is another one. By the uniqueness clause of (C2) for C, applied with A=C and f=ι, the two coincide: ψφ=idC. Swapping the roles of C and C′ gives φψ=idC′. Hence φ is an isomorphism, and it is unique because (C2) already delivers it uniquely. This proves the proposition.

Next, the identity that does all the computing.

Lemma 13.12.2 (The Anticommutation Relation)

Let (C,ι) be a Clifford algebra of (V,q). Then for all u,v∈V, ι(u)ι(v)+ι(v)ι(u)=2β(u,v)1C. In particular ι(u) and ι(v) anticommute whenever β(u,v)=0.

Proof. By (C1) applied to u+v, and then bilinearity of the product together with linearity of ι, q(u+v)1C=ι(u+v)2=ι(u)2+ι(u)ι(v)+ι(v)ι(u)+ι(v)2. By Theorem 13.3.5, q(u+v)=q(u)+2β(u,v)+q(v), and by (C1) again ι(u)2=q(u)1C and ι(v)2=q(v)1C. Canceling those two terms from both sides leaves the stated identity. The last sentence is the case β(u,v)=0.

So an orthogonal basis for β becomes a list of pairwise anticommuting elements of C, each with a scalar square. That is the whole computational content of a Clifford algebra, and Theorem 13.4.2 guarantees such a basis exists whenever charF≠2 — which is where the characteristic hypothesis in Definition 13.12.2 earns its place, since Theorem 13.3.5 needs it too.

13.12.4. Three algebras, worked out

Example (The zero space)

Let V={0}, so q=0 and n=0. Show that (F,ι) with ι=0 is a Clifford algebra of (V,q), so that Cl(0)=F.

Solution. (C1): the only vector is 0, and ι(0)2=0=q(0)1F.

(C2): let A be an F-algebra and f:V→A linear, necessarily f=0, so the condition on f reads 0=0. Define f¯:F→A by f¯(a)=a1A: it is linear, sends 1 to 1A, and is multiplicative because f¯(ab)=(ab)1A=(a1A)(b1A) by bilinearity of the product and 1A1A=1A; and f¯ι=0=f. It is unique, since any algebra homomorphism φ:F→A is linear with φ(1)=1A, hence φ(a)=a1A.

So Cl(0)=F, of dimension 1=20: the construction adds nothing when there is nothing to add.

Example (The complex numbers)

Let V=R with q(x)=−x2, the form of signature (0,1). Show that C, with ι:R→C given by ι(x)=xi, is a Clifford algebra of (V,q), so that Cl0,1(R)=C.

Solution. Here C is an algebra over R of dimension 2, with basis (1,i).

(C1): ι(x)2=(xi)2=x2i2=−x2=q(x)⋅1.

(C2): let A be a real algebra and f:R→A linear with f(x)2=−x21A. Put u=f(1), so f(x)=xu by linearity and u2=−1A. Define f¯:C→A by f¯(a+bi)=a1A+bu, which is R-linear and sends 1 to 1A. To see it is multiplicative, note that (z,w)↦f¯(zw) and (z,w)↦f¯(z)f¯(w) are both R-bilinear, so it suffices to compare them on the basis (1,i): the pairs involving 1 are immediate, and f¯(i⋅i)=f¯(−1)=−1A=u2=f¯(i)f¯(i). Finally f¯ι(x)=f¯(xi)=xu=f(x). For uniqueness, an algebra homomorphism φ with φι=f has φ(1)=1A and φ(i)=φ(ι(1))=f(1)=u, and (1,i) spans C, so φ=f¯.

So Cl0,1(R)=C, of dimension 2=21. Read this backwards: C is not primarily “R with a square root of −1 attached”, it is the smallest algebra in which the number x squares to −x2.

For the two-dimensional case we need the quaternions, and we need them to be associative. The cheapest way to get associativity is to find them inside a matrix algebra, where it is free.

Definition 13.12.3 (The Quaternions)

Inside M2(C), regarded as an algebra over R, put i=(i00−i),j=(01−10),k=(0ii0). The quaternions H are the real span of I2,i,j,k in M2(C).

The three matrices carry Hamilton’s names and are written bold lowercase, as basis vectors of H; the plain i in their entries is the complex unit, which is a different object from the matrix i. Multiplying them out gives i2=j2=k2=−I2,ij=k=−ji, and then jk=i=−kj and ki=j=−ik. A real combination aI2+bi+cj+dk is the matrix with rows (a+bi, c+di) and (−c+di, a−bi), which vanishes only when a=b=c=d=0; so dimR⁡H=4. The relations show the span is closed under multiplication, so H is a real subalgebra of M2(C) — and it is associative for free, because matrix multiplication is.

Example (The quaternions)

Let V=R2 with q(x)=−(x12+x22), the form of signature (0,2). Show that H, with ι(x)=x1i+x2j, is a Clifford algebra of (V,q), so that Cl0,2(R)=H.

Solution. (C1): using ij+ji=0 and i2=j2=−I2, ι(x)2=x12i2+x1x2(ij+ji)+x22j2=−(x12+x22)I2, which is q(x)1H.

(C2): let A be a real algebra and f:R2→A linear with f(x)2=q(x)1A. Put u=f(e1) and w=f(e2), so u2=w2=−1A, and by Lemma 13.12.2 — or directly, by expanding f(e1+e2)2=−2⋅1A — we get uw+wu=0, that is, wu=−uw. Define the linear map f¯(aI2+bi+cj+dk)=a1A+bu+cw+duw, which is well defined because (I2,i,j,k) is a basis of H, and sends 1H=I2 to 1A. Both (z,z′)↦f¯(zz′) and (z,z′)↦f¯(z)f¯(z′) are R-bilinear, so multiplicativity need only be checked on the basis. The pairs involving I2 are immediate, and the remaining nine are ii=−I2↦−1A=u2,jj=−I2↦−1A=w2,ij=k↦uw,ji=−k↦−uw=wu,ik=−j↦−w=u(uw),ki=j↦w=(uw)u,jk=i↦u=w(uw),kj=−i↦−u=(uw)w,kk=−I2↦−1A=(uw)2. Each right-hand claim is one line of algebra in A, using associativity, wu=−uw and u2=w2=−1A. For instance (uw)u=u(wu)=−u(uw)=−u2w=w, and (uw)2=u(wu)w=−u2w2=−1A.

Finally f¯ι(x)=x1u+x2w=f(x). For uniqueness, an algebra homomorphism φ with φι=f is forced on I2, on i=ι(e1) and on j=ι(e2), hence on k=ij, and these four span H.

So Cl0,2(R)=H, of dimension 4=22.

Warning. A Clifford algebra is not commutative, and a product of two vectors is not a vector. In Cl0,2(R)=H the image ι(R2)=span(i,j) is a plane, and ij=k lies outside it; worse, ij=−ji, so the order matters. Two habits have to go: that multiplying two elements of V keeps you in V, and that xy=yx. Beyond the scalars F⋅1C, nothing may be assumed to commute.

13.12.5. How big is it

The three examples all came out with dim=2n. Half of that is a theorem we can prove.

Proposition 13.12.3 (The Clifford Algebra Is Spanned by Sorted Products)

Let (C,ι) be a Clifford algebra of (V,q), where dim⁡V=n and charF≠2, and let (e1,…,en) be an orthogonal basis of V for β. Then C is spanned over F by the 2n products ι(ei1)ι(ei2)⋯ι(eik),1≤i1<i2<⋯<ik≤n, one for each subset {i1,…,ik} of {1,…,n}, the empty product being 1C. In particular dimF⁡C≤2n.

Idea. Two steps. ① Show that C is generated as an algebra by 1C and ι(V) — this is not automatic, and it is where the uniqueness half of the universal property does its work. ② Sort: any product of basis vectors can be put in increasing order by swapping neighbors at the cost of a sign (Lemma 13.12.2), and a repeated neighbor collapses to a scalar (C1).

Proof. An orthogonal basis exists by Theorem 13.4.2, since charF≠2. Write di=q(ei).

Step 1: C is generated by 1C and ι(V). Let C′⊆C be the set of all F-linear combinations of 1C and of finite products ι(v1)⋯ι(vm) with v1,…,vm∈V. It is a subspace closed under multiplication and contains 1C, hence is itself an F-algebra, and ι followed by the change of codomain gives a linear map ι′:V→C′ satisfying (C1). By the universal property of C, applied with A=C′ and f=ι′, there is an algebra homomorphism g:C→C′ with gι=ι′. Let j:C′→C be the inclusion, an algebra homomorphism. Then jg:C→C is an algebra homomorphism with jgι=ι, and so is idC; by the uniqueness clause of (C2), applied with A=C and f=ι, we get jg=idC. Hence j is surjective, so C′=C.

Step 2: sorting. By Step 1 and linearity of ι, C is spanned by 1C together with the products ι(ej1)⋯ι(ejm) over all finite index strings (j1,…,jm). Fix such a product and induct on the pair (m,inv(j1,…,jm)), ordered lexicographically, where inv counts the pairs r<s with jr>js. If the string is strictly increasing, the product is on the list and we are done. Otherwise some neighboring pair has jr≥jr+1. If jr=jr+1, then (C1) replaces ι(ejr)2 by the scalar djr, producing a scalar multiple of a product of length m−2. If jr>jr+1, then Lemma 13.12.2 with β(ejr,ejr+1)=0 lets us swap the two factors at the cost of a sign, producing a product of the same length with one fewer inversion. Either way the induction applies, and the product is a scalar multiple of a listed one.

There are 2n subsets of {1,…,n}, so the spanning list has 2n members and dimF⁡C≤2n. This proves the proposition.

Quick check. Where exactly does Proposition 13.12.3 use the word unique in (C2), and what would go wrong without it?

Solution. In Step 1, at the sentence “by the uniqueness clause of (C2) … we get jg=idC”. Existence alone gives an algebra homomorphism jg:C→C fixing ι, but nothing then makes it the identity, and the argument cannot conclude that j is surjective. The conclusion really would be false. Take V=R with q(x)=−x2, let A=C×C with componentwise multiplication, and set ι(x)=(xi,xi). Then ι(x)2=q(x)1A, so (C1) holds, and so does the existence half of (C2): the first projection, followed by the homomorphism C→B built in Example (The complex numbers), does the job for every target B. But A is not spanned by 1A and products of vectors, since every such product lies in the diagonal {(z,z)}, of dimension 2. What fails is uniqueness — the second projection is a second map — and that is the clause the proof needs.

The other half of the count is a different matter.

Theorem 13.12.4 (Dimension of a Clifford Algebra)

Let charF≠2, let dim⁡V=n, and let (C,ι) be a Clifford algebra of (V,q). Then the 2n sorted products of Proposition 13.12.3 form a basis of C, so dimF⁡C=2n. In particular ι is injective.

What is proved here, and what is not. The inequality dimF⁡C≤2n is Proposition 13.12.3, proved in full. The reverse inequality — that the 2n sorted products are linearly independent — is not proved in this section, and it cannot be, because the definition alone does not even produce an algebra: independence and existence have to be established together, by constructing one algebra of dimension exactly 2n that satisfies (C1) and (C2). Chapter 14 does that, from the tensor algebra. What this section does establish is the theorem in the three cases above: n=0 with dim=1, n=1 with dim=2, and the case q=−x12−x22 of n=2 with dim=4, each by writing the algebra down and checking (C1) and (C2) by hand. For n≥3, read the statement above as quoted from Chapter 14 and not as proved here.

Granting the theorem, the subsets of {1,…,n} indexing the basis are the first sign that Cl(q) is assembled from the exterior powers ΛkV, one for each k. Chapter 14 constructs both and makes that precise.

13.12.6. What Clifford algebras are for

One computation shows where the subject goes. Recall from Section 10 that the isometries of q form a group (Definition 13.10.1). Inside Cl(q), some of them are conjugations.

Proposition 13.12.5 (Reflections Are Conjugations)

Let (C,ι) be a Clifford algebra of (V,q) and let u∈V with q(u)≠0. Then ι(u) is invertible in C, with inverse q(u)−1ι(u), and for every v∈V, −ι(u)ι(v)ι(u)−1=ι(su(v)),su(v):=v−2β(u,v)q(u)u. The map su fixes {v:β(u,v)=0} pointwise, sends u to −u, and satisfies q(su(v))=q(v).

Proof. By (C1), ι(u)2=q(u)1C with q(u)≠0, so ι(u)(q(u)−1ι(u))=1C and likewise on the other side. By Lemma 13.12.2, ι(u)ι(v)=2β(u,v)1C−ι(v)ι(u), so multiplying on the right by ι(u), ι(u)ι(v)ι(u)=2β(u,v)ι(u)−q(u)ι(v). Dividing by q(u) and negating gives −ι(u)ι(v)ι(u)−1=ι(v)−2β(u,v)q(u)ι(u), which is ι(su(v)) by linearity of ι. If β(u,v)=0 then su(v)=v; and su(u)=u−2u=−u. Finally, writing c=2β(u,v)/q(u), q(v−cu)=q(v)−2cβ(u,v)+c2q(u)=q(v), since c2q(u)=c⋅2β(u,v). This proves the proposition.

So the reflection in a hyperplane, the basic isometry of a quadratic form, is carried out inside Cl(q) by conjugating with a single vector. Composing such conjugations reaches more isometries, and the invertible elements of Cl(q) acting on V this way form a group mapping onto the isometry group of q — two-to-one, since ι(u) and −ι(u) conjugate alike. That double covering is where spinors live: objects the covering group acts on and the isometry group does not, because going once around a rotation returns them with a sign. This book will not build them. What it will do is Chapter 14: construct tensor algebras, construct Cl(q) from one, and prove Theorem 13.12.4. Nothing later depends on this section, and the chapter ends here.

13.12.7. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) State the definition of an F-algebra, and give one example of dimension 1 and one of dimension 4.
  • (b) State the universal property (C2), with every quantifier.
  • (c) What is Cl(0) when V={0}, and what is its dimension?
  • (d) True or false: Cl0,2(R) is commutative. Justify your answer.
  • (e) What does this book prove about dim⁡Cl(q) when dim⁡V=3, and what does it only quote?

Solution.

  • (a) A vector space over F with a bilinear, associative, unital multiplication (Definition 13.12.1). Dimension 1: F itself. Dimension 4: M2(F), or H over R.

  • (b) For every F-algebra A and every linear f:V→A such that f(v)2=q(v)1A for every v∈V, there exists a unique algebra homomorphism f¯:C→A such that f¯∘ι=f.

  • (c) Cl(0)=F, of dimension 1=20 (Example (The zero space)).

  • (d) False. It is H by Example (The quaternions), and ij=k≠−k=ji.

  • (e) It proves dim⁡Cl(q)≤23=8, by Proposition 13.12.3. The equality dim⁡Cl(q)=8, and indeed the existence of Cl(q) at all, is quoted from Chapter 14.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: The split case)

Let V=R with q(x)=x2, the form of signature (1,0). Let A=R×R with componentwise addition and multiplication, and ι:R→A, ι(x)=(x,−x). Prove that (A,ι) is a Clifford algebra of (V,q), and state its dimension.

Solution. A is a real algebra of dimension 2 with identity 1A=(1,1), and (1A,ι(1))=((1,1),(1,−1)) is a basis, since a(1,1)+b(1,−1)=(a+b,a−b) vanishes only for a=b=0.

(C1): ι(x)2=(x2,x2)=q(x)1A.

(C2): let B be a real algebra and f:R→B linear with f(x)2=x21B. Put u=f(1), so f(x)=xu and u2=1B. Define f¯(a,b)=a+b21B+a−b2u, which is linear and sends 1A to 1B and ι(1) to u. By bilinearity, multiplicativity need only be checked on that basis: the pairs involving 1A are immediate, and ι(1)2=1A↦1B=u2. Also f¯ι(x)=xu=f(x), and any such homomorphism is forced on 1A and ι(1), which span A.

So Cl1,0(R)=R×R, of dimension 2=21. Comparing with Example (The complex numbers): the same one-dimensional V with the opposite sign of q gives C instead, so the Clifford algebra sees the sign of the form, not just its rank.

Exercise (B2)

Work in Cl0,2(R)=H, with ι(x)=x1i+x2j. Let u=(1,2) and v=(3,−1).

  • (a) Compute ι(u)ι(v) and ι(v)ι(u), and verify Lemma 13.12.2 on this pair.
  • (b) Compute q(u), verify ι(u)2=q(u)1H, and write down ι(u)−1.

Solution.

  • (a) Using i2=j2=−1 and ij=k=−ji, and writing 1 for I2, ι(u)ι(v)=(i+2j)(3i−j)=−3−k−6k+2=−1−7k,ι(v)ι(u)=(3i−j)(i+2j)=−3+6k+k+2=−1+7k. Their sum is −2. On the other side, β(u,v)=−(1⋅3+2⋅(−1))=−1, so 2β(u,v)1H=−2. The two agree.

  • (b) q(u)=−(1+4)=−5. And ι(u)2=(i+2j)2=−1+2k−2k−4=−5, as required. Hence ι(u)−1=q(u)−1ι(u)=−15(i+2j) by Proposition 13.12.5.

Exercise (B3)

In R0,2 let u=e1 and v=e1+e2. Compute the reflection su(v) directly from the formula in Proposition 13.12.5, then compute −ι(u)ι(v)ι(u)−1 inside H, and check that the two agree.

Solution. Here β(x,y)=−(x1y1+x2y2), so β(u,v)=−1 and q(u)=−1. The formula gives su(v)=v−2(−1)−1u=v−2u=−e1+e2. In H: ι(u)=i, ι(v)=i+j, and ι(u)−1=q(u)−1i=−i. Hence −i(i+j)(−i)=i(i+j)i=(−1+k)i=−i+ki=−i+j, using ki=j. That is ι(−e1+e2), which agrees with ι(su(v)). Geometrically su negates the e1 coordinate and fixes the e2 one, which is what the answer shows.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: A Clifford algebra of matrices)

In M2(R) put E1=(100−1) and E2=(01−10), and let V=R2 carry the form q(x)=x12−x22 of signature (1,1), with f(x)=x1E1+x2E2.

  • (a) Prove that f(x)2=q(x)I2 for every x, and that (I2,E1,E2,E1E2) is a basis of M2(R).
  • (b) Assume a Clifford algebra (C,ι) of (V,q) exists. Deduce that C≅M2(R), and hence that dim⁡C=4.

Hint for (b): the homomorphism the universal property hands you has a surjective image; then count.

Solution.

  • (a) A direct multiplication gives E12=I2, E22=−I2 and E1E2=(0110)=−E2E1, so E1E2+E2E1=0 and f(x)2=x12E12+x1x2(E1E2+E2E1)+x22E22=(x12−x22)I2. For the basis: aI2+bE1+cE2+dE1E2 is the matrix with rows (a+b, c+d) and (−c+d, a−b), and setting all four entries to zero forces a=b=c=d=0. Four independent vectors in the 4-dimensional space M2(R) form a basis.

  • (b) By (a) and the universal property (C2) with A=M2(R), there is an algebra homomorphism f¯:C→M2(R) with f¯ι=f. Its image is a subalgebra containing I2, E1=f(e1) and E2=f(e2), hence also E1E2; by (a) those four span M2(R), so f¯ is surjective and dim⁡C≥4. On the other hand dim⁡C≤22=4 by Proposition 13.12.3. Therefore dim⁡C=4 and f¯ is a surjective linear map between spaces of equal finite dimension, hence bijective, hence an algebra isomorphism. So Cl1,1(R)≅M2(R).

Exercise (C2: Why characteristic 2 is excluded)

Explain why Definition 13.12.2 assumes charF≠2, by naming the first statement of this section that fails without it and saying what replaces the missing input.

Solution. The first casualty is Lemma 13.12.2, whose proof cites Theorem 13.3.5 for q(u+v)=q(u)+2β(u,v)+q(v) — and that identity is how β is recovered from q, by a division by 2 that is unavailable in characteristic 2. Without it, the relation ι(u)ι(v)+ι(v)ι(u)=2β(u,v)1C collapses to ι(u)ι(v)+ι(v)ι(u)=0 and carries no information about the form at all. Proposition 13.12.3 then loses its sorting step in the form given, and Theorem 13.4.2, which supplied the orthogonal basis, has the same hypothesis.

What is missing is a definition. In characteristic 2 a quadratic form is not the same data as a symmetric bilinear form — Section 3 warned exactly this — so a quadratic form must be defined in its own right, as a function q:V→F with q(av)=a2q(v) whose associated β(u,v)=q(u+v)−q(u)−q(v) is bilinear. Clifford algebras are defined in that setting too, by the same (C1) and (C2), but this book does not define quadratic forms there and so does not go on.
