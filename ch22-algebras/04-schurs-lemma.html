22.4  Schur’s Lemma

Section 3 cut a space into irreducible pieces. The next question is the one that always follows a decomposition: what are the maps between the pieces? The answer is startling in its poverty — there are almost none, and the ones that exist are invertible — and that poverty is exactly what makes the rest of the chapter work. A one-line lemma will replace an unknown operator by a scalar, over and over.

The lemma has two parts, and they do not have the same hypotheses. The first holds over every field. The second holds only when the field is algebraically closed, and the section ends by exhibiting the failure over R in full. Along the way we get the explanation of why commuting operators share eigenvectors, a fact Chapter 8 proved without explaining.

Recall that a field F is algebraically closed if every non-constant polynomial in F[x] has a root in F. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (Theorem 5.5.1) says C is one; R and Q are not, as x2+1 and x2−2 show. Unless a statement says otherwise, F is an arbitrary field, A is an F-algebra, and A-spaces are finite-dimensional, as in Definition 22.3.2.

22.4.1. Maps that respect the action

We have objects: A-spaces. We have subobjects: invariant subspaces. What is missing is the maps, and the requirement almost writes itself. Chapter 8 §09 got everything it had out of a single hypothesis, ST=TS; read S as a map and T as the action, and that hypothesis is the one below.

An A-map is a linear map for which acting and then mapping is the same as mapping and then acting.

Definition 22.4.1 (A-Map, and Isomorphism of A-spaces)

Let A be an F-algebra and let V and W be A-spaces. A linear map φ:V→W is an A-map if φ(av)=aφ(v)for every a∈A and every v∈V. We write EndA(V) for the set of A-maps from V to itself. A bijective A-map is an isomorphism of A-spaces, and V and W are isomorphic if one exists.

In words: the two actions, on the source and on the target, are tied together by φ. Note that the letter a on the left acts on V and the one on the right acts on W; the condition is a statement about two different operators. The subscript in EndA(V) is part of the symbol and is never dropped: the book writes L(V) for all linear maps V→V, and EndA(V)⊆L(V) is the subset of those that respect the action.

Three routine facts, each one line. EndA(V) is a subalgebra of L(V) containing idV: the defining condition is linear in φ, the identity satisfies it, and if φ,ψ satisfy it then (φψ)(av)=φ(aψ(v))=a(φψ)(v). The inverse of a bijective A-map is an A-map: given w∈W write w=φ(v); then φ−1(aw)=φ−1(φ(av))=av=aφ−1(w). A composite of A-maps is an A-map, by the same computation as the first.

Examples.

  • Scalars. λidV∈EndA(V) for every λ∈F, since the action is linear. These are the A-maps we get for free, and Schur’s lemma will say that over an algebraically closed field they are all of them.
  • The commutant, again. If A⊆L(V) is a subalgebra containing idV, acting tautologically, then φ is an A-map exactly when φT=Tφ for every T∈A. So EndA(V)=A′, the commutant of Section 1 (Definition 22.1.6, Proposition 22.1.4). The two notions are the same notion.
  • A projection onto a summand. If V=U⊕W with both summands A-invariant, the projection of V onto U along W is an A-map: write v=u+w, and av=au+aw is the corresponding splitting of av, so the projection of av is au.

Non-example by minimal change. Take A=F[N]⊆L(F2) with N=J2(0), and φ=E21. Then φ(Ne2)=φ(e1)=e2 while Nφ(e2)=N0=0. Linear, but not an A-map: the clause that fails is the displayed one, at a=N and v=e2.

22.4.2. The lemma

Everything now follows from one observation: an A-map has an A-invariant kernel and an A-invariant image, and an irreducible A-space (Definition 22.3.3) has almost none of either.

Theorem 22.4.1 (Schur’s Lemma)

Let A be an F-algebra.

  • (a) Let V and W be irreducible A-spaces and let φ:V→W be an A-map. Then either φ=0 or φ is an isomorphism of A-spaces. No hypothesis on F is needed.
  • (b) Suppose in addition that F is algebraically closed, and let V be an irreducible A-space. Then every A-map φ:V→V is a scalar: φ=λidV for some λ∈F. Equivalently, EndA(V)={λidV:λ∈F}.

Idea. For (a): the kernel and the image are invariant, so each is one of the two extremes, and a single word — “φ≠0” — rules out the wrong extreme on both sides. For (b): we want to compare φ with a scalar, so we need a scalar to compare it with, and the only source of one is an eigenvalue. That is the whole role of algebraic closure. Once λ is in hand, φ−λidV is an A-map that is not injective, and part (a) says a non-zero one would have to be.

Proof.

  • (a) The kernel is A-invariant: if φ(v)=0 then φ(av)=aφ(v)=0. The image is A-invariant: aφ(v)=φ(av)∈imφ.

    Suppose φ≠0. Then kerφ≠V, and since V is irreducible its only other invariant subspace is {0}, so kerφ={0} and φ is injective (Theorem 3.2.3). Also imφ≠{0}, and since W is irreducible, imφ=W, so φ is surjective. Hence φ is bijective, and its inverse is an A-map by the remark above; so φ is an isomorphism of A-spaces.

  • (b) Since V is irreducible, V≠{0}, so n=dim⁡V≥1 and the characteristic polynomial pφ is monic of degree n, hence non-constant. As F is algebraically closed, pφ has a root λ∈F, and λ is then an eigenvalue of φ (Theorem 8.2.1).

Put ψ=φ−λidV. It is an A-map, because EndA(V) is a subspace containing idV. Its kernel is Eλ(φ)≠{0}, so ψ is not injective and therefore not an isomorphism. By (a), applied with W=V, the only remaining possibility is ψ=0, that is, φ=λidV. Conversely every scalar multiple of idV is an A-map, so EndA(V) consists exactly of these. This proves the lemma.

Part (a) says something worth naming even when the field is small: for an irreducible V, every non-zero element of EndA(V) is invertible. An algebra with that property is called a division algebra, and part (a) is the statement that the commutant of an irreducible action is one. Part (b) says that over an algebraically closed field the only division algebra that can occur is F itself. That is a genuine restriction on the field, not a formality.

Warning. Only the second part needs algebraic closure. Part (a) was proved over an arbitrary field and will be used over arbitrary fields. Part (b) used exactly one thing that (a) did not: the existence of an eigenvalue of φ. Quoting “Schur’s lemma says the commutant is the scalars” without the hypothesis is the standard error, and the next example is the standard witness.

22.4.3. What goes wrong over the reals

Example (A Real Irreducible Representation with Commutant C)

Let n≥3, put θ=2π/n, c=cos⁡θ, s=sin⁡θ, and let R=(c−ssc)∈M2(R). Since Rn=I2, the powers of R repeat with period dividing n; Section 7 will read k↦Rk as a representation of a cyclic group, and that is what we will then call the rotation representation. Let A=R[R]⊆M2(R) be the algebra of polynomials in R, so that the A-invariant subspaces of R2 are exactly the R-invariant ones. Show that R2 is an irreducible A-space and compute EndA(R2).

Solution. Irreducible. A proper non-zero invariant subspace of R2 would be a line Rv with Rv∈Rv, that is, a real eigenvector of R. Now pR(x)=x2−2cx+1, whose discriminant is 4c2−4=−4s2. For n≥3 we have 0<θ≤2π/3<π, so s=sin⁡θ>0 and the discriminant is negative: pR has no real root, hence R has no real eigenvalue (Theorem 8.2.1), hence no invariant line. So the only invariant subspaces are {0} and R2, and R2≠{0}.

The commutant. Since A acts tautologically, EndA(R2) is the commutant A′ (Definition 22.1.6): the set of X∈M2(R) commuting with every element of A; since A consists of polynomials in R, this is the set of X with XR=RX. Write X=(pqrt). Multiplying out, XR=(pc+qs−ps+qcrc+ts−rs+tc),RX=(cp−srcq−stsp+crsq+ct), so that XR−RX=s(q+rt−pt−p−(q+r)). Since s≠0, the equation XR=RX says exactly r=−q and t=p. Writing Q=(0−110) for the quarter turn, we get EndA(R2)={pI2+rQ:p,r∈R}, a two-dimensional real algebra. It is a copy of C: the map p+ri↦pI2+rQ is linear and bijective (it carries the basis 1,i to the independent I2,Q), it sends 1 to I2, and since Q2=−I2, (pI2+rQ)(p′I2+r′Q)=(pp′−rr′)I2+(pr′+rp′)Q, which matches (p+ri)(p′+r′i)=(pp′−rr′)+(pr′+rp′)i. So it is an isomorphism of R-algebras.

What this shows. Part (b) of Theorem 22.4.1 fails over R: here EndA(R2) has dimension 2, not 1. Part (a) does not fail, and we can see it directly: det(pI2+rQ)=p2+r2, which is non-zero unless p=r=0, so every non-zero element of the commutant is invertible, exactly as (a) predicts. The commutant is a division algebra; it is just not R.

Remark. The same computation with n=4 gives R=Q, and the commutant R[Q] is then the algebra generated by R itself. Over C the picture collapses: R has eigenvalues e±iθ there, so the two eigenlines are invariant and C2 is not an irreducible C[R]-space. Irreducibility, like semisimplicity, is a statement about a space and a field.

22.4.4. Families of operators

The corollaries below are about a family of operators — a set of matrices, the image of a group — rather than about an algebra given in advance. The translation costs one lemma, and Section 1’s generated subalgebra is what it produces.

Lemma 22.4.2 (A Family and the Algebra It Generates)

Let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over F and let F⊆L(V) be any set of operators. Write A(F):=F⟨F⟩ for the subalgebra of L(V) generated by F (Definition 22.1.4). Then:

  • (a) A(F) contains F and idV, and is the span of idV together with all products S1S2⋯Sm with m≥1 and S1,…,Sm∈F;
  • (b) a subspace U⊆V is invariant under every member of F if and only if it is A(F)-invariant;
  • (c) an operator S∈L(V) commutes with every member of F if and only if it commutes with every element of A(F);
  • (d) if the members of F commute with one another, then A(F) is commutative.

Proof.

  • (a) A generated subalgebra contains its generators, and every subalgebra contains the identity (Definition 22.1.1), which in L(V) is idV. The span description is Proposition 22.1.1 applied to the set F inside the algebra L(V).

  • (b) (⇐) holds because F⊆A(F). (⇒) If SU⊆U for every S∈F, then applying the members one at a time gives S1⋯SmU⊆U for every word, and idVU⊆U; a subspace is closed under linear combinations, so A(F)U⊆U.

  • (c) (⇐) again holds because F⊆A(F). (⇒) If S commutes with each Si then it commutes with S1⋯Sm, by moving S across one factor at a time; it commutes with idV; and the set of operators commuting with S is a subspace, so it contains the span.

  • (d) Let S∈F. By hypothesis S commutes with every member of F, so by (c) it commutes with every element of A(F). Now fix a∈A(F). What we have just shown says that every member of F commutes with a; applying (c) to a in place of S, the element a commutes with every element of A(F). As a was arbitrary, A(F) is commutative.

Say that a family F⊆L(V) acts irreducibly if V≠{0} and the only subspaces invariant under every member of F are {0} and V. By (a) and (b), this says exactly that V is an irreducible A(F)-space.

Corollary 22.4.3 (An Operator Commuting with an Irreducible Family Is a Scalar)

Let F be algebraically closed, let V be a finite-dimensional vector space over F, and let F⊆L(V) act irreducibly. If S∈L(V) commutes with every member of F, then S=λidV for some λ∈F.

Proof. Put A=A(F), a subalgebra of L(V) containing idV by Lemma 22.4.2 (a), so V is an A-space under the tautological representation, and by (b) it is irreducible. By (c), S commutes with every element of A, that is, S∈EndA(V). Since F is algebraically closed, Theorem 22.4.1 (b) gives S=λidV.

Corollary 22.4.4 (Over an Algebraically Closed Field, Commutative Means One-Dimensional)

Let F be algebraically closed, let A be a commutative F-algebra, and let V be an irreducible A-space. Then dim⁡V=1.

Idea. Commutativity makes every operator of the action an A-map, so Schur turns all of them into scalars at once. But an algebra of scalars leaves every subspace invariant, and only a line can survive that.

Proof. Let a∈A. For every b∈A we have ρ(a)ρ(b)=ρ(ab)=ρ(ba)=ρ(b)ρ(a), so ρ(a) is an A-map from V to itself. By Theorem 22.4.1 (b) there is λ(a)∈F with ρ(a)=λ(a)idV.

Hence av=λ(a)v for every a∈A and v∈V, so every subspace of V is A-invariant. Since V is irreducible, V≠{0}; pick v≠0. Then Fv is a non-zero A-invariant subspace, so Fv=V and dim⁡V=1.

Both hypotheses are doing work. Commutativity is needed: C2 is an irreducible M2(C)-space of dimension 2. Algebraic closure is needed: in Example (A Real Irreducible Representation with Commutant C) the algebra A=R[R] is commutative, being generated by one operator, and R2 is an irreducible A-space of dimension 2.

Quick check. The algebra A=R[R] of Example (A Real Irreducible Representation with Commutant C) is commutative and R2 is an irreducible A-space of dimension 2. Which hypothesis of Corollary 22.4.4 fails, and where exactly does the proof break?

Solution. Algebraic closure fails: R is not algebraically closed. The proof breaks at its first move, the appeal to Theorem 22.4.1 (b): the operator ρ(R)=R is indeed an A-map, but it is not a scalar, because its characteristic polynomial x2−2cx+1 has no root in R and so R has no eigenvalue to be a scalar multiple of. Everything before that point — commutativity, irreducibility, the fact that each ρ(a) is an A-map — is still true.

Section 9 will use Corollary 22.4.4 in exactly the form stated: over C, every irreducible representation of a commutative algebra, and hence of an abelian group, is one-dimensional, so its “matrix” is a single complex number.

22.4.5. Looking back at commuting operators

Chapter 8 §09 proved that a commuting family of operators, each with a splitting characteristic polynomial, has a common eigenvector (Theorem 8.9.3), and built simultaneous diagonalization on it. At the time the proof looked like a clever trick: take a minimal invariant subspace and show every operator is a scalar on it. We can now say what the trick was.

Corollary 22.4.5 (Commuting Operators Have a Common Eigenvector)

Let F be algebraically closed, let V≠{0} be finite-dimensional over F, and let F⊆L(V) be a family whose members commute with one another. Then there is v≠0 that is an eigenvector of every T∈F.

Proof. Among the non-zero subspaces of V invariant under every member of F — there is one, namely V — choose W of smallest dimension. Put A=A(F), which is a commutative subalgebra of L(V) containing idV by Lemma 22.4.2 (a), (d), and note that W is A-invariant by part (b) of the same lemma. So a↦a|W is a representation of A on W.

W is an irreducible A-space: it is non-zero, and a non-zero A-invariant subspace U⊆W is, by Lemma 22.4.2 (b), invariant under every member of F, hence has dim⁡U≥dim⁡W by the choice of W, hence equals W (Theorem 1.6.8).

By Corollary 22.4.4, dim⁡W=1. Write W=Fv with v≠0. For every T∈F we have Tv∈W=Fv, so v is an eigenvector of T. This proves the corollary.

That is Chapter 8’s argument, with its steps given their names: “minimal non-zero invariant subspace” is “irreducible”, and “every operator is a scalar on it” is Schur’s lemma. The version proved there is the stronger one — it asks only that each pT split over F, not that F be algebraically closed — and getting that extra generality is exactly what its Lemma 8.9.2 was for. What this section adds is not strength but explanation: the scalars appeared because a commutative algebra acting irreducibly over an algebraically closed field has nowhere to act except on a line.

The other half of Chapter 8 §09, simultaneous diagonalization, needs one more ingredient: that the whole space splits into such lines, not just that one of them exists. Section 3’s language names that ingredient — the algebra must act semisimply — and in the presence of an inner product it comes for free.

Proposition 22.4.6 (Closed Under Adjoints Implies Semisimple)

Let V be a finite-dimensional inner product space over F=R or F=C, and let A⊆L(V) be a subalgebra containing idV and closed under adjoints: T∈A implies T∗∈A (Definition 10.6.1). Then:

  • (a) for every A-invariant subspace U, the orthogonal complement U⊥ is A-invariant, and V=U⊕U⊥; in particular A acts semisimply on V;
  • (b) V is a direct sum of pairwise orthogonal irreducible A-invariant subspaces.

Proof.

  • (a) Let U be A-invariant, let T∈A and w∈U⊥. For every u∈U, ⟨Tw,u⟩=⟨w,T∗u⟩=0, because T∗∈A gives T∗u∈U, while w∈U⊥. So Tw∈U⊥, and U⊥ is A-invariant. The splitting V=U⊕U⊥ is Theorem 10.3.2 (a). Hence every A-invariant subspace has an A-invariant complement (Definition 22.3.4).

  • (b) Induction on dim⁡V. If V={0} it is the empty direct sum. Otherwise choose, among the non-zero A-invariant subspaces, one of smallest dimension, W; as in the proof of Theorem 22.3.3 it is irreducible. By (a), V=W⊕W⊥ with W⊥ invariant and dim⁡W⊥<dim⁡V.

The restricted algebra A|W⊥={T|W⊥:T∈A}⊆L(W⊥) satisfies the same hypotheses on the inner product space W⊥: it is a subalgebra containing idW⊥, and for w,w′∈W⊥, ⟨T|W⊥w,w′⟩=⟨Tw,w′⟩=⟨w,T∗w′⟩=⟨w,T∗|W⊥w′⟩, so (T|W⊥)∗=T∗|W⊥∈A|W⊥ by uniqueness of the adjoint (Theorem 10.6.1). By the inductive hypothesis W⊥=W1⊕⋯⊕Wr with the Wi pairwise orthogonal and irreducible, and each Wi⊆W⊥ is orthogonal to W. Hence V=W⊕W1⊕⋯⊕Wr as required.

Now take a commuting family F of self-adjoint operators on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space V≠{0}, and put A=A(F). It is commutative by Lemma 22.4.2 (d). It is closed under adjoints, because (S1⋯Sm)∗=Sm∗⋯S1∗=Sm⋯S1=S1⋯Sm, the last step by commutativity, and because φ↦φ∗ is conjugate-linear, so the span of a set of self-adjoint operators is closed under it. By Proposition 22.4.6, V is an orthogonal direct sum of irreducible A-subspaces; by Corollary 22.4.4 those subspaces are lines; and a line invariant under every member of F is spanned by a common eigenvector. Normalizing gives an orthonormal basis of common eigenvectors — Chapter 11 §07’s conclusion (Theorem 11.7.1) for a commuting self-adjoint family, obtained here by putting two general facts together rather than by an induction tailored to the situation. Chapter 11 proves more, for commuting normal families, and pays for it with a longer argument.

Warning. The converse of Corollary 22.4.3 is false. A family whose commutant is only the scalars need not act irreducibly, and the witness is short. Take F=C and let F be the algebra of upper triangular matrices in M2(C), acting on C2. A matrix commuting with E11 must be diagonal, and one commuting with E12 as well must have equal diagonal entries, so the commutant is {pI2:p∈C}, the scalars; exercise C2 does the two computations. Yet Ce1 is invariant under every upper triangular matrix, so F does not act irreducibly. Thus “the commutant is the scalars” is a consequence of irreducibility, never a test for it. The genuine converse statement, that an irreducible subalgebra is all of L(V), is Burnside’s theorem in Section 5, and it needs more than the commutant.

22.4.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) State both parts of Schur’s lemma and say precisely which one needs F to be algebraically closed, and what that hypothesis is used for.
  • (b) True or false: if V and W are irreducible A-spaces with dim⁡V≠dim⁡W, then the only A-map V→W is 0. Justify your answer.
  • (c) Explain why, for an irreducible A-space V, no element of EndA(V) can be non-zero and non-invertible.
  • (d) Give EndA(R2) for the rotation representation of Example (A Real Irreducible Representation with Commutant C), and say which part of Schur’s lemma it illustrates and which it refutes.

Solution.

  • (a) Part (a): an A-map between irreducible A-spaces is 0 or an isomorphism; no hypothesis on F. Part (b): over an algebraically closed F, every A-map from an irreducible V to itself is a scalar. Algebraic closure is used once, to produce a root of the characteristic polynomial of φ, that is, an eigenvalue.

  • (b) True. A non-zero A-map would be an isomorphism by Theorem 22.4.1 (a), and an isomorphism of vector spaces preserves dimension (Theorem 3.5.4), contradicting dim⁡V≠dim⁡W.

  • (c) By Theorem 22.4.1 (a) with W=V: a non-zero A-map V→V is an isomorphism, hence invertible.

  • (d) EndA(R2)={pI2+rQ}≅C. It illustrates part (a) — every non-zero element has determinant p2+r2≠0, so the commutant is a division algebra — and refutes part (b) read without its hypothesis, since the commutant is two-dimensional over R.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: An irreducibility test)

Let D=diag(i,−i)∈M2(C), which satisfies D4=I2, and let A=C[D] be the algebra of polynomials in D.

  • (a) Compute EndA(C2).
  • (b) Deduce from Theorem 22.4.1 (b) that C2 is not an irreducible A-space, and confirm this by exhibiting a proper non-zero invariant subspace.

Solution.

  • (a) An operator is an A-map exactly when it commutes with D, since the elements of A are the polynomials in D. Writing X=(pqrt), XD−DX=(0−2iq2ir0), which vanishes exactly when q=r=0. So EndA(C2) is the algebra of diagonal matrices, of dimension 2.

  • (b) If C2 were irreducible then, C being algebraically closed, Theorem 22.4.1 (b) would force EndA(C2) to have dimension 1. It has dimension 2, so C2 is not irreducible. Indeed Ce1 is invariant, since De1=ie1.

Exercise (B2: The quarter turn, over two fields)

Let Q=(0−110), the rotation by a quarter turn, and let AF=F[Q] for F=R and for F=C.

  • (a) Show that R2 is an irreducible AR-space and that EndAR(R2)≅C.
  • (b) Show that C2 is not an irreducible AC-space, and find two invariant lines.
  • (c) Explain why (a) and (b) together are consistent with Schur’s lemma.

Solution.

  • (a) This is Example (A Real Irreducible Representation with Commutant C) with n=4, so θ=π/2, c=0, s=1: the characteristic polynomial x2+1 has no real root, so there is no invariant line and R2 is irreducible; and the commutant is {pI2+rQ}≅C.

  • (b) Over C the polynomial x2+1 has the roots ±i, so Q has eigenvalues i and −i, with eigenvectors (1,−i) and (1,i) respectively: from Q(1,−i)=(i,1)=i(1,−i) and Q(1,i)=(−i,1)=−i(1,i). Each of C(1,i) and C(1,−i) is a proper non-zero invariant line.

  • (c) Part (b) of Schur’s lemma applies only over an algebraically closed field. Over C it does apply, and it is not contradicted: the space is reducible there, so the lemma says nothing about it. Over R the space is irreducible but the field is not algebraically closed, so only part (a) applies — and part (a) holds, since every non-zero element of {pI2+rQ} is invertible.

Exercise (B3: A common eigenvector)

Let A=(2102),B=(3503)∈M2(C).

  • (a) Verify that AB=BA.
  • (b) Find a common eigenvector, and check that it spans the unique minimal non-zero invariant subspace, as in the proof of Corollary 22.4.5.

Solution.

  • (a) The diagonal entries of both products are 2⋅3=6, the lower left entry of both is 0, and the upper right entries are (AB)12=2⋅5+1⋅3=13 and (BA)12=3⋅1+5⋅2=13. So AB=BA=(61306).

  • (b) Ae1=2e1 and Be1=3e1, so e1 is a common eigenvector and Ce1 is invariant. It is the only invariant line: if v=(x,y) with y≠0 spanned an invariant line, then Av=(2x+y,2y) would be a multiple cv, forcing c=2 from the second coordinate and then y=0 from the first. So Ce1 is the minimal non-zero invariant subspace the proof of Corollary 22.4.5 produces, and it is a line, as Corollary 22.4.4 requires.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: A commutant that is a field)

Let A=(0210)∈M2(Q), the companion matrix of x2−2, and let A=Q[A].

  • (a) Prove that Q2 is an irreducible A-space.
  • (b) Compute EndA(Q2) and prove that it is a field in which 2 has a square root.
  • (c) Explain why this does not contradict Theorem 22.4.1.

Solution.

  • (a) pA=x2−2 by Theorem 9.5.3, and x2−2 has no rational root, since 2 is irrational (Theorem 0.2.3). So A has no eigenvalue in Q (Theorem 8.2.1), hence no invariant line, and the only invariant subspaces of Q2 are {0} and Q2.

  • (b) An A-map is an X=(pqrt) commuting with A. Computing, XA−AX=(q−2r2p−2tt−p2r−q), which vanishes exactly when t=p and q=2r. So EndA(Q2)={pI2+rA:p,r∈Q}, using A=(0210). Since A2=2I2, this is a commutative algebra in which A is a square root of 2I2; and every non-zero element is invertible, because det(pI2+rA)=p2−2r2, which vanishes for rational p,r only when p=r=0: if r=0 then p2=0, and if r≠0 then (p/r)2=2 with p/r∈Q, contradicting Theorem 0.2.3. A commutative algebra in which every non-zero element is invertible is a field.

  • (c) Part (a) of Theorem 22.4.1 predicts exactly this: the commutant of an irreducible action is a division algebra. Part (b) does not apply, because Q is not algebraically closed.

Exercise (C2: Scalar commutant without irreducibility)

Let F be algebraically closed — say F=C, so that Corollary 22.4.3 really applies — and let A⊆M2(F) be the algebra of upper triangular matrices.

  • (a) Compute the set of X∈M2(F) commuting with every element of A. (The computation uses nothing about F.)
  • (b) Deduce that the converse of Corollary 22.4.3 fails: a family with commutant the scalars need not act irreducibly.

Solution.

  • (a) It suffices to commute with the basis E11,E12,E22. Write X=(pqrt). From XE11−E11X=(0−qr0) we get q=r=0, and then XE12−E12X=(0p−t00) gives p=t. Conversely a scalar matrix commutes with everything. So the commutant is {pI2:p∈F}, the scalars.

  • (b) The family F=A has commutant the scalars by (a), yet it does not act irreducibly on F2: the line Fe1 is invariant, as computed in Example (Upper Triangular Matrices Are Not Semisimple). So the implication in Corollary 22.4.3 cannot be reversed. (The hypothesis is not vacuous either: over an algebraically closed field the corollary does apply whenever the family is irreducible.)
