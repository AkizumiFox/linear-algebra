22.6  The Structure Theorem

§05 settled the extreme case: over an algebraically closed field, an algebra of operators with no invariant subspace to hide in is the whole of End(V). Most algebras are not like that. This section shows that, over an algebraically closed field and under one hypothesis — semisimplicity — that extreme case is nevertheless the only building block there is. Every such algebra is a product of full matrix algebras, and the sizes of the blocks are determined by the algebra. That is the structure theorem, and it is the last piece of general theory the chapter needs: from here on the work is to apply it to groups.

Throughout, F is a field and A is an associative algebra with identity, finite-dimensional over F and with A≠{0}.

22.6.1. Products of matrix algebras

Before proving that something is a product of matrix algebras, we should say what the product is and look at a few.

A product of algebras is a list of algebras run side by side, with nothing passing between the slots.

Definition 22.6.1 (Product of algebras)

Let A1,…,Ak be F-algebras. Their product A1×⋯×Ak is the product vector space (Definition 3.9.1) with multiplication defined slot by slot, (x1,…,xk)(y1,…,yk)=(x1y1,…,xkyk), and identity (1A1,…,1Ak).

The axioms of Definition 13.12.1 hold slot by slot because they hold in each Ai, and dim⁡(A1×⋯×Ak)=∑idim⁡Ai by Theorem 3.9.1. Two features of a product will be used constantly. First, the element ei with 1Ai in slot i and 0 elsewhere satisfies ei2=ei, eiej=0 for i≠j, and e1+⋯+ek=1. Second, the product is almost never commutative, but its center always contains the k-dimensional span of e1,…,ek.

Example (Three products, seen from two sides)

Identify each of the following with a product of matrix algebras over F, and give its dimension: (i) the diagonal matrices in Mn(F); (ii) Mn(F) itself; (iii) the block diagonal matrices in M5(F) with blocks of sizes 2,2,1, where the two 2×2 blocks are allowed to differ.

Solution.

  • (i) The map diag(c1,…,cn)↦(c1,…,cn) is a bijection, is linear, sends In to (1,…,1), and is multiplicative because diagonal matrices multiply entry by entry. So the diagonal matrices form F×⋯×F=M1(F)n, of dimension n=∑i=1n12.

  • (ii) Mn(F) is the product with one slot, k=1 and n1=n, of dimension n2.

  • (iii) Writing a block diagonal matrix as the list of its blocks gives M2(F)×M2(F)×M1(F), since block diagonal matrices multiply blockwise (Theorem 7.1.4, applied twice). Its dimension is 4+4+1=9=22+22+12.

In all three the dimension is ∑ini2. The theorem below says that this is not a coincidence of the examples.

22.6.2. The regular representation

An abstract algebra has no vectors to act on until we give it some, and the cheapest supply is the algebra itself.

Remark. The vocabulary is §03’s and §04’s. An A-space is a vector space V with an algebra homomorphism A→End(V), written av (Definition 22.3.2); invariant subspace and irreducible A-space are as in Definition 22.3.3; and a homomorphism of A-spaces f:V→W is what §04 calls an A-map, a linear map with f(av)=af(v) (Definition 22.4.1). A bijective one is an isomorphism, and we then write V≅W.

The regular representation of A, introduced in §03 (Definition 22.3.2), is A acting on itself by left multiplication: L:A→End(A), La(x)=ax. It is injective because La(1A)=a; this is exactly Theorem 22.1.3. Its invariant subspaces are the subspaces I⊆A with AI⊆I, which is to say the left ideals of §02. So the irreducible invariant subspaces of the regular representation are the minimal left ideals: the non-zero left ideals containing no smaller non-zero left ideal.

We call A semisimple when the regular representation is semisimple in the sense of §03 (Definition 22.3.4): every left ideal of A has a complementary left ideal.

22.6.3. The structure theorem

Theorem 22.6.1 (Wedderburn’s Structure Theorem, Algebraically Closed Case)

Let F be an algebraically closed field and let A≠{0} be a semisimple finite-dimensional F-algebra: every left ideal of A has a complementary left ideal. Then there are an integer k≥1, integers n1,…,nk≥1 and an isomorphism of F-algebras A≅Mn1(F)×⋯×Mnk(F). Moreover k and the multiset {n1,…,nk} depend only on A: they are the number of isomorphism classes of irreducible A-spaces and their dimensions.

Idea. Step roadmap. ① Break the regular representation into minimal left ideals. ② Keep one from each isomorphism class, say V1,…,Vk, and map A into End(V1)×⋯×End(Vk) by letting it act on each. ③ The map is injective because an element acting as zero on every Vi acts as zero on all of A, hence kills 1. ④ It is surjective: on W=V1⊕⋯⊕Vk, Schur’s lemma computes the commutant of the image to be the scalars-on-each-slot, and Theorem 22.5.5 turns that into the statement that the image is everything preserving the slots. ⑤ Uniqueness: read the ni off as the dimensions of the irreducible A-spaces, which no choice in the proof can change.

Proof. Step 1: split the regular representation. Regard A as an A-space by left multiplication. Its invariant subspaces are the left ideals, and by hypothesis every one of them has an invariant complement. By Theorem 22.3.3, (a) ⇒ (c), A is a direct sum of finitely many irreducible invariant subspaces: A=L1⊕⋯⊕Lm,Lj a minimal left ideal. Since A≠{0} we have m≥1.

Step 2: one representative per class. Being isomorphic is an equivalence relation on the list L1,…,Lm; pick one member from each class and call the chosen spaces V1,…,Vk. They are pairwise non-isomorphic, and every Lj is isomorphic to exactly one of them. Put ni=dim⁡Vi≥1 and let ρi:A→End(Vi) be the homomorphism giving the action. Then k≥1.

Step 3: the map, and its injectivity. Let Φ:A→End(V1)×⋯×End(Vk),Φ(a)=(ρ1(a),…,ρk(a)). Each ρi is an algebra homomorphism and the product has slot-by-slot operations (Definition 22.6.1), so Φ is an algebra homomorphism, and Φ(1A) is the identity.

Suppose Φ(a)=0. Fix j, let i be the index with Lj≅Vi, and let f:Lj→Vi be an isomorphism of A-spaces. For x∈Lj we get f(ax)=ρi(a)f(x)=0, and f is injective, so ax=0. Thus aLj={0} for every j, and since A=L1+⋯+Lm we get aA={0}. In particular a=a1A=0. So Φ is injective.

Step 4: surjectivity. Let W=V1⊕⋯⊕Vk be the product space, with A acting slot by slot, and let B⊆End(W) be the image of A under this action; B is a subalgebra containing idW. Let D⊆End(W) be the set of operators mapping each Vi into itself. Restricting to the slots identifies D with End(V1)×⋯×End(Vk) as an algebra, and under that identification Φ(A) corresponds to B. So it suffices to prove B=D.

B acts semisimply on W. Each Vi is B-invariant, and a subspace of Vi is B-invariant exactly when it is A-invariant, so each Vi is an irreducible B-invariant subspace; and W=V1+⋯+Vk. By Theorem 22.3.3, B acts semisimply.

The commutant of B. Let ιj:Vj→W and πi:W→Vi be the slot inclusions and projections, and for s∈End(W) put sij=πi∘s∘ιj:Vj→Vi. Then s commutes with the action of every a∈A if and only if sijρj(a)=ρi(a)sijfor all i,j,a, that is, if and only if every sij is a homomorphism of A-spaces. For i≠j the spaces Vi and Vj are irreducible and non-isomorphic, so Theorem 22.4.1 gives sij=0. For i=j the field is algebraically closed and Vi is irreducible, so the second part of Theorem 22.4.1 gives sii=λiidVi for some λi∈F. Conversely every operator of that shape commutes with B. Hence B′={λ1idV1⊕⋯⊕λkidVk:λi∈F}.

The double commutant of B. Let pi∈End(W) be the projection onto Vi along the other slots; it is the member of B′ with λi=1 and the other λj=0. If t∈B″, then t commutes with pi, so t(Vi)=t(impi)=im(tpi)=im(pit)⊆impi=Vi, which says t∈D. Conversely every t∈D commutes with each λ1id⊕⋯⊕λkid, so D⊆B″. Hence B″=D.

Conclusion. B is a subalgebra of End(W) containing idW and acting semisimply, so Theorem 22.5.5 gives B=B″=D. Therefore Φ is surjective, and with Step 3 it is an isomorphism of algebras onto End(V1)×⋯×End(Vk).

Step 5: matrices. Choosing a basis Bi of Vi makes T↦[T]Bi a linear bijection End(Vi)→Mni(F) (Theorem 3.6.4) which is multiplicative (Theorem 3.6.2) and sends idVi to Ini, hence an isomorphism of algebras. Doing this in each slot gives A≅Mn1(F)×⋯×Mnk(F).

Step 6: uniqueness. We first identify all irreducible A-spaces.

Claim. If A=∑jLj with each Lj an irreducible left ideal, then every irreducible A-space is isomorphic to some Lj.

Proof. Let U be an irreducible A-space and pick u∈U, u≠0. The map f:A→U, f(a)=au, is a homomorphism of A-spaces from the regular representation, since f(ba)=(ba)u=bf(a). Its image is an invariant subspace containing f(1A)=u≠0, hence equals U by irreducibility. From A=∑jLj we get U=∑jf(Lj), so f(Lj0)≠{0} for some j0. Then f|Lj0:Lj0→U is a non-zero homomorphism between irreducible A-spaces, hence an isomorphism by Theorem 22.4.1.

Now let B0=Mp1(F)×⋯×Mpl(F) be any product of matrix algebras, and let Cj=Fpj carry the action of B0 through the j-th slot. Each Cj is irreducible: given v≠0 in Fpj and any w, extend v to a basis (Theorem 1.6.5) and let X be the matrix sending v↦w and the other basis vectors to 0; then Mpj(F)v=Fpj, so no proper non-zero invariant subspace exists. They are pairwise non-isomorphic: the idempotent ej∈B0 acts as the identity on Cj and as 0 on Ci for i≠j, so an isomorphism f:Cj→Ci would give f(v)=f(ejv)=ejf(v)=0 for every v, forcing f=0. Finally, for each j and each column index c let Cj,c⊆B0 consist of the elements whose slots other than j are 0 and whose j-th slot has all columns but the c-th equal to 0. Each Cj,c is a left ideal, the map x↦xjec is an isomorphism of B0-spaces Cj,c→Cj, and B0 is the direct sum of the Cj,c. So by the Claim the irreducible B0-spaces are, up to isomorphism, exactly C1,…,Cl: there are l of them and their dimensions are p1,…,pl.

An isomorphism of algebras A→B0 turns B0-spaces into A-spaces, preserving irreducibility and isomorphism. So if A≅Mn1(F)×⋯×Mnk(F), then A has exactly k isomorphism classes of irreducible spaces and their dimensions are n1,…,nk. Both quantities are determined by the isomorphism class of A alone, so they do not depend on the choices made in Steps 1 and 2. This proves the theorem.

Remark. The case k=1 says that a semisimple algebra with only one irreducible space, up to isomorphism, is a full matrix algebra. Burnside’s theorem (Theorem 22.5.2) is the concrete counterpart of that sentence: there the algebra arrives already written as operators on an irreducible space, its hypothesis is irreducibility rather than semisimplicity, and its conclusion is the sharper equality A=End(V) in place of an isomorphism. The proof above does not use Theorem 22.5.2; what it borrows from §05 is the double commutant theorem.

Two hypotheses carried the proof, and it is worth saying again where. Semisimplicity was used twice: to split the regular representation in Step 1, and to apply the double commutant theorem in Step 4. Algebraic closure was used once, in the second half of Schur’s lemma, to know that a homomorphism of an irreducible space to itself is a scalar. Drop algebraic closure and the diagonal slots become division algebras rather than F; that is the general theorem, quoted below and not proved.

Corollary 22.6.2 (Counting Dimensions in the Wedderburn Form)

In the situation of Theorem 22.6.1, dimF⁡A=n12+⋯+nk2, and the center Z(A)={z∈A:za=az for all a∈A} has dimF⁡Z(A)=k.

Proof. The dimension of a product is the sum of the dimensions (Theorem 3.9.1) and dim⁡Mni(F)=ni2, which gives the first formula. An isomorphism of algebras carries the center onto the center, so we may compute in Mn1(F)×⋯×Mnk(F). Multiplication is slot by slot, so (z1,…,zk) is central exactly when each zi is central in Mni(F), and by Proposition 22.1.5, read through the identification of Mni(F) with End(Fni), that means zi=λiIni. So Z(A) is isomorphic to Fk as a vector space, of dimension k.

The two numbers in the corollary are the whole invariant content of a semisimple algebra over an algebraically closed field: k is read from the center, and then the ni are k positive integers with ∑ni2=dim⁡A. When A is the group algebra C[G] of a finite group, whose dimension is |G| by Definition 22.1.5, this becomes the sum-of-squares formula |G|=∑ini2 for the dimensions of the irreducible representations, and k turns out to be the number of conjugacy classes. Both statements are proved later in this chapter, once Maschke’s theorem supplies the missing hypothesis that C[G] is semisimple.

Quick check. A semisimple algebra A over C has dim⁡A=10 and dim⁡Z(A)=4. What are the possible block sizes?

Solution. By Corollary 22.6.2 we need k=4 positive integers with n12+⋯+n42=10. No ni can be 3 or more: that block alone contributes at least 9, and the other three contribute at least 3, for a total of at least 12. So every ni is 1 or 2; if t of them equal 2, the sum is 4t+(4−t)=10, so t=2. Up to order the block sizes are 1,1,2,2 and A≅C×C×M2(C)×M2(C).

22.6.4. What is quoted and not proved

The theorem above is the case this book can carry. The general statement is older and stronger, and we record it honestly.

Remark. The Wedderburn–Artin theorem. Over an arbitrary field F, a finite-dimensional semisimple F-algebra is isomorphic to a product Mn1(D1)×⋯×Mnk(Dk), where each Di is a division algebra over F in the sense of §04 — an F-algebra, here finite-dimensional and not assumed commutative, in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse. The result is due to Wedderburn, and Artin extended it beyond finite dimension. We do not prove it. The reason is not that the argument is long — it follows the same route, with Schur’s lemma supplying EndA(Vi)=Di in place of F — but that this book has never built the theory of division rings. The only non-commutative division algebra it has met is the quaternions H over R (Definition 13.12.3), and one example is not a theory. Nothing in this chapter depends on the general statement.

Remark. Wedderburn’s little theorem. Every finite division ring is commutative, hence a field. This is a separate theorem of Wedderburn’s, and we do not prove it either; the standard proofs use counting arguments in group theory or cyclotomic polynomials, neither of which this book has developed. Its effect on the theorem above is that over a finite field the division algebras Di are all fields, so the blocks are matrix algebras over field extensions of F.

Warning. Semisimplicity is not automatic, so the theorem does not apply to every algebra. The upper triangular matrices in M2(F) form a three-dimensional algebra which is not a product of matrix algebras: a product Mn1(F)×⋯×Mnk(F) of total dimension 3 would need ∑ni2=3, hence k=3 and all ni=1, hence a commutative algebra; but E11E12=E12 while E12E11=0. What fails is semisimplicity: as §05 noted, span(e1) is an invariant subspace with no invariant complement. §03 identifies the obstruction as the radical of the algebra (Definition 22.3.5).

22.6.5. Two worked decompositions

Example (The group algebra of a cyclic group of order four)

Let C4={1,g,g2,g3} be a cyclic group of order 4, so g4=1; it exists, for instance as the subgroup {1,i,−1,−i} of (C∖{0},⋅) with g=i (Theorem 0.10.2). Find the Wedderburn form of the group algebra C[C4] (Definition 22.1.5) explicitly, and exhibit the idempotents e1,…,e4 of the product.

Solution. Let ω1=1, ω2=i, ω3=−1, ω4=−i be the four complex numbers with ω4=1. Define Φ:C[C4]→C4,Φ(∑t=03atgt)=(∑tatωrt)r=14. It is linear by construction. It is multiplicative: it is enough to check on the basis gs,gt, where gsgt=gs+t with the exponent read modulo 4, and ωrs+t=ωrsωrt with ωr4=1 making the reduction harmless. It sends 1=g0 to (1,1,1,1). Its matrix in the basis (1,g,g2,g3) of the source and the standard basis of C4 is (11111i−1−i1−11−11−i−1i), whose determinant is −16i≠0, so Φ is bijective. Hence C[C4]≅C×C×C×C=M1(C)4, with k=4 and n1=n2=n3=n4=1; the dimension count ∑ni2=4=|C4| checks out, and Z(C[C4]) is everything, of dimension 4, as it must be for a commutative algebra.

The idempotents pull back to er=14∑t=03ωrt―gt,r=1,…,4, explicitly e1=14(1+g+g2+g3),e2=14(1−ig−g2+ig3),e3=14(1−g+g2−g3),e4=14(1+ig−g2−ig3). A direct expansion confirms er2=er, eres=0 for r≠s, and e1+e2+e3+e4=1. For instance 16e22=(1−ig−g2+ig3)2=4−4ig−4g2+4ig3=16e2, where the middle equality collects the sixteen products (±1,±i)gs+t with g4=1.

The matrix in that example is, up to the factor 12 that normalizes its columns, the Fourier matrix of size four (Example (The Fourier matrix of size four), Theorem 11.9.3). That is not a coincidence, and a later section of this chapter explains it: the characters of a finite abelian group assemble into a Fourier matrix, and the isomorphism above is the discrete Fourier transform.

Example (An algebra already in Wedderburn form)

What is the Wedderburn form of M2(C), and what are its irreducible spaces?

Solution. It is M2(C) itself: k=1 and n1=2, with dim=4=22 and dim⁡Z=1, the scalars (Proposition 22.1.5). To see that the theorem applies, note that M2(C)=C1⊕C2 where Cc is the set of matrices whose only non-zero column is the c-th; each Cc is a left ideal isomorphic to C2 as an M2(C)-space, and C2 is irreducible, so Theorem 22.3.3 makes the algebra semisimple. There is exactly one isomorphism class of irreducible space, namely C2, of dimension 2 — which is Step 6’s description of k and n1. Note that M2(C) is not a product of two smaller algebras: it is simple (Theorem 22.2.2), while a product with k≥2 has the proper non-zero two-sided ideal e1A.

22.6.6. Exercises

A. Check your understanding

Exercise (A1)
  • (a) State Theorem 22.6.1 with all of its hypotheses, and say which hypothesis fails for the upper triangular matrices in M2(C).
  • (b) Where in the proof is algebraic closure used, and what replaces the conclusion when it is dropped?
  • (c) A semisimple algebra over C has dimension 5. List all possibilities for its Wedderburn form.
  • (d) True or false, with a reason: a commutative semisimple algebra over C of dimension n is isomorphic to Cn.

Solution.

  • (a) F algebraically closed, A≠{0} finite-dimensional over F, and A semisimple, that is, every left ideal has a complementary left ideal. For the upper triangular matrices semisimplicity fails: span(e1) is an invariant subspace of the natural action with no invariant complement, and correspondingly the left ideal of matrices with second row zero has no complementary left ideal.

  • (b) In Step 4, in the second half of Theorem 22.4.1, to know that sii is a scalar. Without algebraic closure the slots become Mni(Di) for division algebras Di, which is the Wedderburn–Artin theorem quoted above and not proved here.

  • (c) We need positive integers with ∑ni2=5: either 1+4, giving C×M2(C), or 1+1+1+1+1, giving C5.

  • (d) True. By Theorem 22.6.1, A≅∏iMni(C), and Mni(C) is commutative only for ni=1 (otherwise E11E12≠E12E11). So every ni=1 and k=∑ni2=n.

B. Practice

Exercise (B1: Determine which are semisimple)

Determine which of the following algebras over C are semisimple, and give the Wedderburn form of those that are. Justify your answer.

  • (a) The diagonal matrices in M3(C).
  • (b) C[x]/(x2).
  • (c) C[x]/(x2−1).

Solution.

  • (a) Semisimple, and equal to C×C×C by Example (Three products, seen from two sides) (i). As a check on semisimplicity: the algebra is the direct sum of the three left ideals CEii, each of dimension 1 and hence irreducible, so Theorem 22.3.3 applies.

  • (b) Not semisimple. Both algebras here are quotients of the infinite-dimensional algebra C[x] by the ideal generated by one polynomial (Definition 5.2.1, Definition 22.2.2), and each has dimension 2, a basis being the classes of 1 and x. Write ε for the class of x in C[x]/(x2), so the algebra is {a+bε} with ε2=0. The line Cε is a left ideal, and it is the only one-dimensional one: a left ideal spanned by a+bε with a≠0 contains ε(a+bε)=aε, hence ε, hence is at least two-dimensional. So Cε has no complementary left ideal, which would have to be one-dimensional. Consistently, a product of matrix algebras of dimension 2 would be C×C, which has no non-zero element squaring to 0.

  • (c) Semisimple, with Wedderburn form C×C. The map p+(x2−1)↦(p(1),p(−1)) is linear, multiplicative and unital, and on the representatives a+bx it reads (a+b,a−b), which vanishes only for a=b=0; an injective linear map between spaces of dimension 2 is bijective (Theorem 3.3.1). A product of copies of C is semisimple by the argument in (a).

Exercise (B2: A dimension count)

Let A be a semisimple algebra over C with dim⁡A=8 and dim⁡Z(A)=5. Determine the Wedderburn form of A, and the dimensions of the irreducible A-spaces.

Solution. By Corollary 22.6.2, k=dim⁡Z(A)=5 and n12+⋯+n52=8 with every ni≥1. Writing 8=5+3, we need ∑(ni2−1)=3, and n2−1 takes the values 0,3,8,… for n=1,2,3,…. So exactly one ni equals 2 and the rest equal 1. Hence A≅C×C×C×C×M2(C), and the irreducible A-spaces have dimensions 1,1,1,1,2.

Exercise (B3: The group algebra of a group of order three)

Let C3={1,g,g2} with g3=1. Imitating Example (The group algebra of a cyclic group of order four), produce an explicit algebra isomorphism C[C3]→C3 and write down the three idempotents.

Solution. Let ω=e2πi/3, so 1,ω,ω2 are the cube roots of 1 and 1+ω+ω2=0. Define Φ(∑t=02atgt)=(∑tat, ∑tatωt, ∑tatω2t). As in the example, Φ is linear, multiplicative (because ωs+t=ωsωt and ω3=1) and unital. Its matrix has rows (1,1,1), (1,ω,ω2), (1,ω2,ω); it is a Vandermonde matrix in the distinct nodes 1,ω,ω2, hence invertible. So C[C3]≅C3, and ∑ni2=3=|C3|. The idempotents are e1=13(1+g+g2),e2=13(1+ω2g+ωg2),e3=13(1+ωg+ω2g2), the r-th being 13∑tω(r−1)t―gt.

C. Going deeper

Exercise (C1: Simple plus semisimple)

Let F be algebraically closed and let A≠{0} be a finite-dimensional semisimple F-algebra.

  • (a) Prove that A is simple (has no two-sided ideal but {0} and A) if and only if k=1, that is, A≅Mn(F) for some n.
  • (b) Deduce that a finite-dimensional simple semisimple algebra over C has square dimension.

Hint for (a): in a product, the set of elements whose slots after the first are zero is a two-sided ideal.

Solution.

  • (a) (⇐) If k=1 then A≅Mn(F), which is simple by Theorem 22.2.2, and simplicity is preserved by an algebra isomorphism.

    (⇒) Suppose k≥2 and write A≅Mn1(F)×⋯×Mnk(F). The set I of elements whose slots 2,…,k are 0 is closed under addition and under multiplication by any element on either side, because multiplication is slot by slot; so I is a two-sided ideal. It is non-zero, since e1∈I, and it is not all of A, since e2∉I. So A is not simple. Contrapositively, simple forces k=1.

  • (b) By (a), A≅Mn(F), so dim⁡A=n2 by Corollary 22.6.2 with k=1.
Exercise (C2: How often each irreducible appears)

Keep the notation of Theorem 22.6.1 and of its proof, so that A=L1⊕⋯⊕Lm with each Lj isomorphic to exactly one Vi. Let mi be the number of indices j with Lj≅Vi. For A-spaces V,W write HomA(V,W) for the vector space of homomorphisms of A-spaces V→W.

  • (a) Prove that f↦f(1A) is a linear bijection HomA(A,V)→V for every A-space V, where A carries the regular representation.
  • (b) Prove that dim⁡HomA(Lj,Vi) is 1 if Lj≅Vi and 0 otherwise.
  • (c) Deduce that mi=ni: each irreducible appears in the regular representation exactly as many times as its dimension. Check the result against Corollary 22.6.2.

Hint for (b): Theorem 22.4.1, both parts.

Solution.

  • (a) The map is linear. It is injective: if f(1A)=0 then f(a)=f(a1A)=af(1A)=0 for every a. It is surjective: given v∈V, the map a↦av is linear and satisfies (ba)v=b(av), so it is a homomorphism of A-spaces sending 1A to v.

  • (b) If Lj≇Vi, the first part of Theorem 22.4.1 says every homomorphism between these irreducible spaces is 0, so the dimension is 0. If Lj≅Vi, fix an isomorphism h; then f↦h−1f is a linear bijection HomA(Lj,Vi)→HomA(Lj,Lj), and by the second part of Theorem 22.4.1, valid because F is algebraically closed, every self-homomorphism of the irreducible space Lj is a scalar. So the dimension is 1.

  • (c) A homomorphism out of a direct sum is the list of its restrictions to the summands, so HomA(A,Vi) is linearly isomorphic to ⨁jHomA(Lj,Vi), of dimension mi by (b). By (a) that dimension is dim⁡Vi=ni. Hence mi=ni. As a check, dim⁡A=∑jdim⁡Lj=∑imini=∑ini2, which is Corollary 22.6.2.

Exercise (C3: Change the field)

Let A={xI2+yQ:x,y∈R}⊆M2(R), where Q=(0−110).

  • (a) Prove that A is a semisimple R-algebra of dimension 2.
  • (b) Show that A is not isomorphic to a product Mn1(R)×⋯×Mnk(R), and identify which hypothesis of Theorem 22.6.1 fails.
  • (c) Prove that A is itself a division algebra over R and identify it.

Solution.

  • (a) Q2=−I2, so A is closed under multiplication and is a subalgebra containing I2, of dimension 2. Every non-zero element xI2+yQ has determinant x2+y2>0, hence is invertible in M2(R), and its inverse (x2+y2)−1(xI2−yQ) lies in A. So every non-zero left ideal contains an invertible element and hence equals A; the only left ideals are {0} and A, and each has a complementary left ideal (the other one). So A is semisimple.

  • (b) A product with ∑ni2=2 must be R×R, which contains the non-zero element (1,0) with (1,0)(0,1)=(0,0); but in A the product of two non-zero elements is non-zero, since both are invertible. So no such isomorphism exists. The hypothesis that fails is algebraic closure: R is not algebraically closed.

  • (c) By (a) every non-zero element of A is invertible, and its inverse again lies in A, so A is a division algebra over R; it is a copy of C, the map xI2+yQ↦x+yi being a bijective algebra homomorphism. This is the D1 that would appear in the quoted Wedderburn–Artin form of A.
