**Purpose.** Chapters 16 and 17 answered two questions: *do* eigenvalues move continuously (yes, by a quoted theorem), and *how far* do the eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix move (by at most ‖E‖₂, Weyl). This chapter answers the rest.
- **Where** are the eigenvalues, before computing any of them? This is Gershgorin and the other inclusion regions.
- **How far** do they move for a non-Hermitian matrix? This is the continuity of roots, now proved, Elsner's bound, and Bauer–Fike.
- **Which** eigenvalue goes where? This is Hoffman–Wielandt.
- **How far** do *eigenvectors and invariant subspaces* move? This is the Sylvester equation, principal angles, and Davis–Kahan.
- How do the singular values and the polar factors move?
- How sensitive is one simple eigenvalue, to first order?

**The chapter's big discovery (coordinator, verified).** Chapter 16 §07 quoted `thm-roots-depend-continuously` without proof and said a proof "needs complex analysis". **That is false. The theorem follows from compactness, fact (A3), and nothing else.**
- The roots of a monic polynomial are bounded by 1 + max|c_j|.
- The coefficients are polynomials in the roots.
- A limit of root vectors is therefore a root vector of the limit polynomial, and unique factorization in ℂ[x] pins down its multiset.

§03 proves it. The chapter must say so plainly and without fanfare. It records that the theorem Chapter 16 quoted is now a theorem of the book, and that `cor-eigenvalues-continuous` thereby rests on proved ground. **Do not edit Chapter 16.** Its sentence is the coordinator's to handle.

**Field.** The field is ℂ throughout, unless a statement says ℝ. Hermitian matrices have eigenvalues λ₁ ≥ … ≥ λₙ (NOTATION), and `\vlambda(\A)` is the decreasing vector.

**Conventions to state once and obey.**
- **The perturbation is `\E`**, and the perturbed matrix is `\A + \E` (Ch 16, Ch 17 precedent). Write `\widetilde{\A}` only when the perturbed matrix is not naturally A + E, as in §09.
- **Norms.**
  - `\norm{\cdot}_2` is the spectral norm, and `\norm{\cdot}_F` is the Frobenius norm.
  - Every bound says which norm it is in.
  - "‖·‖" without a subscript is an arbitrary operator norm, and only where the statement really holds for all of them.
- **Distances between spectra.** Say "every eigenvalue of A + E lies within r of some eigenvalue of A" (a one-sided spectral variation) as distinct from "the eigenvalues can be **numbered** so that each pair is within r" (a matching). This distinction is the point of §§03–05. Never blur it.
- **Local notation, recorded by the coordinator in NOTATION.md:**
  - `r_i(\A)` is the i-th Gershgorin radius Σ_{j≠i}|a_ij|. The column radii are `r_i(\A\tp)`. Plain r, never R, because `R_A` is the Rayleigh quotient.

  - `O_{ij}(\A)` is the Brauer oval.

  - `\operatorname{sep}(\A,\B)` is the separation.
  - θ₁ ≤ … ≤ θ_k are the principal angles, and `\Theta(U,W)` is diag(θ_i).
  - `\kappa_{\A}(\lambda)` is the condition number of a simple eigenvalue. It is subscripted so it never collides with κ(A) = ‖A‖‖A⁻¹‖.

## 01-gershgorin.md — Gershgorin discs
- `def-gershgorin-discs`: r_i(A) = Σ_{j≠i}|a_ij| and D_i(A) = {z ∈ ℂ : |z − a_ii| ≤ r_i(A)}.
- `thm-gershgorin` (**major**): every eigenvalue of A ∈ M_n(ℂ) lies in ∪ D_i(A). Also in ∪ D_i(Aᵀ), the column discs, and hence in the intersection of the two unions.
  - Proof: take an eigenvector, pick the index of its largest modulus coordinate, and use the triangle inequality. The move: *dominant coordinate plus the triangle inequality.*
  - The proof gives more: the eigenvalue lies in the disc of the index where the eigenvector peaks. Record this in the statement or as a remark.
- `cor-diagonally-dominant-invertible`: a strictly (row) diagonally dominant matrix is invertible (Lévy–Desplanques). State and use "strictly diagonally dominant" as a defined term, inside the corollary or in a small def.
- `prp-gershgorin-scaling`: for D = diag(d₁,…,d_n) with d_i > 0, D⁻¹AD has the same eigenvalues and radii Σ_{j≠i}|a_ij|d_j/d_i. Scaling shrinks some discs at the expense of others.
  - Worked example: isolate an eigenvalue in a disc that the unscaled discs could not isolate.
  - Deferred to §03: **the claim that an isolated disc contains exactly one eigenvalue needs the counting theorem.** Here, only say that the union contains all of them.
- `thm-taussky`: if A is **irreducible** (Ch 19 §02) and an eigenvalue λ lies on the boundary of ∪D_i(A), then λ lies on the boundary circle of **every** disc. Corollary: an irreducible matrix that is weakly diagonally dominant, with strict inequality in at least one row, is invertible (`cor-irreducibly-dominant-invertible`).
  - Proof: follow the eigenvector's peak coordinates along the graph edges. Uses `thm-irreducible-iff-strongly-connected`.
  - Example: the second-difference matrix K_n of Ch 19 §09 is invertible by this, since only its first and last rows are strict.
- Real symmetric or Hermitian matrices: the discs meet ℝ in intervals, so this gives a bound on the eigenvalues. Compare it with `cor-spectral-radius-row-column-bound`: Gershgorin implies ρ(A) ≤ max row sum of |A|.
- Warning: a single disc need not contain an eigenvalue ([[0,1],[4,0]]). The union is the claim.

## 02-inclusion-regions.md — Other inclusion regions
- `thm-brauer-ovals`: every eigenvalue lies in ∪_{i≠j} O_ij(A), where O_ij(A) = {z : |z − a_ii||z − a_jj| ≤ r_i r_j}. Needs n ≥ 2.
  - Proof: take the **two** largest coordinates of an eigenvector.
  - Then prove ∪ O_ij ⊆ ∪ D_i, so the ovals are never worse.
  - Example where they are strictly better.
- `def-numerical-range`: W(A) = {x*Ax : ‖x‖₂ = 1}.
- `prp-numerical-range-basic`, with these parts:
  - (a) σ(A) ⊆ W(A);
  - (b) W(αA + βI) = αW(A) + β;
  - (c) W(U*AU) = W(A) for unitary U;
  - (d) W(A) is compact: a continuous image of the unit sphere, (A3), with continuity by the Ch 16 index convention;
  - (e) W(A) ⊆ {|z| ≤ ‖A‖₂}.
- `thm-toeplitz-hausdorff` (**major**): W(A) is convex. Route:
  - Reduce to showing that if 0 and 1 are in W(A) then [0,1] ⊆ W(A), using (b).
  - Write A = H + iK with H and K Hermitian. Let x and y be unit vectors with x*Ax = 0 and y*Ay = 1, so x*Kx = y*Ky = 0. Multiply y by a unimodular scalar so that x*Ky + y*Kx is real, in fact 0 (check this carefully).
  - The path v(t) = (1−t)x + t y is never zero, because x and y are independent. Otherwise x*Ax = y*Ay after the phase change.
  - v(t)*Av(t)/‖v(t)‖² is real and continuous. It takes the value 0 at t = 0 and 1 at t = 1, so it takes every value in [0,1] by the IVT, (A5).
  - **The drafter must verify every step,** in particular the claim that the imaginary part vanishes along the whole path.
- `prp-numerical-range-normal`: for A normal, W(A) = conv σ(A). Proof: the spectral theorem and x*Ax = Σ|c_i|²λ_i.
  - For non-normal A, W(A) can be larger. Example: W(J₂(0)) is the disc of radius 1/2. Prove it, or at least prove the containment of the boundary circle.
- `thm-bendixson`: every eigenvalue λ of A satisfies λ_n(H) ≤ Re λ ≤ λ₁(H), where H = (A + A*)/2, and likewise Im λ lies between the extreme eigenvalues of (A − A*)/2i.
  - Proof: Re(x*Ax) = x*Hx for a unit eigenvector, then `prp-rayleigh-basic` or the Ch 17 §01 bounds.
- Warning: W(A) ⊇ σ(A) can be strict and far larger, so W is **not** a perturbation bound for non-normal A. But W(A + E) ⊆ W(A) + W(E) ⊆ W(A) + {|z| ≤ ‖E‖₂} is.

## 03-continuity-of-roots.md — Continuity of roots
- **Open with Chapter 16's quoted theorem, then prove it.**
- `lem-cauchy-root-bound`: every root z of a monic x^n + c_{n−1}x^{n−1} + … + c_0 satisfies |z| ≤ 1 + max_j|c_j|. Prove it: if |z| > 1, then |z|^n ≤ M(|z|^{n−1} + … + 1) < M|z|^n/(|z| − 1).
- `thm-roots-continuous` (**major**) is the statement of `thm-roots-depend-continuously`, **restated in full and proved**.
  - Route, by contradiction:
    1. Suppose the conclusion fails for some ε, with q_k → p coefficientwise.
    2. By Cauchy's bound, the root vectors of q_k are bounded in ℂⁿ.
    3. By (A3) a subsequence converges to some ν ∈ ℂⁿ.
    4. The coefficients of ∏(x − ν_i^{(k)}) are polynomials in the ν^{(k)}, namely ± the elementary symmetric functions. Expanding the product is enough; no named theory is needed. So they converge to the coefficients of ∏(x − ν_i). They also converge to those of p.
    5. So ∏(x − ν_i) = p, and by `thm-unique-factorization-polynomials` (or directly, by counting roots with multiplicity), ν is a rearrangement of μ.
    6. For large k in the subsequence, that rearrangement is a valid numbering within ε. Contradiction.
  - **Afterwards, state plainly:**
    - Chapter 16 quoted this theorem and said its proof needed complex analysis.
    - That was too pessimistic: compactness suffices.
    - `cor-eigenvalues-continuous`, and everything Chapter 16 built on it, now rests on a proved theorem.
    - Use one sentence, measured, with no drama.
- `thm-elsner-spectral-variation` (**major**): for A, B ∈ M_n(ℂ) and every eigenvalue μ of B,

  min_i |λ_i(A) − μ| ≤ (‖A‖₂ + ‖B‖₂)^{1 − 1/n} ‖A − B‖₂^{1/n}.

  - Route:
    1. Take a unit eigenvector x of B for μ and extend it to an orthonormal basis, giving a unitary U with first column x.
    2. Then ∏|λ_i − μ| = |det(A − μI)| = |det((A − μI)U)| ≤ ∏_j ‖(A − μI)u_j‖, by `thm-hadamard-inequality` (b).
    3. The first factor is ‖(A − B)x‖ ≤ ‖A − B‖₂. The others are ≤ ‖A‖₂ + |μ| ≤ ‖A‖₂ + ‖B‖₂.
    4. The left side is ≥ (min_i|λ_i − μ|)^n.
  - This gives a **Hölder-1/n modulus of continuity with no hypothesis on A**.
  - The matching version, with a constant factor such as (2n − 1), may be **stated with credit and flagged as not proved**. Nothing may depend on it. Alternatively, prove it if the proof via the counting argument below is clean.
- `exm-jordan-root-perturbation`: J_n(0) + εe_ne_1ᵀ has characteristic polynomial xⁿ − ε, so the eigenvalues are the n n-th roots of ε, of modulus ε^{1/n}. The exponent in Elsner's bound therefore cannot be improved.
  - **This pays Ch 10 §03's "Chapter 20 returns to this point".** Say so: a perturbation of size 10⁻¹⁶ moves the eigenvalues of J₁₆(0) by 10⁻¹. This is the numerical reason the Jordan form is not computed.
- `thm-gershgorin-counting` (**major**): if the union of k of the Gershgorin discs is disjoint from the union of the other n − k, it contains exactly k eigenvalues, counted with algebraic multiplicity.
  - Route:
    1. Take A(t) = D + tF, with D = diag(a_ii) and F the off-diagonal part, for t ∈ [0,1].
    2. Its discs have the same centers and radii t·r_i, so they sit inside the t = 1 discs.
    3. Let U and V be the two disjoint compact unions, at distance d > 0. The count N(t) of eigenvalues in U is locally constant by `cor-eigenvalues-continuous`, taking ε < d/2 and noting that all eigenvalues lie in U ∪ V.
    4. A locally constant integer function on [0,1] is constant. Prove this with (A1): take the sup of the set where N = N(0). Or use (A5).
    5. N(0) = k.
  - Corollary: an isolated disc contains exactly one eigenvalue, and **for a real matrix that eigenvalue is real** when the disc is centered on ℝ and isolated, since non-real eigenvalues come in conjugate pairs. Tie this back to §01's scaling example.

## 04-bauer-fike.md — The Bauer–Fike theorem
- `lem-perturbed-eigenvalue-resolvent`: if μ ∈ σ(A + E) ∖ σ(A), then 1 ≤ ‖(A − μI)⁻¹E‖ ≤ ‖(A − μI)⁻¹‖‖E‖, for any operator norm.
  - Proof: factor A + E − μI = (A − μI)(I + (A − μI)⁻¹E) and use `thm-neumann-series`.
  - **Credit `exr-continuity-of-eigenvalues-c1` for the idea; the proof here is the one of record.**
- `thm-bauer-fike` (**major**): A = XΛX⁻¹ diagonalizable, E arbitrary. Every eigenvalue μ of A + E satisfies min_i|λ_i − μ| ≤ κ_p(X)‖E‖_p, for p = 1, 2, ∞, or for any operator norm in which the norm of a diagonal matrix is the largest modulus of its entries. State the p-norm version; κ₂ is the headline.
  - Proof: apply the lemma to Λ and X⁻¹EX.
- `cor-bauer-fike-normal`: for normal A, the bound is min|λ_i − μ| ≤ ‖E‖₂.
  - Reuse Ch 17 §03's example: A = 0, E the rotation, eigenvalues ±i.
  - Also prove the "exactly" of Ch 12 §11: inf over diagonalizing X of κ₂(X) equals 1 iff A is normal.
- `thm-residual-bound` (a posteriori, `thm-residual-bound`): if ‖Ax̂ − μx̂‖₂ ≤ η‖x̂‖₂ with x̂ ≠ 0, then some eigenvalue λ of A has |λ − μ| ≤ κ₂(X)η. For normal A, |λ − μ| ≤ η.
  - Proof: μ is an eigenvalue of A + E with E = −r x̂*/‖x̂‖², and ‖E‖₂ = ‖r‖/‖x̂‖.
  - Aftermath: a computed eigenpair with small residual is an exact eigenpair of a nearby matrix. This is the germ of backward error, and Chapter 24 builds on it (pointer only).
- `thm-bauer-fike-defective`: with the Jordan form A = XJX⁻¹, where m is the size of the largest Jordan block, every eigenvalue μ of A + E satisfies min_i|λ_i − μ| ≤ max(θ, θ^{1/m}) with θ = m·κ₂(X)‖E‖₂.
  - **The drafter fixes and verifies the exact constant.**
  - Route: ‖(J_m(λ) − μI)⁻¹‖₂ ≤ Σ_{k=1}^{m} δ^{−k} with δ = |λ − μ|. The inverse is the finite Neumann series of the nilpotent part, and the norm of that part is ≤ 1. Then case-split on δ ≥ 1 or δ < 1.
  - **Pays Ch 10's promise:** the m-th root is the Jordan block's signature, and §03's example shows it is sharp.
- Worked example: a diagonalizable 2×2 with a badly conditioned eigenvector matrix, for instance [[1, M],[0, 2]]. Compute κ₂(X) and the actual eigenvalue movement under a corner perturbation, exactly. Compare with Ch 12 §11's `exm-eigenvalue-sensitivity`, cited.
- Warning: κ₂(X) depends on the choice of X, since eigenvectors can be rescaled. The theorem holds for every choice, so one takes the best. A matrix can have a well-conditioned eigenvalue inside a badly conditioned X; §11 refines this per eigenvalue.

## 05-hoffman-wielandt.md — The Hoffman–Wielandt theorem
- Hook: Bauer–Fike says each perturbed eigenvalue is near **some** eigenvalue. It does not say the eigenvalues can be **paired up**; two could crowd near one. Give an example with n = 2 where a one-sided bound holds but the nearest-eigenvalue map is not a bijection.
- `thm-hoffman-wielandt` (**major**): A and B normal, with eigenvalues λ_i and μ_i. There is a permutation σ with Σ_i|λ_i − μ_{σ(i)}|² ≤ ‖A − B‖_F².
  - Route:
    1. A = UΛU* and B = VMV*, with W = U*V.
    2. ‖A − B‖_F² = ‖ΛW − WM‖_F² = Σ_{i,j}|w_ij|²|λ_i − μ_j|², by `lem-frobenius-unitarily-invariant`.
    3. S = (|w_ij|²) is doubly stochastic.
    4. S ↦ Σ s_ij c_ij is linear on Ω_n, which is compact and convex (it is closed and bounded, `cor-closed-bounded-compact`). So its minimum is attained at an extreme point (`cor-linear-max-at-extreme`), which is a permutation matrix (`thm-birkhoff`).
  - **The payoff of Ch 19 §07.**
- `lem-sorted-pairing`: for real a₁ ≥ … ≥ a_n and b₁ ≥ … ≥ b_n, the identity pairing minimizes Σ(a_i − b_{σ(i)})² over σ ∈ S_n. Proof: an exchange argument. If i < j and σ(i) > σ(j), swapping does not increase the sum.
- `cor-hoffman-wielandt-hermitian`: A and B Hermitian give Σ(λ_i(A) − λ_i(B))² ≤ ‖A − B‖_F², in sorted order.
  - Compare with Weyl (`cor-weyl-perturbation`), which is the ∞-norm version, and remark that Lidskii (`thm-lidskii-inequality`) contains both. Chapter 21 turns that into a statement for every unitarily invariant norm (pointer).
- Negative result: a non-normal pair for which Σ|λ_i − μ_σ(i)|² > ‖A − B‖_F² for **every** σ. Use a Jordan-block pair, and **compute it exactly**.
- Real-world phrasing: "the eigenvalues of a normal matrix, as a multiset, are a 1-Lipschitz function of the matrix in the Frobenius norm and the matching distance."

## 06-hermitian-perturbations.md — Hermitian perturbations
- Recall Weyl (`cor-weyl-perturbation`) in one paragraph; cite it, don't reprove it.
- `thm-hermitian-residual-bound`: A Hermitian, x unit, μ ∈ ℝ, r = Ax − μx. Then some eigenvalue lies in [μ − ‖r‖, μ + ‖r‖]. This is the normal case of §04's `thm-residual-bound` (cite it), or a direct proof via the spectral theorem. Choose one and say which.
- `thm-kato-temple` (**major**): the quadratic residual bound.
  - Setting: A Hermitian, x unit, μ = x*Ax the Rayleigh quotient, ε = ‖Ax − μx‖₂.
  - Statement: if λ is an eigenvalue and there is no other eigenvalue in (λ, β) with μ < β, then λ ≥ μ − ε²/(β − μ). The symmetric statement holds with α < μ.
  - **The drafter fixes the exact hypotheses and verifies them.** Route: x*(A − λI)(A − βI)x ≥ 0 because (t − λ)(t − β) ≥ 0 at every eigenvalue t, and x*(A − λ)(A − β)x = ε² + (μ − λ)(μ − β).
  - Aftermath: the Rayleigh quotient's error is **quadratic** in the residual when there is a gap. This is why the Rayleigh quotient is the right eigenvalue estimate; Ch 17 §01 and Ch 24 point to it.
- `thm-ostrowski-relative` (**major**): A Hermitian and S invertible. Then λ_k(SAS*) = θ_k λ_k(A) with θ_k ∈ [λ_n(SS*), λ₁(SS*)].
  - Route:
    1. Put μ = λ_k(A). S(A − μI)S* is congruent to A − μI, so by `thm-sylvester-inertia` it has at most k − 1 positive eigenvalues and at least k non-negative ones. Hence λ_k(S(A − μI)S*) = 0.
    2. SAS* = S(A − μI)S* + μSS*.
    3. By `cor-weyl-monotone` / Weyl, λ_k(SAS*) lies between μλ_n(SS*) and μλ₁(SS*), with the order depending on the sign of μ.
  - Consequence: congruence changes each eigenvalue by a **relative** factor. This is a quantitative Sylvester's law of inertia, and says so.
- Example, verified: a graded diagonal matrix, where absolute bounds (Weyl) are useless for the tiny eigenvalues but relative bounds are sharp.
- Warning: Weyl is sharp for absolute error, and useless for eigenvalues much smaller than ‖E‖.

## 07-sylvester-bounds.md — The equation AX − XB = Y
- Pays Ch 8 §04 and Ch 12 §10, in their words.
- `def-sep`: sep_F(A,B) = min_{‖X‖_F = 1}‖AX − XB‖_F.
  - Well-defined: a minimum of a continuous function on the compact unit sphere of M_{m×n}, (A3)/(A4). Or, better, it equals σ_min of the Kronecker-sum matrix K = I ⊗ A − Bᵀ ⊗ I. Make this `prp-sep-properties` (a).
- `prp-sep-properties`:
  - (a) sep_F(A,B) = σ_min(K), where the vec identity is Ch 8 §04's and Ch 12 §10's; cite it.
  - (b) sep_F(A,B) > 0 iff σ(A) ∩ σ(B) = ∅ (`thm-sylvester-equation`).
  - (c) sep_F(A,B) ≤ min|λ_i − μ_j|: take X = x y*, with x a right eigenvector of A and y a left eigenvector of B.
  - (d) The unique solution satisfies ‖X‖_F ≤ ‖Y‖_F / sep_F(A,B). **This is the quantitative form, and says so.**
- `thm-sep-normal`: for A and B normal, sep_F(A,B) = min_{i,j}|λ_i − μ_j|.
  - Route: unitary diagonalization, then entrywise x̃_ij(λ_i − μ_j) = ỹ_ij, then `lem-frobenius-unitarily-invariant`.
- Non-normal warning, with an example: sep can be far below the eigenvalue gap. Use A = [[0, M],[0, 0]] + small shift and B = scalar, or similar, and **compute it exactly**.
- `thm-sylvester-one-sided` (**major**): A ∈ M_m and B ∈ M_n Hermitian, with A ⪰ αI, B ⪯ βI and α > β. The unique solution of AX − XB = Y satisfies ‖X‖₂ ≤ ‖Y‖₂/(α − β).
  - Route: the **singular-pair trick**.
    1. Let s = ‖X‖₂ with unit u and v such that Xv = su and X*u = sv (`thm-svd`).
    2. Then u*Yv = u*AXv − u*XBv = s·u*Au − s·v*Bv ≥ s(α − β).
    3. |u*Yv| ≤ ‖Y‖₂.
  - The same argument works in ‖·‖_F. Note that the Frobenius version also follows from the normal case, since the spectra are separated by α − β.
- `cor-positive-sylvester`: S ⪰ αI and T ⪰ βI Hermitian with α + β > 0. The solution of SX + XT = Y satisfies ‖X‖ ≤ ‖Y‖/(α + β), in both the spectral and the Frobenius norm. Apply the theorem to (S, −T). §10 uses this corollary twice.

## 08-principal-angles.md — Pairs of subspaces and principal angles
- Hook: how far apart are two k-dimensional subspaces? A single angle fails when k ≥ 2; give the example.
- `def-principal-angles`: U, W ⊆ ℂⁿ with dim U = k ≤ dim W = l, and Q_U, Q_W with orthonormal columns. cos θ_i = σ_i(Q_U*Q_W), for i = 1, …, k and 0 ≤ θ₁ ≤ … ≤ θ_k ≤ π/2.
- `prp-principal-angles-well-defined`: the angles do not depend on the choice of orthonormal bases (Q_U ↦ Q_U R with R unitary). σ_i ≤ 1 by `thm-operator-norm-formulas`. For k = l = 1 this is the angle between lines, with |⟨u,w⟩|, not Ch 11's `def-angle`. **Warning: lines have no orientation, so the absolute value, and the angles lie in [0, π/2].**
- `thm-sines-of-principal-angles` (**major**): with Q_{W⊥} an orthonormal basis of W^⊥, the singular values of Q_{W⊥}*Q_U are the sines sin θ_i, as a multiset with the appropriate zeros.
  - Route: I = Q_U*Q_U = (Q_W*Q_U)*(Q_W*Q_U) + (Q_{W⊥}*Q_U)*(Q_{W⊥}*Q_U). Two Hermitian matrices summing to I share eigenvectors, and their eigenvalues are c² and 1 − c².
  - Consequences: ‖(I − P_W)P_U‖₂ = sin θ_k, and ‖(I − P_W)Q_U‖_F = ‖sin Θ‖_F.
- `thm-projection-difference-norm`: for dim U = dim W = k, ‖P_U − P_W‖₂ = sin θ_k (the largest angle), and ‖P_U − P_W‖_F = √2‖sin Θ‖_F.
  - **Verify both. Prove the spectral one completely.** A route: (P_U − P_W)² commutes with P_U and with P_W, or use the orthonormal basis adapted to both subspaces from the SVD of Q_U*Q_W. If the full two-subspace canonical form is used, prove it.
  - Also: ‖P_U − P_W‖₂ ≤ 1 always, with equality iff U ∩ W^⊥ ≠ {0} (for equal dimensions).
- `def-subspace-distance` (optional): d(U,W) = ‖P_U − P_W‖₂, a metric on k-dimensional subspaces. Check the triangle inequality, which is immediate from the norm.
- Worked example: two planes in ℝ⁴ with angles computed exactly, for instance angles π/6 and π/4.
- Chapter 22's Grassmannians are **not** to be promised; the plan does not list them.

## 09-davis-kahan.md — The Davis–Kahan sin Θ theorem
- Setting: A and Ã = A + E Hermitian n×n.
  - A = Q₁Λ₁Q₁* + Q₂Λ₂Q₂*, where Q₁ (n×k) spans an invariant subspace.
  - Ã = Q̃₁Λ̃₁Q̃₁* + Q̃₂Λ̃₂Q̃₂*.
  - The key identity: Λ̃₂(Q̃₂*Q₁) − (Q̃₂*Q₁)Λ₁ = Q̃₂*EQ₁, from ÃQ₁ = Q₁Λ₁ + EQ₁. **This is a Sylvester equation. The move.**
- `thm-davis-kahan-frobenius` (**major**): if every eigenvalue of Λ̃₂ differs from every eigenvalue of Λ₁ by at least δ > 0, then ‖sin Θ(R(Q₁), R(Q̃₁))‖_F ≤ ‖EQ₁‖_F/δ ≤ √k·‖E‖₂/δ.
  - Uses `thm-sep-normal` and `thm-sines-of-principal-angles`, with W = R(Q̃₁), so W^⊥ = R(Q̃₂).
- `thm-davis-kahan-one-sided`: if the eigenvalues of Λ₁ lie in [a, ∞) and those of Λ̃₂ in (−∞, a − δ], or the mirror case, then ‖sin Θ‖₂ ≤ ‖EQ₁‖₂/δ. Uses `thm-sylvester-one-sided`.
  - Remark: the two-sided interval case, with Λ₁ ⊂ [a,b] and Λ̃₂ outside (a − δ, b + δ), also holds in ‖·‖₂ with the same constant (Davis–Kahan). **State it as not proved here**, or prove it if the drafter has a clean argument. Nothing may depend on it unless proved.
- `cor-eigenvector-perturbation`: for a simple eigenvalue λ_i of A with unit eigenvector x, let gap = min_{j≠i}|λ_i − λ_j| and suppose ‖E‖₂ < gap/2. Then the unit eigenvector x̃ of Ã for λ_i(Ã) satisfies sin∠(x, x̃) ≤ ‖E‖₂/(gap − ‖E‖₂) ≤ 2‖E‖₂/gap.
  - Route: δ ≥ gap − ‖E‖₂ by Weyl, `cor-weyl-perturbation`. **Verify the constant.**
- Negative example, exact: A = diag(1 + η, 1 − η) with E = [[−η, η],[η, η]] or similar. A tiny η-sized perturbation rotates the eigenvectors by π/4. When the gap is small, eigenvectors are ill-conditioned **even though the eigenvalues are perfectly conditioned** (Weyl). Contrast with §06.
- Aftermath: invariant **subspaces** of a cluster of eigenvalues are stable, even when the individual eigenvectors inside the cluster are not. Illustrate with k = 2.

## 10-singular-values-and-polar.md — Singular values and polar factors
- Recall `cor-singular-value-perturbation`, the spectral-norm version, from Ch 17 §09, in one paragraph.
- `thm-mirsky-frobenius` (**major**): Σ_i (σ_i(A) − σ_i(B))² ≤ ‖A − B‖_F² for A, B ∈ M_{m×n}.
  - Route: `prp-hermitian-dilation` plus `cor-hoffman-wielandt-hermitian`. 𝓗(A) − 𝓗(B) = 𝓗(A − B) and ‖𝓗(M)‖_F² = 2‖M‖_F². The sorted eigenvalues of 𝓗(A) are σ₁ ≥ … ≥ σ_p ≥ 0 … ≥ −σ₁. **Check the bookkeeping of the zeros and the factor 2 exactly.**
  - This pays Ch 17 §09's "Chapter 20 leans on it for perturbation bounds".
- `thm-sqrt-perturbation`: P, Q ≻ 0 Hermitian. Then ‖P^{1/2} − Q^{1/2}‖ ≤ ‖P − Q‖/(λ_n(P)^{1/2} + λ_n(Q)^{1/2}) in ‖·‖₂ and ‖·‖_F.
  - Route: with S = P^{1/2} and T = Q^{1/2}, P − Q = S(S − T) + (S − T)T. Then `cor-positive-sylvester`.
- `thm-polar-positive-perturbation`: A, B ∈ M_n(ℂ) invertible. ‖|A| − |B|‖ ≤ (‖A‖₂ + ‖B‖₂)‖A − B‖/(σ_n(A) + σ_n(B)).
  - Route: |A| = (A*A)^{1/2}, then A*A − B*B = A*(A − B) + (A − B)*B, then the previous theorem. **Check which norms.**
- `thm-polar-unitary-perturbation` (**major**): A = UP and B = VQ invertible, the polar decompositions. ‖U − V‖ ≤ 2‖A − B‖/(σ_n(A) + σ_n(B)), in ‖·‖₂ and ‖·‖_F.
  - Route: with W = V*U, (W − I)P + Q(W − I) = V*(A − B) − (A − B)*U, whose norm is ≤ 2‖A − B‖. Then `cor-positive-sylvester` with S = Q and T = P, since λ_n(P) = σ_n(A). **Verify the identity** (the coordinator checked it: V*(A − B) = WP − Q and (A − B)*U = P − QW).
- Warning: for singular A the unitary factor is not unique, so no such bound can hold. Show a 2×2 family A_ε → A whose unitary factors do not converge.
- Aftermath: `cor-unitary-nearest` from Ch 13 §09 says U is the unitary nearest to A. This section says the nearest unitary moves continuously, with a rate, when A is invertible.

## 11-eigenvalue-condition.md — Condition numbers of eigenvalues
- `lem-simple-eigenvalue-left-right`: for a simple eigenvalue λ, with right x and left y (y*A = λy*), y*x ≠ 0.
  - Route: `cor-block-diagonalization` (c) with Schur, or the Jordan form. Choose P with P⁻¹AP = λ ⊕ B, λ ∉ σ(B). Then x ∝ Pe₁ and y* ∝ e₁*P⁻¹.
  - Warning: for a double eigenvalue with one eigenvector (J₂), y*x = 0.
- `thm-simple-eigenvalue-derivative` (**major**): λ simple for A. For all small real t, A + tE has exactly one eigenvalue λ(t) near λ (from `cor-eigenvalues-continuous` plus the counting of §03, or directly), and

  λ(t) = λ + t·(y*Ex)/(y*x) + o(t), that is, λ′(0) = y*Ex/(y*x).

  - Route:
    1. An eigenvector x_t = adj(λ(t)I − A − tE)e_k, for a column k with adj(λI − A)e_k ≠ 0. The adjugate is non-zero because rank(λI − A) = n − 1 (`thm-rank-via-minors`); its columns lie in ker(λI − A) by `thm-adjugate-identity`.
    2. x_t → x_0 by continuity: the entries are polynomials in t and λ(t).
    3. y*(A + tE)x_t = λ(t)y*x_t gives (λ(t) − λ)y*x_t = t·y*Ex_t.
    4. Divide by t and let t → 0.
  - **No implicit function theorem is needed. Say so.**
- `def-eigenvalue-condition-number`: κ_A(λ) = ‖x‖₂‖y‖₂/|y*x|, for simple λ.
- `prp-eigenvalue-condition-properties`:
  - (a) it is independent of the scaling of x and y, and ≥ 1;
  - (b) |λ′(0)| ≤ κ_A(λ)‖E‖₂, with equality for E = yx*/(‖x‖‖y‖);
  - (c) it equals 1 for every eigenvalue of a normal A;
  - (d) for diagonalizable A = XΛX⁻¹, κ_A(λ_i) ≤ κ₂(X), which links it to §04;
  - (e) it equals 1/cos∠(x, y).
- Example: [[1, M],[0, 2]] gives κ = √(1 + M²) for both eigenvalues. Verify, and compare with §04's κ₂(X).
- Remark: as two eigenvalues coalesce into a Jordan block, y*x → 0 and κ → ∞. This is the first-order shadow of the ε^{1/n} of §03, and it completes the Ch 10 thread.
- **Closing "Summary and transfer"** for the chapter. Named moves:
  - dominant coordinate plus the triangle inequality;
  - a determinant bounded two ways (Hadamard);
  - compactness plus uniqueness (continuity of roots);
  - an integer-valued continuous function is constant (counting);
  - factor out the resolvent (Bauer–Fike);
  - Birkhoff turns a doubly stochastic weight into a permutation;
  - the perturbation of a subspace is a Sylvester equation;
  - the singular-pair trick;
  - the adjugate as a polynomial eigenvector.
