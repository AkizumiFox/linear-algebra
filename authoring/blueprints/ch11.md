# Blueprint: Chapter 11 — Spectral Theory

**Directory:** `src/ch11-spectral-theory/` (new). **Chapter title:** "Spectral Theory". **Part:** continues "Part III · Inner Products and Spectral Theory" (no `part` key; Chapter 10 opens the part).

**Purpose.** Chapter 8 asked when an operator is diagonalizable and got an answer in terms of multiplicities; Chapter 9 built canonical forms for the operators that are not. Neither used geometry. With an inner product available, the question sharpens: when is there an **orthonormal** basis of eigenvectors? The answer is clean and complete — exactly the normal operators over ℂ, exactly the self-adjoint operators over ℝ — and it is the most used theorem in applied linear algebra. The chapter builds

- Schur's unitary triangularization, the workhorse behind everything else;
- self-adjoint operators: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenspaces;
- normal operators and their many characterizations;
- the complex and real spectral theorems;
- the canonical form of an orthogonal matrix, and rotations of ℝ³;
- commuting normal families, diagonalized together;
- spectral resolutions, orthogonal projections and the continuous functional calculus for normal operators;
- circulants and the discrete Fourier transform;
- the Sylvester equation and block diagonalization;
- how far from normal a matrix is, measured by Schur's inequality.

**Allowed prerequisites.** Chapters 0–10. Chapter 9 is used lightly (only for the remark that a normal operator has no Jordan blocks of size > 1, and in §10). The field is **ℝ or ℂ**, as in Chapter 10; say which one each theorem needs and why. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (Ch 5) is what makes the complex theory work, and the chapter should say so out loud at least twice.

**Signature moves:**
- **One eigenvector, then its orthogonal complement.** The complement is invariant as soon as the operator is normal or self-adjoint; induct on dimension. This replaces Chapter 8's "extend a basis and pass to the quotient".
- **Compute \( \inner{T\v}{\v} \) two ways.**
- **Work with \( \norm{T\v}^2 = \inner{T^{*}T\v}{\v} \).**
- **Triangular plus normal forces diagonal**, entry by entry: the cheapest route to the spectral theorem once Schur is available.
- **Split into real and imaginary parts:** \( T = A + iB \) with A, B self-adjoint; T is normal exactly when A and B commute.
- **Diagonalize, then read the answer off the diagonal** (powers, square roots, the functional calculus).

## 01-schur-triangularization.md — Schur's unitary triangularization

- **Hook:** Chapter 8 triangularized over ℂ in *some* basis. With an inner product we can ask for an **orthonormal** one — and get it, for free, by running the same induction inside \( (\Span \v)^{\perp} \).
- **Theorem** `thm-schur-triangularization` (**major**): every \( T \in \cL(V) \) on a finite-dimensional complex inner product space has an orthonormal basis in which its matrix is upper triangular. Matrix form `cor-schur-matrix`: every \( \A \in M_n(\nC) \) is \( \A = \U\T\U^{*} \) with \( \U \) unitary and \( \T \) upper triangular; the diagonal of \( \T \) is the eigenvalue list of \( \A \) with multiplicity, in any prescribed order (say why the order is free).
  - **Idea:** take one eigenvector of \( T^{*} \) (not of T — explain the swap, or equivalently build the basis from the top down), normalize, and recurse on the orthogonal complement.
  - **Proof:** induction on \( \dim V \). Existence of an eigenvalue of \( T^{*} \) is the FTA (cite Ch 8). Set \( U = (\Span\e_1)^{\perp} \); show \( U \) is invariant under T when \( \e_1 \) is an eigenvector of \( T^{*} \); apply the inductive hypothesis to \( T|_U \); check the matrix is triangular.
- **First consequences:** `cor-trace-sum-eigenvalues-again` and `cor-det-product-eigenvalues-again` in one line (recall the Ch 8 versions rather than reproving); `cor-schur-normal-matrix-nearby`: every square complex matrix is a limit of diagonalizable ones (perturb the diagonal of \( \T \)) — a short, memorable payoff, and the tool behind "prove it for diagonalizable matrices, then take a limit" (forward pointer to Ch 15 and to the Cayley–Hamilton reproof in the exercises).
- **Real Schur form** `thm-real-schur`: every \( \A \in M_n(\nR) \) is \( \Q\T\Q\tp \) with \( \Q \) orthogonal and \( \T \) **block** upper triangular with 1×1 and 2×2 diagonal blocks, the 2×2 blocks carrying conjugate pairs. Proof: take a complex eigenvector of the adjoint and span its real and imaginary parts, giving a real invariant subspace of dimension 1 or 2, then induct. **Ch 9 §04 has no such result** — the statement lives in Ch 8 §07 as `exr-triangularization-c2` (c). Since a theorem should not rest on an exercise, write the three-line argument inline and cite the exercise only as a pointer.
- **Warning:** \( \T \) is not unique, and neither is \( \U \); only the diagonal multiset is. A triangular matrix is *not* the operator's "canonical form" in any useful sense — Jordan (Ch 9) is, but Jordan cannot be reached unitarily.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** Schur-triangularize a 2×2 and a 3×3 by hand; read off eigenvalues; show a unitarily triangularizable real matrix with non-real eigenvalues cannot be real-triangularized.
  - **C:** Cayley–Hamilton over ℂ from Schur (multiply the triangular factors); \( \norm{\A}_F \) is unchanged by \( \A \mapsto \U\A\V \) with U, V unitary; every complex matrix is a limit of matrices with n distinct eigenvalues.

## 02-self-adjoint-operators.md — Self-adjoint operators

- **Hook:** the matrices that behave like real numbers. \( T = T^{*} \) is the operator analogue of \( \conj z = z \).
- **Recall** `def-self-adjoint` from Ch 10 §06 (do not redefine; restate in one line and cite).
- **Theorem** `thm-self-adjoint-real-eigenvalues`: every eigenvalue of a self-adjoint operator is real, over ℝ **and** over ℂ. Proof: \( \lambda\norm{\v}^2 = \inner{T\v}{\v} = \inner{\v}{T\v} = \conj\lambda\norm{\v}^2 \).
- **Theorem** `thm-self-adjoint-has-eigenvalue`: a self-adjoint operator on a non-zero **real** inner product space has an eigenvalue. Two routes — give the complexification route (complexify, get a complex eigenvalue, it is real by the previous theorem, so the real operator \( T - \lambda I \) is singular) and mention the maximize-\( \inner{T\v}{\v} \)-on-the-sphere route as a remark (analysis, so not used). Chapter 1 introduced complexification; cite its label.
- **Theorem** `thm-self-adjoint-orthogonal-eigenspaces`: eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. Proof: pair and compare.
- **Theorem** `thm-self-adjoint-invariant-complement`: if U is invariant under a self-adjoint T then so is \( U^{\perp} \), and both restrictions are self-adjoint. (This is the induction step §04 needs; prove it here.)
- **Zero test** `thm-self-adjoint-zero-test`: for self-adjoint T, \( \inner{T\v}{\v} = 0 \) for all v implies \( T = 0 \). Over ℂ the hypothesis "self-adjoint" can be dropped — `thm-complex-zero-test` — and over ℝ it cannot: rotation by 90° is the counterexample. This pair is the cleanest place in the book to show that ℂ is genuinely easier than ℝ.
- **Warning:** "symmetric matrix" means \( \A\tp = \A \) and is the real case; over ℂ symmetric is **not** the right notion, Hermitian is. Give the 2×2 complex symmetric matrix with a non-real eigenvalue.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** decide self-adjointness for listed operators (matrices, differentiation with boundary conditions, the projection \( P_U \)); compute eigenvalues of small Hermitian matrices and check orthogonality of the eigenvectors.
  - **C:** \( T^{*}T \) is self-adjoint with non-negative \( \inner{T^{*}T\v}{\v} \) for any T (a forward pointer to Ch 12); the sum of self-adjoint operators is self-adjoint but the product is only when they commute; over ℝ, \( \inner{T\v}{\v} = 0 \) for all v means T is skew-adjoint.

## 03-normal-operators.md — Normal operators

- **Hook:** self-adjointness is too strong — a unitary map has an orthonormal eigenbasis over ℂ but is far from self-adjoint. What do both have in common? They commute with their adjoints.
- **Definition** `def-normal-operator` recalled from Ch 10 §06; **major** here.
- **Theorem** `thm-normal-characterizations`: for \( T \in \cL(V) \), TFAE — (a) T is normal; (b) \( \norm{T\v} = \norm{T^{*}\v} \) for every v; (c) the self-adjoint parts \( A = \tfrac12(T + T^{*}) \), \( B = \tfrac1{2i}(T - T^{*}) \) commute (complex case); (d) \( \inner{T\u}{T\v} = \inner{T^{*}\u}{T^{*}\v} \) for all u, v. Proof through the zero test of §02 applied to \( T^{*}T - TT^{*} \).
- **Consequences:** `cor-normal-kernel`: \( \ker T = \ker T^{*} \), hence \( \im T = \im T^{*} \) and \( V = \ker T \oplus \im T \) **orthogonally** (contrast with a general operator, where the sum need not even be direct — give the nilpotent 2×2 example); `thm-normal-eigenvector-shared`: \( T\v = \lambda\v \) iff \( T^{*}\v = \conj\lambda\v \); `cor-normal-orthogonal-eigenspaces`: eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal (the self-adjoint proof, now with conjugates); `thm-normal-powers`: \( \ker T^{2} = \ker T \), so a normal nilpotent operator is zero — this is the sentence that explains why normal operators have no Jordan blocks (cite Ch 9).
- **Examples:** self-adjoint, skew-adjoint, unitary and orthogonal operators are all normal; a diagonal matrix; a circulant (forward pointer to §09); a non-normal example, the 2×2 Jordan block, with \( \norm{T\e_1} \neq \norm{T^{*}\e_1} \) computed.
- **Warning:** *(corrected — the original wording here was false, and the drafter caught it)* in finite dimensions an invariant subspace of a normal operator **is** reducing and the restriction **is** normal: writing \( T = \begin{psmallmatrix} A & B \\ 0 & C\end{psmallmatrix} \) in an adapted orthonormal basis, normality gives \( \tr(A^{*}A) = \tr(AA^{*}) + \tr(BB^{*}) \), so \( \tr(BB^{*}) = 0 \) and \( B = 0 \). The true trap is the **compression to a non-invariant subspace**: the leading \( 2\times2 \) of the \( 3\times3 \) cyclic shift is \( \begin{psmallmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 0\end{psmallmatrix} \), which is not normal. State the true invariant-subspace fact as an exercise.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** decide normality for a list of matrices; verify \( \ker T = \ker T^{*} \) in an example; show an upper triangular normal matrix is diagonal (2×2 by hand, the general case is §04's proof).
  - **C:** a normal operator with real eigenvalues is self-adjoint; a normal operator with \( |\lambda| = 1 \) for every eigenvalue is unitary (both over ℂ, both after §04 — place them here as "you will be able to do these after the next section" or move them to §04's exercises; decide when drafting and keep the dependency honest).

## 04-spectral-theorem-complex.md — The complex spectral theorem

- **Theorem** `thm-spectral-complex` (**major**): for a finite-dimensional complex inner product space, T is normal **iff** V has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of T; matrix form `cor-spectral-complex-matrix`: \( \A \in M_n(\nC) \) is normal iff \( \A = \U\D\U^{*} \) with \( \U \) unitary and \( \D \) diagonal (**unitarily diagonalizable**).
  - **Idea:** Schur gives a triangular matrix; normality of a triangular matrix forces the off-diagonal entries to vanish. Show the 2×2 computation first: compare the (1,1) entries of \( \A^{*}\A \) and \( \A\A^{*} \).
  - **Proof:** the entry-by-entry induction on columns, written out in full.
- **Consequences:** `cor-normal-spectral-corollaries` — a normal operator is self-adjoint iff its spectrum is real, unitary iff its spectrum lies on the unit circle, and (with Ch 12 pending) positive iff its spectrum is non-negative; `cor-normal-commuting-poly`: \( T^{*} = p(T) \) for some polynomial p (Lagrange interpolation on the spectrum, Ch 5) — a striking statement worth its own paragraph.
- **Uniqueness discussion:** the eigenvalues and their eigenspaces are unique; the unitary \( \U \) is not (permute columns, or rotate inside an eigenspace). State exactly what is unique.
- **Warning:** "diagonalizable" and "unitarily diagonalizable" are different conditions. \( \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \) is diagonalizable but not normal, so no orthonormal eigenbasis exists; compute both diagonalizations to make the difference visible.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** unitarily diagonalize small normal matrices (a Hermitian 2×2, a unitary 2×2, a circulant 3×3); decide unitary diagonalizability for a list.
  - **C:** the two exercises deferred from §03; a normal T with \( T^{k} = I \) is unitary and diagonalizable; two normal matrices with the same spectrum and multiplicities are unitarily similar.

## 05-spectral-theorem-real.md — The real spectral theorem

- **Theorem** `thm-spectral-real` (**major**): for a finite-dimensional real inner product space, T is self-adjoint **iff** V has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors; matrix form `cor-spectral-real-matrix`: \( \A \in M_n(\nR) \) is symmetric iff \( \A = \Q\D\Q\tp \) with \( \Q \) orthogonal and \( \D \) real diagonal.
  - **Proof:** induction using `thm-self-adjoint-has-eigenvalue` and `thm-self-adjoint-invariant-complement` from §02 — no complexification needed at this step, which is worth pointing out.
  - **Why "normal" is not enough over ℝ:** the rotation matrix is normal with no real eigenvector. This is the promised negative result of the chapter.
- **Real normal operators** `thm-real-normal-form`: a real normal operator has an orthonormal basis in which its matrix is block diagonal with 1×1 blocks and 2×2 blocks \( \vLambda(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix} \), \( b > 0 \) (the Ch 9 §04 rotation-scaling block, reused — cite it). Proof from the real Schur form of §01 plus normality, or by pairing conjugate eigenvectors of the complexification; pick one route and do it completely.
- **Spectral decomposition of a symmetric matrix:** \( \A = \sum \lambda_i \q_i\q_i\tp \), a sum of rank-one orthogonal projections — `cor-symmetric-rank-one-sum`. Worked 3×3 example with integer eigenvalues.
- **Application, the principal axis theorem** `thm-principal-axes`: a real quadratic form \( q(\x) = \x\tp\A\x \) becomes \( \sum \lambda_i y_i^2 \) in the orthonormal eigenbasis; the level set \( q = 1 \) is an ellipse/hyperbola with axes along the eigenvectors. One TikZ picture of a rotated ellipse with its axes. Forward pointer to Chapter 13 (congruence and inertia) — emphasize that this is a **different** normal form from congruence: here \( \Q\tp = \Q^{-1} \), so similarity and congruence coincide.
- **Warning:** eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real, but the *same matrix over ℚ* may have irrational eigenvalues — the theorem needs ℝ. And a real matrix with real eigenvalues need not be symmetric.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** orthogonally diagonalize symmetric 2×2 and 3×3 matrices with integer spectra; identify the conic \( 5x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2 = 1 \); write a symmetric matrix as a sum of rank-one projections.
  - **C:** a symmetric matrix with all eigenvalues equal is a scalar multiple of I; simultaneously orthogonally diagonalize two commuting symmetric matrices (a preview of §07); the real normal form applied to a 2×2 rotation-scaling.

## 06-orthogonal-matrices-and-rotations.md — The shape of an orthogonal matrix

- **Theorem** `thm-orthogonal-canonical-form`: every \( \Q \in \Orth(n) \) is orthogonally similar to a block diagonal matrix with blocks \( [1] \), \( [-1] \) and 2×2 rotation blocks \( \vLambda(\theta) \), \( 0 < \theta < \pi \). Proof from `thm-real-normal-form` (§05) plus \( |\lambda| = 1 \).
- **Corollary** `cor-so3-is-rotation`: every element of \( \SO(3) \) is a rotation about an axis — the axis is the eigenvector for the eigenvalue 1, whose existence comes from \( \det \Q = 1 \) and the eigenvalue structure. Extract the angle from \( \tr\Q = 1 + 2\cos\theta \) — `thm-rotation-angle-trace`. Worked example: a given 3×3 rotation matrix, its axis and its angle, with exact arithmetic.
- **Rodrigues' formula** `thm-rodrigues`: the rotation by θ about a unit axis \( \n \) is \( \I + \sin\theta\, \K + (1 - \cos\theta)\K^2 \) with \( \K \) the cross-product matrix of \( \n \). Prove it by decomposing \( \v \) into its components along and perpendicular to \( \n \) (a TikZ picture); verify \( \K^3 = -\K \).
- **Reflections generate** `thm-cartan-dieudonne-small`: every element of \( \Orth(n) \) is a product of at most n reflections. Prove by induction using the Householder reflection of Ch 10 §08 that sends a vector to another of the same norm (cite the actual label).
- **Composition of rotations:** \( \SO(3) \) is not commutative — two concrete rotations whose products differ, computed. The remark that \( \SO(2) \) is commutative and why.
- **Warning:** \( \det\Q = 1 \) does not mean "rotation" in dimension 4 and above: give the double rotation with no fixed axis.

## 07-commuting-normal-families.md — Commuting families

- **Hook:** Chapter 8 simultaneously diagonalized commuting diagonalizable operators. With an inner product we can ask for one orthonormal basis for all of them at once.
- **Theorem** `thm-simultaneous-unitary-diagonalization`: a family \( \cF \) of **normal** operators on a complex inner product space that commute pairwise has a common orthonormal eigenbasis. Proof: restrict to eigenspaces of one member (they are invariant, since the operators commute — cite the Ch 8 lemma), note the restriction of a normal operator to such an eigenspace is normal, and induct on dimension.
  - Matrix form `cor-simultaneous-unitary-matrix`: a commuting family of normal matrices is simultaneously unitarily diagonalizable; and the real symmetric version `cor-simultaneous-orthogonal-real`.
  - **Warning:** commuting alone is not enough (two non-normal commuting matrices), and normal alone is not enough (two non-commuting normal matrices). Give both examples.
- **Theorem** `thm-commuting-normal-sum-normal`: if S, T are commuting normal operators then \( S + T \) and \( ST \) are normal. Give the one-line proof from the common eigenbasis, and note that it is false without commutativity.
- **Simultaneous triangularization, unitary version** `thm-simultaneous-unitary-triangularization`: any commuting family on a complex inner product space is simultaneously unitarily triangularizable (Schur plus the Ch 8 common-eigenvector lemma). This one needs no normality.

## 08-spectral-resolutions-and-functional-calculus.md — Spectral resolutions and functions of a normal operator

- **Definition** `def-spectral-resolution`: for normal T with distinct eigenvalues \( \lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_k \), \( T = \sum \lambda_i P_i \) with \( P_i \) the **orthogonal** projection onto \( E_{\lambda_i}(T) \); the \( P_i \) are self-adjoint, idempotent, pairwise orthogonal (\( P_iP_j = 0 \)) and sum to I — `thm-spectral-resolution`.
  - Contrast with Chapter 8's projections onto generalized eigenspaces (Ch 9 §08), which are **not** orthogonal in general; give a 2×2 example where they differ.
- **Uniqueness** `thm-spectral-resolution-unique`: a decomposition \( T = \sum \mu_i Q_i \) with distinct \( \mu_i \) and pairwise orthogonal non-zero self-adjoint idempotents summing to I is *the* spectral resolution.
- **Functional calculus** `def-function-of-normal-operator`: \( f(T) \coloneqq \sum f(\lambda_i)P_i \) for any function f on the spectrum; `thm-functional-calculus-properties`: it is an algebra homomorphism, agrees with the polynomial definition of Chapter 5 when f is a polynomial (prove the agreement — this is the compatibility that makes the notation safe), \( f(T)^{*} = \conj f(T) \), and \( f(T) \) is normal, commuting with everything that commutes with T.
- **Square roots:** every normal T with non-negative real spectrum has a unique normal square root with non-negative spectrum — `thm-normal-square-root` (full uniqueness proof; forward pointer to Ch 12 where positivity is the subject). Worked: the square root of a 2×2 symmetric matrix with integer eigenvalues.
- **Exponential and unitary groups:** \( e^{iH} \) is unitary for self-adjoint H, and every unitary is \( e^{iH} \) — `thm-unitary-exponential`. Cite Ch 9 §09 for the matrix exponential rather than redeveloping it.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** write given normal matrices as \( \sum\lambda_iP_i \); compute \( \sqrt{\A} \), \( e^{\A} \), \( \A^{100} \) through the resolution.
  - **C:** the projections \( P_i \) are polynomials in T (Lagrange interpolation); a normal operator whose spectral projections are all rank one has distinct eigenvalues; \( f(T) = 0 \) iff f vanishes on the spectrum.

## 09-circulants-and-the-dft.md — Circulants and the discrete Fourier transform

- **Definition** `def-circulant`: the matrix whose rows are cyclic shifts of \( (c_0, \dots, c_{n-1}) \). Equivalently \( \C = p(\S) \) with \( \S \) the cyclic shift — `thm-circulant-is-polynomial-in-shift`.
- **The shift is unitary and normal**, with eigenvalues the n-th roots of unity and eigenvectors the Fourier vectors \( \f_j = \tfrac1{\sqrt n}(1, \omega^j, \omega^{2j}, \dots) \), \( \omega = e^{2\pi i/n} \) — `thm-shift-eigenvectors`; the Fourier matrix \( \F \) is unitary — `thm-fourier-matrix-unitary` (verify orthonormality with the geometric-series computation; do it in full, it is the heart of the section).
- **Theorem** `thm-circulant-diagonalization`: every circulant is diagonalized by \( \F \), with eigenvalues \( p(\omega^j) \). So all circulants commute, their products and sums are circulant, and the inverse of an invertible circulant is circulant — `cor-circulants-algebra`.
- **Convolution:** multiplying circulants is cyclic convolution of their symbols, and the DFT turns convolution into pointwise multiplication — `thm-convolution-theorem`. Worked 4×4 example throughout, with exact roots of unity.
- **Applications (brief, honest):** solving a circulant system by dividing Fourier coefficients; the FFT is *how* one applies \( \F \) quickly, and belongs to Chapter 23 (forward pointer only).
- **Warning:** a Toeplitz matrix (constant diagonals) is **not** a circulant and is not diagonalized by \( \F \); give a 3×3 example.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** eigenvalues of small circulants; solve a circulant system via the DFT; check the convolution theorem in an example.
  - **C:** a matrix commuting with the cyclic shift is a circulant; the determinant of a circulant as \( \prod p(\omega^j) \); the real orthogonal form of a real circulant (pair conjugate Fourier vectors into sines and cosines).

## 10-sylvester-equation.md — The Sylvester equation and block diagonalization

- **Hook:** Chapter 7 wrote operators as block triangular matrices \( \begin{pmatrix} \A & \B \\ \0 & \C \end{pmatrix} \) and Chapter 9 asked when the corner can be cleared. The answer is a linear equation.
- **Theorem** `thm-sylvester-equation`: for \( \A \in M_m \), \( \B \in M_n \), the map \( \X \mapsto \A\X - \X\B \) on \( M_{m \times n} \) is invertible **iff** \( \spec(\A) \cap \spec(\B) = \varnothing \); in that case \( \A\X - \X\B = \C \) has exactly one solution for every \( \C \).
  - **Proof:** the eigenvalues of the Sylvester operator are \( \lambda_i - \mu_j \). Route: triangularize both (Schur, §01), or use the Kronecker product of Ch 7 §04 — \( \vecop(\A\X - \X\B) = (\I \otimes \A - \B\tp \otimes \I)\vecop\X \) — and the Ch 7 eigenvalue result for Kronecker sums. Use the Kronecker route and cite Ch 7's labels; it is shorter and pays off that chapter.
- **Corollary** `cor-block-diagonalization`: \( \begin{pmatrix} \A & \C \\ \0 & \B \end{pmatrix} \) is similar to \( \A \oplus \B \) iff the Sylvester equation \( \A\X - \X\B = -\C \) is solvable; in particular always, when the spectra are disjoint. The similarity is by \( \begin{pmatrix} \I & \X \\ \0 & \I \end{pmatrix} \) — verify the block multiplication.
- **Consequences:** the primary decomposition of Ch 8 §08 revisited in matrix form; commuting with a matrix of disjoint spectra forces block structure — `cor-commutant-block-diagonal` (state carefully; Chapter 9 §04 has a warning about a false version of this claim, so check that page and stay consistent with it).
- **Lyapunov's equation** as the special case \( \B = -\A^{*} \), with the statement (no proof) that \( \A\X + \X\A^{*} = -\Q \) has a positive definite solution when \( \A \) is stable — forward pointer to Chapter 12 and Chapter 15.
- **Check.**

## 11-defect-from-normality.md — How far from normal?

- **Schur's inequality** `thm-schur-inequality`: for \( \A \in M_n(\nC) \) with eigenvalues \( \lambda_i \), \( \sum |\lambda_i|^2 \le \norm{\A}_F^2 \), with **equality iff \( \A \) is normal**. Proof: Schur-triangularize, and note \( \norm{\A}_F \) is unitarily invariant (prove that first — `lem-frobenius-unitarily-invariant`), so the defect is exactly the off-diagonal mass of \( \T \).
  - This is the chapter's quantitative theorem: it turns "normal" from a yes/no property into a measurement.
- **Definition** `def-departure-from-normality`: \( \Delta(\A)^2 = \norm{\A}_F^2 - \sum|\lambda_i|^2 \); it is zero exactly for normal matrices, and unitarily invariant. Compute it for a Jordan block and for a small non-normal example.
- **Eigenvalue sensitivity, stated honestly:** a normal matrix has perfectly conditioned eigenvalues; a nearly defective one does not. Give the classic 2×2 example \( \begin{pmatrix} 1 & M \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \) perturbed in the corner, computing the eigenvalue shift exactly, and say that Chapter 19 (Bauer–Fike, perturbation theory) is where this is developed.
