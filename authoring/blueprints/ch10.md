# Blueprint: Chapter 10 — Inner Product Spaces

**Directory:** `src/ch10-inner-products/` (new). **Chapter title:** "Inner Product Spaces". **Part:** starts "Part III · Inner Products and Spectral Theory" (config `part` key on this chapter).

**Purpose.** Add geometry: length, angle and perpendicularity. The chapter builds
- inner products and norms, Cauchy–Schwarz;
- orthonormal bases and Gram–Schmidt;
- orthogonal complements and projections;
- least squares and minimum-norm solutions;
- the Riesz representation theorem, which pays off Chapter 4's "no natural isomorphism V ≅ V*";
- the adjoint, and the four fundamental subspaces of a matrix in their orthogonal form;
- isometries and unitary maps;
- QR, Householder and Givens, and Hessenberg form;
- Bessel, Parseval and Fourier series;
- orthogonal polynomials.

**Allowed prerequisites.** Chapters 0–9, though very little of 7–9 is needed; keep the chapter readable right after Chapter 4. **The field is ℝ or ℂ throughout** (say so once, prominently, and explain why: we need conjugation and positivity). No eigenvalues are required, but they may be used in examples and exercises (Ch 8 is available). Analysis facts needed for Fourier series and completeness are limited to: the convergence of a monotone bounded real sequence, and (for the Fourier example) term-by-term integration of a finite sum only. Infinite-dimensional spaces appear only in clearly flagged examples; the theory is finite-dimensional unless stated.

**Notation** (NOTATION.md has most of it):
- \( \inner{\u}{\v} \), **linear in the first slot**, conjugate-linear in the second (state and use consistently; warn that many books do the opposite);
- \( \norm{\v} \);
- \( U^{\perp} \);
- \( P_U \) orthogonal projection;
- \( T^{*} \) adjoint, \( A^{*} \) conjugate transpose;
- \( \Orth(n) \), \( \Unit(n) \);
- \( \delta_{ij} \) (add a row if missing).

**Signature moves:**
- **To show a vector is zero, pair it with itself** (non-degeneracy).
- **To move T across an inner product, pay with \( T^{*} \).**
- **Work with the norm squared**, then expand.
- **Fix an orthonormal basis** to turn an abstract statement into coordinates.
- **Project, then subtract:** Gram–Schmidt and least squares are the same move.

## 01-inner-products.md — Inner products and norms

- **Hook:** the dot product on \( \nR^n \) computes lengths and angles by the law of cosines. Abstract exactly the properties that make this work.
- **Definition** `def-inner-product` (**major**): for F = ℝ or ℂ, a map V × V → F that is linear in the first argument, satisfies \( \inner{\v}{\u} = \conj{\inner{\u}{\v}} \), and \( \inner{\v}{\v} > 0 \) for \( \v \neq \0 \). Derive conjugate-linearity in the second slot.
  - **Bold:** positive **definite**; \( \inner{\v}{\v} \) is **real** (proof); the conjugate in the second slot.
- **Examples:** the dot product on ℝⁿ; the standard inner product on ℂⁿ; weighted inner products; \( \inner{f}{g} = \int_0^1 f\conj{g} \) on continuous functions; \( \inner{A}{B} = \tr(B^{*}A) \) on matrices (the Frobenius inner product); \( \inner{p}{q} = \sum p(c_i)\conj{q(c_i)} \) on \( F[x]_{\le n} \) for n + 1 distinct nodes (positivity from the root bound, Ch 5).
- **Non-examples by minimal change:** the bilinear form \( x_1y_1 - x_2y_2 \) on ℝ² (fails positivity: name the vector); \( \inner{\u}{\v} = \u\tp\v \) on ℂ² (fails positivity: take \( (1, i) \)) — this is why the conjugate is needed; \( \int_0^1 fg \) on all Riemann-integrable functions (a non-zero function with zero integral of its square — flag the subtlety and restrict to continuous functions).
- **Norm** `def-induced-norm`; `thm-norm-properties`: homogeneity \( \norm{c\v} = |c|\norm{\v} \), positivity, and the polarization/expansion identity \( \norm{\u + \v}^2 = \norm{\u}^2 + 2\Re\inner{\u}{\v} + \norm{\v}^2 \).
- **Orthogonality** `def-orthogonal`; **Pythagoras** `thm-pythagoras`; orthogonal vectors are independent — `thm-orthogonal-independent` (the "pair with itself" move).
- **Cauchy–Schwarz** — `thm-cauchy-schwarz`: \( |\inner{\u}{\v}| \le \norm{\u}\norm{\v} \), with equality iff dependent.
  - **Idea:** the ℝ² picture, then the algebraic proof by projecting \( \u \) onto \( \v \) and expanding \( \norm{\u - c\v}^2 \ge 0 \).
- **Triangle inequality** — `cor-triangle-inequality`; the reverse triangle inequality as an exercise. **Angle** `def-angle` in the real case, and why it needs Cauchy–Schwarz.
- **Warnings:** the conjugate goes in the second slot here, and books differ; \( \norm{\v}^2 = \inner{\v}{\v} \) is real and non-negative, never complex; not every norm comes from an inner product (forward pointer to Ch 15, and the parallelogram law as the test — state it, prove one direction as an exercise).
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** verify the axioms for listed candidates (planted failures); compute angles and norms in ℝ³ and in the polynomial and matrix examples; Cauchy–Schwarz with explicit vectors, and the classical inequality \( (\sum a_ib_i)^2 \le \sum a_i^2 \sum b_i^2 \).
  - **C:** the parallelogram law characterizes inner-product norms in ℝ² (one direction); \( \inner{\u}{\v} \) recovered from norms (polarization, both fields); the Cauchy–Schwarz equality case in full.

## 02-orthonormal-bases.md — Orthonormal bases and Gram–Schmidt

- **Definition** `def-orthonormal-list` (**major**), `def-orthonormal-basis`.
- **Coordinates are easy** — `thm-orthonormal-coordinates`: \( \v = \sum \inner{\v}{\e_i}\e_i \), \( \norm{\v}^2 = \sum |\inner{\v}{\e_i}|^2 \) (Parseval for finite bases), and \( \inner{\u}{\v} = \sum \inner{\u}{\e_i}\conj{\inner{\v}{\e_i}} \).
- **Gram–Schmidt** — `thm-gram-schmidt`: existence of an orthonormal basis; the process preserves spans of initial segments; every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis; every orthonormal list extends to an orthonormal basis (`cor-extend-orthonormal-basis`).
  - Idea with a TikZ picture in ℝ³ (project, subtract, normalize).
  - **Worked:** Gram–Schmidt on three vectors in ℝ³ with clean numbers; and on \( (1, x, x^2) \) with \( \int_{-1}^1 \) giving the Legendre polynomials up to scaling — `exm-gram-schmidt-legendre` (forward pointer to §10).
- **Isometric classification** — `cor-inner-product-spaces-isomorphic`: every n-dimensional inner product space over F is isometrically isomorphic to Fⁿ with the standard product (the coordinate map with respect to an orthonormal basis preserves inner products).
- **Gram matrices** — `def-gram-matrix`, `thm-gram-matrix-properties`: \( G = (\inner{\v_j}{\v_i}) \) is Hermitian; \( G \) is invertible ⇔ the list is independent (proof via \( \x^{*}G\x = \norm{\sum x_j\v_j}^2 \)); det G ≥ 0 (recall Ch 6 `cor-gram-determinant-nonnegative`, paying off that pointer).
- **Warning:** Gram–Schmidt depends on the order of the list; the result is not unique. Numerical instability of the classical process (forward pointer to Ch 23).
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** Gram–Schmidt in ℝ³/ℝ⁴ and on polynomials; coordinates and norms via an orthonormal basis; Gram matrix computations deciding independence.
  - **C:** the Gram matrix of a list determines it up to an isometry; Gram–Schmidt applied to a dependent list (which step fails, and how to repair it by skipping).

## 03-orthogonal-complements-and-projections.md — Orthogonal complements and projections

- **Definition** `def-orthogonal-complement` (**major**); it is a subspace for any subset.
- **Theorem** `thm-orthogonal-decomposition`: for a finite-dimensional subspace U of any inner product space, \( V = U \oplus U^{\perp} \), so \( \dim U^{\perp} = \dim V - \dim U \) in finite dimension; \( (U^{\perp})^{\perp} = U \).
- **Orthogonal projection** `def-orthogonal-projection`; `thm-projection-formula`: \( P_U\v = \sum \inner{\v}{\e_i}\e_i \) for an orthonormal basis of U; \( P_U \) is linear, idempotent, and \( P_U^{*} = P_U \) (state the adjoint part after §06, or prove the symmetry \( \inner{P_U\u}{\v} = \inner{\u}{P_U\v} \) directly here).
- **Best approximation** — `thm-best-approximation`: \( \norm{\v - P_U\v} \le \norm{\v - \u} \) for all u ∈ U, with equality only at \( \u = P_U\v \). Idea: a TikZ picture; proof by Pythagoras.
- **Distance to a subspace** `def-distance-to-subspace`; worked example in ℝ³ and in a polynomial space (best quadratic approximation to \( x^3 \) or to \( \sin \) on an interval, with a clean integral).
- **Warning:** orthogonal complement vs annihilator (Ch 4): both "kill" U, but one lives in V and the other in V*; §06 explains the exact relation. Also: \( U^{\perp} \) depends on the inner product, and "orthogonal projection" differs from the oblique projections of Ch 3 §10.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** compute complements and projections in ℝ³/ℝ⁴; distance from a point to a plane; best quadratic approximation on an interval.
  - **C:** \( (U + W)^{\perp} = U^{\perp} \cap W^{\perp} \) and \( (U \cap W)^{\perp} = U^{\perp} + W^{\perp} \) (finite dimension); a subspace of an infinite-dimensional space with \( U \oplus U^{\perp} \neq V \) (the polynomials inside a space of sequences, or a non-closed subspace: keep it concrete and honest, flagging that completeness is the issue).

## 04-least-squares.md — Least squares and minimum-norm solutions

- **Hook:** an inconsistent system \( A\x = \b \). Ask for the \( \x \) making \( \norm{A\x - \b} \) smallest.
- **Normal equations** — `thm-least-squares`: the minimizers are exactly the solutions of \( A^{*}A\x = A^{*}\b \); they exist; the minimizing \( A\x \) is \( P_{\col A}\b \).
  - `cor-least-squares-unique`: unique ⇔ the columns of A are independent ⇔ \( A^{*}A \) is invertible; then \( \x = (A^{*}A)^{-1}A^{*}\b \).
  - **Warning:** \( (A^{*}A)^{-1} \neq A^{-1}(A^{*})^{-1} \) in general (A need not be square or invertible).
- **Minimum-norm solution** — `thm-minimum-norm-solution`: among the solutions of a consistent system, exactly one lies in \( (\ker A)^{\perp} = \row(A^{*}) \)-type space; it is the one of smallest norm.
- **Worked:** fitting a line to four data points (integer data, exact arithmetic) — `exm-least-squares-line`; fitting a quadratic; a minimum-norm solution of an underdetermined system.
- **Forward pointer:** the pseudoinverse in Ch 12 packages both answers.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** least-squares line and parabola fits; verify the normal equations; minimum-norm solution of a 2×3 system.
  - **C:** weighted least squares; the residual is orthogonal to the column space, and its norm computed two ways; least squares with a rank-deficient matrix (all minimizers described).

## 05-riesz-and-duality.md — The Riesz representation theorem

Short: ≈2,500–3,000 words.

- **Riesz** — `thm-riesz-representation`: every linear functional on a finite-dimensional inner product space is \( \v \mapsto \inner{\v}{\w} \) for a unique w. Proof via an orthonormal basis (or via \( (\ker\varphi)^{\perp} \)).
- **The conjugate-linear isomorphism** V → V*; over ℝ it is linear, over ℂ conjugate-linear — `cor-riesz-isomorphism`. **This is the "chosen isomorphism" Ch 4 promised**: recall Ch 4's warning that V ≅ V* needs extra data, and say that the inner product is exactly that data. Pay off the Ch 4 → Ch 10 promise.
- **Annihilator vs orthogonal complement** — `thm-annihilator-vs-orthogonal-complement`: under the Riesz map, \( U^{0} \) corresponds to \( U^{\perp} \).
- **Infinite dimension:** a functional on an infinite-dimensional space need not be representable; give the concrete example on polynomials with an honest remark that the general theorem needs completeness and continuity (forward pointer to Hilbert spaces, no theory).
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** find the Riesz vector for listed functionals (evaluation at a point on \( F[x]_{\le 2} \) with a given inner product; trace on matrices); verify the correspondence \( U^{0} \leftrightarrow U^{\perp} \) for a concrete U.
  - **C:** the Riesz map is conjugate-linear and bijective, in full; a functional on \( \nR[x] \) with no Riesz vector.

## 06-adjoints.md — The adjoint

- **Hook:** move an operator to the other side of an inner product.
- **Existence and uniqueness** — `thm-adjoint-exists`: for T ∈ 𝓛(V, W) between finite-dimensional inner product spaces there is a unique \( T^{*} \in \cL(W, V) \) with \( \inner{T\v}{\w} = \inner{\v}{T^{*}\w} \). Proof via Riesz. **Definition** `def-adjoint` (**major**).
- **Properties** — `thm-adjoint-properties`: \( (S + T)^{*} \), \( (cT)^{*} = \conj{c}T^{*} \), \( (ST)^{*} = T^{*}S^{*} \), \( T^{**} = T \), \( (T^{-1})^{*} = (T^{*})^{-1} \).
- **Matrix of the adjoint** — `thm-matrix-of-adjoint`: in orthonormal bases, \( [T^{*}] = [T]^{*} \) (conjugate transpose). **Warning:** false for non-orthonormal bases, with an example. Relation to the dual map (Ch 4 `thm-matrix-of-dual-map`): the adjoint is the dual map read through Riesz — `thm-adjoint-vs-dual`.
- **Four fundamental subspaces** — `thm-four-subspaces-orthogonal`: \( \ker T^{*} = (\im T)^{\perp} \), \( \im T^{*} = (\ker T)^{\perp} \), and the two decompositions \( V = \ker T \oplus \im T^{*} \), \( W = \im T \oplus \ker T^{*} \). For matrices: null(A) ⟂ row(A) etc. (recall Ch 2 and Ch 4's `thm-row-space-annihilates-null-space`, paying off that pointer).
  - `cor-rank-adjoint`: rank T\* = rank T; \( \ker(T^{*}T) = \ker T \) (used for least squares in §04 — if §04 needed it earlier, prove the small lemma there and cite it here).
- **Self-adjoint and normal operators:** define `def-self-adjoint` and `def-normal-operator` here, and prove only the immediate facts: a self-adjoint operator has real diagonal entries in every orthonormal basis, and \( \inner{T\v}{\v} \) is real for self-adjoint T. All spectral theory waits for Ch 11 (forward pointer).
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** compute adjoints of concrete maps (matrix maps, differentiation on a polynomial space with a given inner product, the shift on a finite sequence space); verify \( (ST)^{*} = T^{*}S^{*} \); find \( \ker T^{*} \) and check orthogonality to im T.
  - **C:** the adjoint of the integration operator; \( \rank(T^{*}T) = \rank T \); a map with \( T^{*} = -T \) has \( \inner{T\v}{\v} \) purely imaginary (ℂ) or zero (ℝ).

## 07-isometries-and-unitary-maps.md — Isometries and unitary maps

- **Definition** `def-isometry`: \( \norm{T\v} = \norm{\v} \) for all v.
- **Characterizations** — `thm-isometry-characterizations` (finite dimension): isometry ⇔ preserves inner products ⇔ \( T^{*}T = I \) ⇔ sends some/every orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis ⇔ the columns of the matrix in orthonormal bases are orthonormal. For operators on a finite-dimensional space, also ⇔ \( TT^{*} = I \) ⇔ T is invertible with \( T^{-1} = T^{*} \) (**unitary**, `def-unitary-orthogonal`).
- **Groups** \( \Unit(n) \), \( \Orth(n) \), \( \SO(n) \); |det| = 1 for unitary, det = ±1 for orthogonal (recall Ch 6).
- **Classification in low dimension** — `thm-orthogonal-2x2`: every element of O(2) is a rotation or a reflection; the rotation–reflection pictures (TikZ). ℝ³: a rotation about an axis (state; the proof needs eigenvalues — it is available from Ch 8, so give it: a real eigenvalue ±1 exists in odd dimension).
- **Warning:** an isometry between spaces of different dimensions can satisfy \( T^{*}T = I \) but not \( TT^{*} = I \) (a 3×2 example): "identity on what?".
- **Rigid motions** (brief): a distance-preserving map of ℝⁿ fixing 0 is linear and orthogonal — `thm-rigid-motion-linear` (proof via polarization), and the general rigid motion is orthogonal plus translation.
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** decide which matrices are orthogonal/unitary; find the rotation angle or reflection axis of a 2×2; show a permutation matrix is orthogonal.
  - **C:** O(n) is a group and its elements have det ±1; a linear map preserving norms preserves inner products (polarization, both fields); the product of two reflections in ℝ² is a rotation by twice the angle.

## 08-qr-and-householder.md — QR, Householder and Hessenberg

- **QR factorization** — `thm-qr-factorization`: every A ∈ M_{m×n} with independent columns is QR with Q having orthonormal columns and R upper triangular with positive diagonal; uniqueness. Proof = Gram–Schmidt in matrix form.
  - **Worked:** a 3×2 QR by Gram–Schmidt — `exm-qr-3x2`.
  - **Use:** least squares via QR (\( R\x = Q^{*}\b \)), more stable than the normal equations (state, with the reason deferred to Ch 23).
- **Householder reflections** — `def-householder-reflection`: \( H = I - 2\ww^{*}/\norm{\w}^2 \); it is unitary, self-adjoint, an involution, det = −1. `thm-householder-maps-vector`: a reflection taking any x to \( \norm{\x}\e_1 \) (with the sign chosen for stability, explained).
  - **QR by Householder** — `thm-qr-householder`: successive reflections triangularize A; this version works for dependent columns too.
- **Givens rotations** (brief): zeroing one entry at a time.
- **Hessenberg form** — `thm-hessenberg-form`: every A ∈ M_n(ℂ) is unitarily similar to a Hessenberg matrix (zeros below the first subdiagonal), by Householder reflections applied on both sides. Forward pointer: this is the first step of the QR algorithm (Ch 23).
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** QR of a 3×3 by Gram–Schmidt and by one Householder step; solve a least-squares problem via QR; the Householder matrix taking a given vector to a multiple of e₁.
  - **C:** uniqueness of QR with positive diagonal; the Householder QR of a matrix with dependent columns; Givens rotations to triangularize a 2×2.

## 09-bessel-parseval-fourier.md — Bessel, Parseval and Fourier series

- **Orthogonal expansions:** for an orthonormal list \( (\e_1, \dots, \e_k) \) in any inner product space, the projection is \( \sum \inner{\v}{\e_i}\e_i \), and **Bessel's inequality** — `thm-bessel-inequality` — holds; equality ⇔ v is in the span (`thm-parseval-identity` for an orthonormal basis).
- **Trigonometric system:** on the continuous functions on \( [-\pi, \pi] \) with \( \inner{f}{g} = \frac1\pi\int f\conj{g} \), the functions \( 1/\sqrt2, \cos kx, \sin kx \) are orthonormal — `exm-trigonometric-orthonormal` (verify the integrals). Fourier coefficients as inner products; the partial sum is the best approximation in this norm (from §03) — `thm-fourier-best-approximation`.
  - **Worked:** the Fourier coefficients of a square wave or of \( x \) on \( [-\pi, \pi] \); Bessel's inequality gives \( \sum 1/k^2 \le \pi^2/6 \)-type bounds. Keep every integral elementary and state the analysis facts assumed (integration of finite sums; no convergence theorems).
  - **Honest boundary:** whether the Fourier series converges pointwise is not a linear-algebra question; say so, and say what the projection statement does give.
- **Legendre polynomials** revisited (from §02); Chebyshev with the weight \( 1/\sqrt{1-x^2} \) (state the orthogonality, verify n ≤ 2 by direct integration).
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** compute Fourier coefficients of simple functions; verify Bessel for a truncation; best approximation of \( x^2 \) by \( \Span(1, \cos x) \).
  - **C:** Parseval for the square wave gives \( \sum 1/(2k-1)^2 = \pi^2/8 \) (assume the Parseval identity for the full orthonormal system, flagged as an analysis input); the orthogonality of the Chebyshev polynomials by substitution.

## 10-orthogonal-polynomials.md — Orthogonal polynomials

≈2,500–3,000 words.

- **Setting:** an inner product on \( \nR[x] \) (or \( \nR[x]_{\le n} \)) from a positive weight on an interval, or from distinct nodes.
- **Existence** by Gram–Schmidt on \( (1, x, x^2, \dots) \) — `def-orthogonal-polynomial-sequence`; monic normalization.
- **Three-term recurrence** — `thm-three-term-recurrence`: \( p_{k+1} = (x - a_k)p_k - b_kp_{k-1} \), with \( a_k = \inner{xp_k}{p_k}/\inner{p_k}{p_k} \), \( b_k = \inner{p_k}{p_k}/\inner{p_{k-1}}{p_{k-1}} \). Proof: expand \( xp_k \) in the orthogonal basis and use \( \inner{xp_k}{p_j} = \inner{p_k}{xp_j} \) (the multiplication operator is self-adjoint).
- **Roots** — `thm-orthogonal-polynomial-roots`: \( p_k \) has k distinct real roots inside the interval. Proof: sign-change argument (a polynomial with fewer sign changes would pair non-orthogonally).
- **Gaussian quadrature** — `thm-gaussian-quadrature`: nodes at the roots of \( p_k \) with weights from Lagrange interpolation integrate every polynomial of degree ≤ 2k − 1 exactly. Proof via division by \( p_k \) (Ch 5). **Worked:** two-point Gauss–Legendre on \( [-1, 1] \) (nodes \( \pm 1/\sqrt3 \)), integrating a cubic exactly.
- **Examples table:** Legendre, Chebyshev, Hermite, Laguerre with their weights (statements only; verify the first two members of each by integration in an exercise).
- **Check.**
- **Exercises:**
  - **A.**
  - **B:** compute the first three monic orthogonal polynomials for a given weight; verify the recurrence; two-point quadrature on an example.
  - **C:** the roots of \( p_k \) interlace those of \( p_{k+1} \) (forward pointer to Ch 16 interlacing; prove it for k ≤ 2 by hand or via the recurrence); Gaussian quadrature is exact for degree 2k − 1 but not 2k.
