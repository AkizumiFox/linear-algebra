# Blueprint: Chapter 23 — Algebras and Representations

**Directory:** `src/ch23-algebras/` (new). **Chapter title:** "Algebras and Representations". **Part:** continues "Part VI · Geometry, Algebra, Computation". The coordinator inserts `"src/ch23-algebras"` in `config/config.json` **between** `src/ch22-geometry` and `src/ch24-applied`, with **no `part` key** — Chapter 22 already carries the part heading. Chapter numbers come from directory names.

**Purpose.** The book has studied one operator at a time, or a commuting family. This chapter studies a whole **set of operators closed under composition** — an algebra — and the main theorem says that over an algebraically closed field there is essentially only one kind of building block: a full matrix algebra. Representation theory is the same statement read for groups: a group acting linearly decomposes into irreducible pieces, and the characters of those pieces are an orthonormal basis for the class functions.

The chapter is where the book's recurring themes converge. Schur's lemma is the reason eigenspaces of commuting operators matched up in Chapter 9; the double commutant theorem is what Chapter 10's Weyr commutant was circling; and the representation theory of a finite abelian group is exactly the discrete Fourier transform of Chapter 12 §09, seen from above.

**The chapter is owed nothing and owes nothing.** No section of the book promises Chapter 23 anything — grep confirms it. That is freedom, and it is also a risk: **nothing outside the chapter forces its scope**, so the honesty rule below decides how far it goes.

**Conventions to state once and obey.**
- \( F \) is a field; say at each theorem whether it must be algebraically closed, of characteristic zero, or of characteristic not dividing \( \lvert G\rvert \). **Most of the chapter's theorems are false without the right hypothesis**, and the chapter's value is in getting those hypotheses right.
- Algebras are **associative, with identity, and finite-dimensional over \( F \)** unless a statement says otherwise. Say this once, in the index and again at `def-algebra` — Chapter 14 §12's `def-algebra-over-field` is the definition already in the book, so **build on it, do not restate it**.
- Groups are **finite** in §§07–10.
- A representation is a homomorphism \( \rho \colon G \to \GL(V) \) with \( V \) finite-dimensional over \( F \); the module language is mentioned once and then avoided, since the book has no module theory beyond Chapter 10 §07's \( F[x] \)-modules.
- **Label hygiene:** `def-irreducible` is taken (Ch 19 §02, irreducible matrices) and `def-irreducible-polynomial` is taken (Ch 6). Use `def-irreducible-representation` and `def-simple-algebra`. `def-dft-matrix` is **Chapter 24's** and must not be cited — Chapter 24 comes later; cite Chapter 12 §09's `thm-fourier-matrix-unitary` and `def-circulant` instead.

**Verified labels to build on.** Each checked against `src/`; grep anything else and read the statement before citing it.
- **Ch 0 §10:** `def-group`, `def-subgroup`, `def-group-homomorphism`, `thm-group-basic-properties`, `thm-subgroup-test`, `exm-groups`, `def-alternating-group`. This is all the group theory the book has: **there is no Lagrange's theorem, no quotient group, no Sylow theory.** If a proof needs one, prove it in place or restructure — and say which you did.
- **Ch 14 §12:** `def-algebra-over-field`, `def-quaternions`, `exm-clifford-quaternions`, `def-clifford-algebra`. **Ch 15 §06, §10:** `def-tensor-algebra`, `thm-tensor-algebra-is-an-algebra`, `thm-tensor-algebra-universal`, `def-two-sided-ideal`, `lem-quotient-algebra`, `prp-ideal-generated`.
- **Ch 14 §10:** `def-classical-groups`, `thm-classical-groups-are-groups`, `prp-congruent-forms-conjugate-groups`.
- **Ch 9 §09:** `thm-commuting-common-eigenvector`, `thm-commuting-preserves-eigenspaces`, `thm-simultaneous-diagonalization`, `thm-simultaneous-triangularization` (grep the exact label). **Ch 10:** `def-semisimple-operator`, `prp-semisimple-iff-diagonalizable-over-extension`, `prp-weyr-commutant`, `thm-smith-normal-form` and §07's \( F[x] \)-module material — the one place the book has anything module-shaped.

**Honesty and scope — read this twice.** This chapter can expand without limit, and the book does not need it to. The rule is the one Chapter 24 used: **prove it, or state it with credit and mark it plainly as not proved here, with nothing depending on it.** Specifically:
- **Wedderburn–Artin is stated and proved only in the case the book can carry:** \( F \) algebraically closed, \( A \) finite-dimensional and semisimple. The general form over a division ring is **stated with credit and not proved**, and the text says the book has not built division rings.
- **Burnside's theorem** (a subalgebra of \( \End(V) \) acting irreducibly over an algebraically closed field is all of \( \End(V) \)) **is proved** — it is the engine of the chapter and it is within reach.
- **Character orthogonality is proved over \( \nC \) for finite groups**; the modular case is not touched, and §08 says so once.
- **No induced representations, no Frobenius reciprocity, no character tables beyond small examples.** If a drafter wants one, it goes in an exercise, not the text.

**Negative results the chapter must contain.**
- A representation that does not decompose: the group \( \nZ \) acting on \( F^2 \) by \( \begin{psmallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{psmallmatrix} \), or a finite group in characteristic \( p \) dividing \( \lvert G\rvert \) — **Maschke's theorem fails and the standard witness is \( \nZ/p \) acting on \( \nF_p^2 \)** (§07 or §08). Verify it.
- Schur's lemma fails without algebraic closure: the rotation representation of a cyclic group on \( \nR^2 \) is irreducible with commutant \( \nC \), not \( F \) (§04). Verify it.
- Two non-isomorphic groups with the same character table (the smallest pair is of order 8) — **state with credit, do not construct**, unless the drafter can do it cleanly in an exercise.
- An algebra that is not semisimple: the upper triangular \( 2 \times 2 \) matrices, with its radical exhibited (§03).

## index.md
The story: from one operator, to a commuting family, to an algebra; and the same statement read for groups. **What you need**, by chapter — with the warning that Chapter 0 §10 is *all* the group theory the book has. **Roadmap.** **Named moves.** The standing hypotheses on the field, and an honest list of what the chapter quotes and does not prove.

## 01-associative-algebras.md — Associative algebras
- Build on Ch 14 §12's `def-algebra-over-field`; do not restate it. Add `def-subalgebra`, `def-algebra-homomorphism`, `def-algebra-isomorphism`, and the notion of the algebra **generated** by a set of operators.
- Examples, each verified: \( \End(V) \) and \( M_n(F) \); \( F[x] \) and \( F[x]/(p) \); the polynomial algebra \( F[T] \) generated by one operator, whose dimension is \( \deg m_T \) (Ch 6's `thm-annihilator-ideal`); the circulants (Ch 12 §09's `cor-circulants-algebra`); the group algebra \( F[G] \) — **define it here**, since §§07–10 need it; the quaternions (Ch 14 §12); the upper triangular matrices.
- `thm-cayley-for-algebras`: every finite-dimensional algebra embeds in \( \End(A) \) by left multiplication. Prove it. It is the reason matrices are general and not a special case.
- `def-commutant` \( A' = \{b : ab = ba \text{ for all } a \in A\} \); `prp-commutant-is-algebra`; the centre. Worked commutants: of a single Jordan block, of a diagonal matrix with distinct entries, of \( M_n(F) \) itself (scalars — prove it).
- Warning: an algebra is not a group, and invertibility is not assumed; \( F[x] \) is the standing example.

## 02-ideals-and-quotients.md — Ideals and quotients
- Build on Ch 15 §10's `def-two-sided-ideal`, `prp-ideal-generated` and `lem-quotient-algebra`; add left and right ideals, which the book does not have.
- `thm-matrix-algebra-is-simple` (**major**): \( M_n(F) \) has no two-sided ideals but \( 0 \) and itself. Prove it by the matrix-unit computation \( E_{ij}XE_{kl} = x_{jk}E_{il} \). **This is the chapter's first real theorem and everything later leans on it.**
- Its left ideals are not trivial: the column ideals \( \{X : \col(X) \subseteq U\} \), and `prp-left-ideals-of-matrix-algebra` identifying them with subspaces. Prove what is used later; state the classification only if proved.
- `def-quotient-algebra` and the first isomorphism theorem for algebras (`thm-algebra-first-isomorphism`), from Ch 4 §04's quotient machinery.
- Worked example: the upper triangular matrices, their ideal of strictly upper triangular matrices, and the quotient, which is \( F \times F \).

## 03-simple-and-semisimple.md — Simple and semisimple algebras
- `def-simple-algebra`, `def-simple-module` (state it for a representation of an algebra on a vector space, to avoid module theory: an \( A \)-space with no invariant subspace but \( 0 \) and itself), `def-semisimple-algebra` (every invariant subspace has an invariant complement — the definition the chapter can actually use).
- `thm-semisimple-iff-sum-of-simples`: for an algebra acting on \( V \), every invariant subspace has an invariant complement iff \( V \) is a sum of irreducible invariant subspaces. **Prove both directions**; the forward direction is an induction on dimension and is the section's real content.
- `def-algebra-radical` for the case at hand (note: `def-radical` is taken), and `exm-triangular-not-semisimple`: the upper triangular matrices are not semisimple, with the failing invariant subspace exhibited.
- Connect to Chapter 10: an operator is semisimple exactly when the algebra \( F[T] \) it generates acts semisimply on \( V \) (Ch 10 §08's `def-semisimple-operator`). Prove it — it ties the new definition to the book's old one and is the best check that the definition is right.

## 04-schurs-lemma.md — Schur's lemma
- `thm-schurs-lemma` (**major**): a homomorphism of irreducible representations is zero or an isomorphism; and over an algebraically closed field the endomorphisms of an irreducible representation are the scalars. Prove both parts; the second needs an eigenvalue, hence algebraic closure (Ch 6's FTA, or `thm-complex-operator-has-eigenvalue`).
- **Negative result:** without algebraic closure the second part fails — the cyclic rotation representation on \( \nR^2 \) is irreducible and its commutant is a copy of \( \nC \). Verify the commutant explicitly.
- `cor-commuting-with-irreducible`: an operator commuting with an irreducible family is a scalar. Then look back: this is why Chapter 9 §09's commuting operators shared eigenspaces, and why Chapter 12 §07's commuting normal families diagonalize together. Make that connection explicitly — it is the chapter's best payoff to earlier material.
- `cor-abelian-irreducibles-are-lines`: over an algebraically closed field every irreducible representation of an abelian group (or a commutative algebra) is one-dimensional. Prove it; §10 rests on it.

## 05-double-commutant.md — Burnside and the double commutant
- `thm-burnside` (**major**): a subalgebra of \( \End(V) \) acting irreducibly, over an algebraically closed field, is all of \( \End(V) \). Prove it. Route: use Schur to get the commutant equal to the scalars, then show the algebra contains a rank-one operator and, by irreducibility, all of them. **Verify the rank-one step carefully; that is where the proof usually has a hole.**
- `thm-double-commutant` (**major**): for a semisimple subalgebra \( A \subseteq \End(V) \) containing the identity, \( A'' = A \). Prove it in the case the chapter can carry, and state the hypotheses exactly.
- `cor-weyr-commutant-revisited`: read Chapter 10 §04's `prp-weyr-commutant` and Chapter 12 §10's `cor-commutant-block-diagonal` in this language, as instances. Cite, do not reprove.
- Worked example: \( A \) the diagonal matrices in \( M_n \), \( A' = A \), \( A'' = A \); and \( A = \) scalars, \( A' = M_n \), \( A'' = \) scalars.

## 06-wedderburn.md — The structure theorem
- `thm-wedderburn` (**major**, in the case the book can prove): a finite-dimensional semisimple algebra over an **algebraically closed** field is isomorphic to a product \( M_{n_1}(F) \times \dots \times M_{n_k}(F) \), and the \( n_i \) are determined up to order. Prove existence and uniqueness. Route: decompose the regular representation into irreducibles, group the isotypic components, and apply §05.
- `cor-dimension-count`: \( \dim A = \sum n_i^2 \). For a group algebra this becomes \( \lvert G\rvert = \sum n_i^2 \) in §08 — **flag that forward pointer as prose, not as an `@ref`.**
- **Stated, with credit, and not proved:** the general Wedderburn–Artin theorem over a division ring, and Wedderburn's little theorem (a finite division ring is a field). Say the book has not built division rings, and let nothing depend on either.
- Worked example: decompose \( \nC[\nZ/4] \) as \( \nC^4 \) explicitly, and the algebra of \( 2\times2 \) matrices over \( \nC \) as its own Wedderburn form.

## 07-group-representations.md — Group representations
- `def-representation`, `def-equivalent-representations`, `def-subrepresentation`, `def-irreducible-representation`; the regular representation; the permutation representation; the trivial and sign representations of \( S_n \).
- `thm-representations-are-modules-over-the-group-algebra`: representations of \( G \) over \( F \) are exactly representations of the algebra \( F[G] \). Prove it — this is the bridge that lets §§01–06 do the work.
- `thm-maschke` (**major**): if \( \operatorname{char} F \nmid \lvert G\rvert \) then every representation of a finite group is semisimple. Prove it by averaging a projection; the hypothesis is exactly what makes \( \tfrac{1}{\lvert G\rvert} \) exist. **Negative result:** \( \nZ/p \) on \( \nF_p^2 \) by unipotent matrices, where the invariant line has no invariant complement — verify it.
- `cor-complete-reducibility`: over \( \nC \), every representation of a finite group is a direct sum of irreducibles.
- Worked examples, all verified: the irreducible complex representations of \( \nZ/n \); of \( S_3 \) (trivial, sign, and the two-dimensional one), with matrices written out.

## 08-characters.md — Characters and orthogonality
- `def-character` \( \chi_\rho(g) = \tr\rho(g) \); `prp-character-basic`: constant on conjugacy classes, \( \chi(1) = \dim V \), invariant under equivalence, additive over direct sums. Prove each; Ch 7's trace results do the work.
- `def-class-function` and the inner product \( \langle \chi, \psi\rangle = \tfrac1{\lvert G\rvert}\sum_g \chi(g)\overline{\psi(g)} \).
- `thm-character-orthogonality` (**major**, over \( \nC \)): the irreducible characters are orthonormal. Prove it — the averaging operator of §07 plus Schur's lemma from §04.
- `thm-characters-span-class-functions`: the irreducible characters are a basis of the class functions, so **the number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes**. Prove it, or prove the counting statement by the route the drafter can close and flag anything quoted.
- `cor-sum-of-squares`: \( \lvert G\rvert = \sum (\dim V_i)^2 \), from §06's dimension count.
- `cor-irreducibility-criterion`: \( \langle\chi,\chi\rangle = 1 \) iff irreducible. Use it on §07's \( S_3 \) example.
- The character table of \( S_3 \) and of \( \nZ/4 \), computed in full and checked against every orthogonality relation.
- **Stated with credit, not proved:** that the character table does not determine the group, the standard witness being two groups of order 8.

## 09-the-abelian-case.md — Finite abelian groups and the Fourier transform
- `thm-abelian-characters-are-homomorphisms`: for abelian \( G \) over \( \nC \), every irreducible representation is one-dimensional (§04), so a character is a homomorphism \( G \to \nC^{\times} \).
- `def-dual-group` \( \widehat G \), `thm-dual-group-iso`: for finite abelian \( G \), \( \widehat G \cong G \) — **prove it for cyclic groups and for products of cyclic groups, and say honestly that the book has not proved every finite abelian group is such a product** (that is the structure theorem, which Chapter 10 §07's Smith normal form is the linear-algebra shadow of; cite it and flag the gap).
- `thm-fourier-inversion-on-a-group` and the orthogonality of characters as the unitarity of the character matrix.
- **The payoff:** for \( G = \nZ/n \) the character matrix **is** Chapter 12 §09's Fourier matrix, the convolution theorem is the statement that the Fourier transform turns the group algebra into a product of copies of \( \nC \), and a circulant is exactly an element of \( \nC[\nZ/n] \) acting by multiplication. Prove each of these three identifications — they are the chapter's reason to exist, and they retire Chapter 12 §09's "a circulant is a polynomial in the shift" into a structural statement.
- Worked example: \( n = 4 \), with the character table, the Fourier matrix and a circulant diagonalized, all matching Chapter 12 §09's numbers exactly.

## 10-summary-and-transfer.md — What the chapter buys

- The chapter's named moves: average over the group to build an invariant object; use Schur to force a scalar; count dimensions with \( \sum n_i^2 \); read a commuting family as a commutative algebra.
