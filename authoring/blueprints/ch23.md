**Purpose.** Chapter 20 closed Part V with the sentence this chapter answers: "What none of these chapters has done is compute anything: every proof here is an existence or a comparison, and not one of them is an algorithm." This is the book's largest single debt — **26 promises across 18 files**. The chapter is about three questions:
- **What does a computed answer mean?** Floating-point arithmetic, backward error, and the rule that ties them to the condition numbers of Chapters 15 and 19.
- **How are the objects of this book actually computed?** Elimination with pivoting, least squares, eigenvalues by iteration, Krylov methods, the fast Fourier transform, fast multiplication.
- **What is linear algebra used for?** Vibrations, principal components, graphs.

**The chapter never mentions software.** It speaks of arithmetic, algorithms and cost. No product, library or language is named anywhere, and no lecturer.

**The one new kind of assumption, and how to state it.** Floating-point arithmetic is not a theorem of linear algebra; it is a **model of a machine**. §01 states it as a model (`def-floating-point-model`), in the register the book uses for a definition, not as an imported theorem — everything after it is a theorem *about that model*. Say plainly: the model is an idealization, real arithmetic satisfies it for the four operations and the square root, and every rounding claim in the chapter is a consequence of it. **No analysis beyond Chapter 15's (A1)–(A6) is imported; Chapter 20's (A7) is not used at all.**

- A **flop** is one arithmetic operation. Costs are leading-order counts, written \( \tfrac23 n^3 + O(n^2) \); say once that the constant matters and the exponent matters more.

## 01-floating-point-and-backward-error.md — Floating point and backward error
- Hook: two algorithms for the same problem, one returning \( (0,1) \) and one the right answer (Ch 15 §08 ~533 — read it and continue it).
- `def-floating-point-model`: a finite set of representable numbers, the rounding map \( \fl \), and the model \( \fl(x \odot y) = (x \odot y)(1 + \delta) \), \( |\delta| \le u \), for \( \odot \in \{+,-,\times,\div\} \), and the same for \( \sqrt{\cdot} \). State that overflow and underflow are excluded by assumption, and that \( u \) is about \( 10^{-16} \) in the arithmetic in common use.
- `lem-gamma-bound`: a product of \( k \) factors \( (1+\delta_i)^{\pm1} \) is \( 1 + \theta \) with \( |\theta| \le \gamma_k = ku/(1-ku) \) when \( ku < 1 \). Prove it by induction. This is the workhorse of the chapter.
- `thm-inner-product-backward-error`: the computed inner product satisfies \( \fl(\x^{*}\y) = (\x + \Delta\x)^{*}\y \) with \( |\Delta\x| \le \gamma_n|\x| \) entrywise. Prove it.
- `def-backward-stable`, `def-forward-error`, `def-mixed-stability`: an algorithm is backward stable for a problem when its computed answer is the exact answer for data within \( O(u) \) of the given data. **Pays Ch 15 §08's "defines backward stability".**
- `thm-forward-from-backward` (the rule of thumb, made a theorem): forward relative error \( \le \) condition number \( \times \) backward relative error, up to the linearization already proved in Ch 15 §08 (`thm-relative-error-bound`). State exactly what is being assumed.
- `exm-cancellation`: subtracting nearly equal numbers; the quadratic formula; the same root computed stably. Verify every digit.
- Warning: a small residual is not a small error, and a small error is not a small residual — the two are related by the condition number, and Ch 19 §04's residual bound is the eigenvalue version.

## 02-elimination-pivoting-and-cost.md — Elimination, pivoting and cost
- `thm-elimination-is-schur-complements`: one step of Gaussian elimination replaces the trailing block by the Schur complement of the pivot. **Pays Ch 7 §03.** Prove it with Ch 7's block machinery, and read Ch 2's `thm-lu-exists-without-swaps` as its corollary.
- `prp-lu-cost`: \( \tfrac23 n^3 + O(n^2) \) flops for LU, \( 2n^2 \) for the two triangular solves, \( \tfrac13 n^3 \) for Cholesky. **Pays Ch 12 §02's "about half the arithmetic".** Count honestly, with the sums written out.
- `thm-triangular-solve-backward-error`: the computed solution of \( \T\x = \b \) is exact for \( \T + \Delta\T \) with \( |\Delta\T| \le \gamma_n|\T| \). **Prove it in full** — it is the cleanest backward error analysis in the subject and the model case for the chapter.
- `def-growth-factor` \( g_n \); `thm-lu-backward-error`: the computed factors satisfy \( \widehat{\L}\widehat{\U} = \A + \Delta\A \) with \( |\Delta\A| \le \gamma_n |\widehat{\L}||\widehat{\U}| \), hence a bound in terms of \( g_n \). **Prove what you can; if the general induction is too long, prove it for \( n = 2 \) and state the general case with credit, flagged as not proved here.** Do not bluff.
- Partial pivoting: `prp-partial-pivoting-bounds-multipliers` (all multipliers \( \le 1 \)) and `thm-growth-partial-pivoting` (\( g_n \le 2^{n-1} \)), with `exm-growth-attained` (the matrix with \( 1 \)s on the diagonal, \( -1 \) below and a last column of \( 1 \)s attains it — verify for \( n = 4, 5 \)).
- **Pays Ch 2 §06:** work the \( \varepsilon \)-pivot example in the floating-point model and show that the swapped version is backward stable while the unswapped one is not. Quote Chapter 2's sentence.
- Cholesky needs no pivoting: `thm-cholesky-stability`, because \( |\ell_{ij}| \le \sqrt{a_{jj}} \) bounds every entry. Prove the entry bound.
- Warning: "stable" is not "accurate"; for an ill-conditioned matrix a backward stable solve still loses \( \log_{10}\kappa \) digits, which is §03's subject.

## 04-power-method-and-inverse-iteration.md — The power method
- `def-power-iteration`; `thm-power-method` (**major**): for diagonalizable \( \A \) with \( |\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \) and a starting vector with a non-zero first coordinate, the normalized iterates converge to an eigenvector, with error \( O(|\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^k) \), and the Rayleigh quotients converge to \( \lambda_1 \). **Pays Ch 18 §04, §08, §09.** Prove it.
- `cor-power-method-hermitian`: for Hermitian \( \A \) the Rayleigh quotient converges at rate \( |\lambda_2/\lambda_1|^{2k} \) — the quadratic gain of Ch 16 §01.
- `def-inverse-iteration`, `thm-inverse-iteration`: applying the method to \( (\A - \mu\I)^{-1} \) converges to the eigenvector whose eigenvalue is nearest \( \mu \), at rate governed by the two nearest. Note that the shifted matrix is deliberately ill-conditioned and that this does **no** harm — the error is in the direction of the wanted eigenvector. Say why, citing Ch 19.
- `def-rayleigh-quotient-iteration`, `thm-rqi-cubic`: for Hermitian \( \A \), Rayleigh quotient iteration converges cubically near a simple eigenvalue. **Pays Ch 16 §01 and Ch 19 §06 in their own words.** Prove the cubic rate using Ch 19 §06's `thm-kato-temple` or the residual bound; if the full proof is long, prove the quadratic rate and state the cubic one with credit, flagged.
- Deflation, and the negative result: the cyclic permutation of Ch 18 §05 has \( |\lambda_1| = |\lambda_2| \) and the iteration does not converge.

## 05-the-qr-algorithm.md — The QR algorithm
- Hook: Ch 10 §08's sentence about Abel and Ruffini — no finite algorithm can exist, so every eigenvalue method iterates.
- `def-qr-iteration`: \( \A^{(k)} = \Q^{(k)}\R^{(k)} \), \( \A^{(k+1)} = \R^{(k)}\Q^{(k)} \). `prp-qr-iteration-similar`: each step is a unitary similarity.
- `thm-qr-is-orthogonal-iteration` (**major**): the QR iterates are the orthogonal iteration applied to \( \A^k \); precisely, \( \A^k = (\Q^{(1)}\cdots\Q^{(k)})(\R^{(k)}\cdots\R^{(1)}) \). Prove it by induction. This is why it converges, and it ties the section to §04.
- `thm-qr-convergence`: for \( |\lambda_1| > \dots > |\lambda_n| \) the iterates converge to upper triangular form, with the \( (i+1,i) \) entry going to zero like \( |\lambda_{i+1}/\lambda_i|^k \). Prove it, or prove the \( 2 \times 2 \) and state the general case; be explicit which.
- Hessenberg first: `prp-hessenberg-preserved` (a QR step preserves Hessenberg form) and the cost argument, \( O(n^2) \) per step instead of \( O(n^3) \). **Pays Ch 10 §08 ~488 in its own words.**
- Shifts: the shifted step \( \A - \mu\I = \Q\R \), \( \A' = \R\Q + \mu\I \); the Rayleigh shift and the Wilkinson shift; why a shift near an eigenvalue makes one subdiagonal entry collapse (connect to §04's inverse iteration).
- Negative result: the unshifted algorithm on a matrix with two eigenvalues of equal modulus (a rotation) does nothing; the shift repairs it.
- Worked example, exact where possible: three steps on a \( 3 \times 3 \) symmetric matrix, with the subdiagonal entries shrinking.

## 06-krylov-subspaces.md — Krylov subspaces, Arnoldi and Lanczos
- `def-krylov-subspace` \( \cK_k(\A,\b) = \Span(\b, \A\b, \dots, \A^{k-1}\b) \); `prp-krylov-properties`: nested, invariant once it stops growing, and its dimension is the degree of the minimal polynomial of \( \b \) (cite Ch 9's cyclic subspaces).
- `thm-arnoldi` (**major**): the Arnoldi process produces an orthonormal basis of \( \cK_k \) with \( \A\Q_k = \Q_{k+1}\widetilde{\H}_k \), \( \widetilde{\H}_k \) upper Hessenberg. Prove it; it is Gram–Schmidt with a purpose.
- `thm-lanczos`: for Hermitian \( \A \) the recurrence is **three-term**, and \( \H_k \) is tridiagonal. Prove it, and point out that this is the three-term recurrence of Ch 10's orthogonal polynomials, with the same reason (`thm-tridiagonal-recurrence`).
- `def-ritz-values`, `thm-ritz-interlacing`: the Ritz values are the eigenvalues of \( \Q_k^{*}\A\Q_k \), they interlace those of \( \A \) by Ch 16's `cor-interlacing-general-submatrix`, and each Ritz pair has residual \( |h_{k+1,k}| \) times a coordinate — so Ch 19 §06's bounds apply verbatim. **This is where the chapter's theory pays off.**
- Convergence: state the classical Chebyshev-based estimate for the extreme Ritz values and prove the clean special case; flag anything not proved.
- Negative result, honestly framed: in the model the Lanczos vectors lose orthogonality once a Ritz value converges, and converged eigenvalues reappear as duplicates. Describe the mechanism, name it, and say plainly that the analysis is outside the chapter.

## 07-conjugate-gradient.md — Steepest descent and conjugate gradients
- Setting: \( \A \succ 0 \), the energy norm \( \norm{\x}_{\A} \), and the quadratic \( \phi(\x) = \tfrac12\x^{*}\A\x - \b^{*}\x \), whose minimizer solves \( \A\x = \b \). Prove that.
- `thm-steepest-descent`: convergence with factor \( (\kappa-1)/(\kappa+1) \) per step in the energy norm. Prove it (the Kantorovich inequality is the classical route; if that is too long, prove the two-eigenvalue case and state the general bound, flagged).
- `def-conjugate-gradient`, `thm-cg-optimality` (**major**): the \( k \)-th iterate minimizes the energy-norm error over \( \x^{(0)} + \cK_k(\A, \r^{(0)}) \), and the residuals are orthogonal while the directions are \( \A \)-orthogonal. Prove it.
- `cor-cg-finite-termination`: exact arithmetic gives the answer in at most \( n \) steps, and in at most \( d \) steps when \( \A \) has \( d \) distinct eigenvalues.
- `def-chebyshev-polynomials`, `lem-chebyshev-extremal`: \( T_k \) defined by the recurrence, bounded by 1 on \( [-1,1] \), and growing like \( \tfrac12(z + \sqrt{z^2-1})^k \) outside. **Prove both with algebra only** — the substitution \( x = \tfrac12(z + z^{-1}) \) needs no trigonometry, which the book does not have.
- `thm-cg-convergence`: the error after \( k \) steps is at most \( 2\bigl((\sqrt\kappa-1)/(\sqrt\kappa+1)\bigr)^{k} \) times the initial error, in the energy norm. Prove it from the optimality and the Chebyshev bound. **The square root is the point of the section.**
- Chebyshev iteration as the polynomial method that does not need inner products; preconditioning in one paragraph, honestly labelled as a subject rather than a theorem.

## 08-lax-pairs-and-the-toda-flow.md — Lax pairs and the Toda flow
- `def-lax-pair`: \( \dot{\L} = [\B, \L] = \B\L - \L\B \).
- `thm-lax-isospectral` (**major**): the eigenvalues of \( \L(t) \) are constant. Prove it: the solution of \( \dot{\U} = \B\U \), \( \U(0) = \I \) satisfies \( \L(t) = \U(t)\L(0)\U(t)^{-1} \), by differentiating \( \U^{-1}\L\U \) with Ch 15 §06's product and inverse rules. State the existence of \( \U \) honestly — for constant \( \B \) it is \( e^{t\B} \) (Ch 9 §09); for time-dependent \( \B \) it is an ODE the book does not solve, so **either restrict the theorem to the case where the matrix flow is given, or prove it for the Toda flow directly**.
- `def-toda-flow`: \( \L \) tridiagonal symmetric, \( \B \) its skew part; write the equations for the entries and verify the isospectral property by hand for \( 2 \times 2 \).
- `thm-toda-qr-connection`: the QR algorithm is the time-one map of the Toda flow, in the precise sense the drafter can prove — at minimum, that the flow through \( e^{t\L(0)} = \Q(t)\R(t) \) gives \( \L(t) = \Q(t)^{*}\L(0)\Q(t) \), and that \( t = 1 \) is one unshifted QR step. **Verify this statement before writing it**; if only the continuous statement closes, say so and give the discrete one as a remark.
- Aftermath: an algorithm and a differential equation are the same object seen at different resolutions. This is the chapter's one piece of pure mathematics.

## 09-vibrations.md — Vibrations
- The model: masses and springs, \( \M\ddot{\x} = -\K\x \) with \( \M \succ 0 \), \( \K \succeq 0 \) symmetric. Derive the matrices for a chain of three masses.
- `thm-normal-modes` (**major**): the substitution \( \x = e^{i\omega t}\v \) turns the system into the generalized eigenproblem \( \K\v = \omega^2\M\v \); by Ch 12 §04 there is an \( \M \)-orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, and every solution is a superposition of normal modes. Prove it, citing `thm-simultaneous-diagonalization` and `thm-generalized-eigenvalues-real`.
- Worked example, exact: three equal masses and four equal springs, frequencies \( \sqrt{2 - \sqrt2}, \sqrt2, \sqrt{2+\sqrt2} \) up to scaling. Verify.
- `cor-rayleigh-frequency-bounds`: the Rayleigh quotient bounds the lowest frequency, and Ch 16's min–max gives all of them; a hand estimate of the lowest mode with a guessed shape, compared with the true value.
- Physical readings: stiffening raises every frequency (`cor-loewner-eigenvalue-monotone`), adding a mass lowers them, and interlacing says what happens when a mass is pinned (Ch 16 §04).

## 10-principal-components.md — Principal components
- The data matrix \( \X \in M_{n \times N} \): \( N \) points in \( \nR^n \), centered by subtracting the mean column.
- `thm-best-fitting-affine-subspace` (**major**): among all \( k \)-dimensional subspaces, the one minimizing the sum of squared distances from the points is spanned by the top \( k \) left singular vectors of the centered \( \X \), and the minimum is \( \sum_{i>k}\sigma_i^2 \). Prove it from Ch 16 §09 or Ch 12 §10, and note the affine version (the best affine subspace passes through the mean — prove that too).
- `def-sample-covariance`, `prp-covariance-and-svd`: \( \S = \tfrac1{N}\X\X^{*} \) and its eigenvalues are \( \sigma_i^2/N \); the principal directions are the left singular vectors. **Never form \( \X\X^{*} \)**: §03's squared condition number is the reason, and this pays Ch 12 §10's "which is never by forming \( \A^{*}\A \)".
- The finite-population reading: with the \( N \) columns equally weighted, expectation is an average, variance is a quadratic form in \( \S \), and "variance explained" \( \sum_{i\le k}\sigma_i^2/\sum_i\sigma_i^2 \) is a theorem, not a convention. Prove it. **Then say plainly what is not proved**: that a sample tells you about a larger population is statistics, which this book does not develop.
- Worked example with small integer data, computed exactly.
- Warning: principal components are not "the important directions" — they depend on the units of each coordinate, and rescaling a coordinate changes them. Give the example.

## 11-graph-laplacians.md — Graph Laplacians
- `def-graph-laplacian` \( \L = \D - \A = \N\N\tp \) with \( \N \) the incidence matrix of Ch 2 §07. **Pays Ch 2 §07's "returns in Chapter 23".**
- `prp-laplacian-basic`: symmetric, positive semidefinite, \( \L\1 = \0 \), and \( \x^{*}\L\x = \sum_{\text{edges}}(x_u - x_v)^2 \).
- `thm-laplacian-kernel`: the multiplicity of the eigenvalue \( 0 \) is the number of connected components; the kernel is spanned by the indicator vectors. Cite Ch 2 §07's exercise for the incidence version but **prove it here**.
- `def-fiedler-value`: \( \lambda_2 \), the algebraic connectivity; `prp-fiedler-positive-iff-connected`; the Courant–Fischer reading of \( \lambda_2 \) as a minimum over vectors orthogonal to \( \1 \), and its use for partitioning, with a worked example. Monotonicity: adding an edge does not decrease any eigenvalue (Ch 16).
- `lem-incidence-minors-unimodular`: every square submatrix of \( \N \) has determinant \( 0 \) or \( \pm1 \), and for a set of \( m-1 \) edges the minor is \( \pm1 \) exactly when those edges form a spanning tree. **Prove it** (expand along a vertex of degree one for a tree; a cycle gives a dependent set).
- `thm-matrix-tree` (**major**): for a connected graph, \( \det \L_0 \) equals the number of spanning trees, where \( \L_0 \) deletes one row and column. **Pays Ch 6 §07's "which Chapter 23 completes".** Route: \( \L_0 = \N_0\N_0\tp \), then `thm-cauchy-binet`, then the lemma. **Chapter 6's pieces are in an exercise, so prove everything here and credit the exercise.**
- Worked example: the four-vertex graph of Chapter 6's exercise, counted both ways.

## 12-the-fast-fourier-transform.md — The fast Fourier transform
- Recall Ch 11 §09: the matrix \( \F_n \), the diagonalization of circulants, and the sentence promising this section.
- `thm-fft-factorization` (**major**): for \( n = 2m \), \( \F_n \) factors as a permutation, two copies of \( \F_m \) and a diagonal twiddle factor — write the factorization as a block identity and prove it by computing an entry.
- `thm-fft-cost`: the resulting recursion costs \( O(n\log n) \) operations; solve the recurrence \( C(2m) = 2C(m) + O(m) \) exactly for \( n = 2^p \). **Pays Ch 11 §09's "the cost accounting that makes 'quickly' precise".**
- `cor-fast-convolution`: convolution in \( O(n\log n) \) via Ch 11's `thm-convolution-theorem`; multiplying two polynomials of degree \( n \) in the same time.
- `cor-fast-circulant-solve`: solving a circulant system in \( O(n\log n) \).
- Worked example: \( n = 4 \) and \( n = 8 \) written out, checked against Ch 11's `exm-fourier-matrix-4`.

## 13-fast-matrix-multiplication.md — Fast matrix multiplication
- The question: is \( n^3 \) necessary? `def-bilinear-complexity`, informally but precisely enough to state the exponent.
- `thm-strassen` (**major**): two \( 2 \times 2 \) matrices can be multiplied with **seven** multiplications instead of eight; write the seven products and verify the four blocks. **Verify symbolically.**
- `thm-strassen-cost`: recursion gives \( O(n^{\log_2 7}) = O(n^{2.808\ldots}) \); solve the recurrence.
- The exponent \( \omega \): define it, state that \( 2 \le \omega < 2.373 \) is known and that the best exponent is open, with credit and clearly flagged as not proved here.
- Stability, the negative result: Strassen's algorithm satisfies a **norm** backward bound but not an entrywise one; give the standard example where a small entry is computed with large relative error. Verify.
- Practicality in one honest paragraph: the crossover, and why the classical algorithm remains the one in use for small sizes.
- **Closing "Summary and transfer"** for the chapter: conditioning is a property of a problem, stability of an algorithm, and the two multiply; every eigenvalue algorithm iterates because no finite one can exist; the Krylov space is the right home for iteration; a fast algorithm is a factorization of a matrix of operations.
