# Blueprint: Chapter 13 — Bilinear and Quadratic Forms

**Directory:** `src/ch13-forms/` (new). **Chapter title:** "Bilinear and Quadratic Forms". **Part:** opens "Part IV · Forms and Multilinear Algebra" (set the `part` key on this chapter in `config/config.json`).

**Purpose.** An inner product is one bilinear form among many, singled out by symmetry and positivity. Drop positivity and the subject widens: forms that vanish on non-zero vectors, forms that are skew rather than symmetric, forms over fields where \( 2 \) is not invertible. The organizing relation is no longer similarity but **congruence** \( \A \mapsto \P^{*}\A\P \), and the chapter classifies forms under it. It builds

- bilinear forms, their matrices, and congruence as change of basis;
- symmetric, alternating and Hermitian forms, and why characteristic 2 splits the first two apart;
- diagonalization of a symmetric form by completing the square;
- Sylvester's law of inertia, the classification over ℝ, and the classification over ℂ;
- positive definiteness revisited, and the second-derivative test;
- isotropic vectors, hyperbolic planes, and Witt's decomposition;
- Witt's extension and cancellation theorems;
- symplectic forms and Darboux bases;
- the classical groups the forms define;
- Minkowski space, as the form that physics uses;
- a first look at Clifford algebras.

**Allowed prerequisites.** Chapters 0–12. **The field is a general \( F \) here, not only ℝ or ℂ** — that is the point of the chapter, and it is the first time since Chapter 9 that the field is genuinely open. Say so in the index page, and attach the characteristic hypothesis to every statement that needs it (`def-characteristic`, `thm-characteristic-prime`, Ch 0 §07). Chapters 10–12 are used for the ℝ and ℂ specializations, never as hypotheses of the general theory.

**Notation** (NOTATION.md has most): `\beta \colon V \times V \to F` for a bilinear form, \( q(\v) = \beta(\v, \v) \) for the quadratic form, `\mtx{\beta}{\sB}{}` for the Gram matrix of a form. Add rows for congruence (\( \A \simeq \B \) or whatever you choose — pick one symbol and record it), the radical, the signature, and the symplectic group. **Do not reuse `\sim` (similarity) or `\approx` (equivalence over F[x], Ch 9 §07).**

**Signature moves:**
- **Polarize.** Recover the form from the quadratic function — and notice the recovery divides by 2, which is exactly where characteristic 2 breaks.
- **Complete the square.** The whole diagonalization theorem is this, done by induction.
- **Split off a hyperbolic plane.** The structural move for indefinite forms, as "peel off an eigenvector" was for operators.
- **Count signs, not values.** Inertia says the signs are the invariant; the numbers themselves are not.
- **Congruence, not similarity.** Ask at every theorem which relation is in play.

## 01-bilinear-forms.md — Bilinear forms and their matrices

- **Definition** `def-bilinear-form` (**major**): \( \beta \colon V \times V \to F \) linear in each argument with the other fixed. Recall Chapter 6 §02's multilinearity rather than redefining it.
- **Examples:** the dot product; \( \x\tp\A\y \) for any \( \A \); \( \int fg \); the determinant of a 2×2 as a form on \( F^2 \times F^2 \); the evaluation pairing \( V^{*} \times V \to F \) (recall Ch 4); a non-example (\( \norm{\x}\norm{\y} \), not additive).
- **The matrix of a form** `def-form-matrix`: \( (\mtx{\beta}{\sB}{})_{ij} = \beta(\v_i, \v_j) \), and `thm-form-matrix-determines`: \( \beta(\u, \v) = [\u]_{\sB}\tp \mtx{\beta}{\sB}{} [\v]_{\sB} \); the correspondence between forms and matrices is a bijection, and it is linear.
- **Warning:** the matrix of a *form* and the matrix of a *map* transform differently. Same symbol \( \A \), two rules. This warning is the reason the chapter exists; make it vivid.
- **The dual-map view** (brief): \( \beta \) induces \( V \to V^{*} \), \( \v \mapsto \beta(\cdot, \v) \); the form is **non-degenerate** when this is an isomorphism — `def-nondegenerate`, `def-radical`. Cite Chapter 4 for \( V^{*} \).
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 02-congruence.md — Congruence

- **Theorem** `thm-change-of-basis-form`: changing basis replaces \( \A \) by \( \P\tp\A\P \) with \( \P \) the change-of-coordinates matrix. **Definition** `def-congruent` (**major**).
- **Congruence is an equivalence relation** `prp-congruence-equivalence`; what it preserves: rank (`thm-congruence-preserves-rank`), symmetry, alternation, non-degeneracy, and the *class* of the determinant in \( F^{\times}/(F^{\times})^2 \) — `thm-congruence-determinant-class`, the discriminant.
- **What it does not preserve:** eigenvalues. Give two congruent matrices with different spectra, and say plainly that this is why Chapters 11 and 12 are not this chapter. **This discharges Chapter 12 §01's promise** — read it and answer in its words.
- **When congruence and similarity coincide:** \( \P \) orthogonal makes \( \P\tp = \P^{-1} \). That is why the real spectral theorem doubles as a congruence statement (recall `cor-spectral-real-matrix`).
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 03-symmetric-alternating-hermitian.md — Three kinds of form

- **Definitions** `def-symmetric-form`, `def-alternating-form` (\( \beta(\v, \v) = 0 \) for all v), `def-skew-symmetric-form` (\( \beta(\u,\v) = -\beta(\v,\u) \)), `def-hermitian-form`.
- **Theorem** `thm-alternating-vs-skew`: alternating \( \Rightarrow \) skew always; the converse holds **iff** \( \operatorname{char} F \ne 2 \). Give the \( \nF_2 \) counterexample explicitly. **This is where Chapter 0's promise is paid** — cite `def-characteristic` and say so.
- **Theorem** `thm-symmetric-alternating-decomposition`: in characteristic \( \ne 2 \), every bilinear form is uniquely symmetric plus alternating, via \( \tfrac12(\beta \pm \beta^{\top}) \) — and the formula visibly fails in characteristic 2.
- **Polarization** `thm-polarization-forms`: in characteristic \( \ne 2 \), a symmetric form is determined by its quadratic form \( q \); **definition** `def-quadratic-form` (**major**), agreeing with the prose definition Chapter 11 §05 used. Over ℂ and over ℝ compare with the polarization identities of Chapter 10 §07 (cite `lem-polarization-identities`).
- **Warning:** in characteristic 2 the quadratic form is *not* determined by, and does not determine, the bilinear form; quadratic forms there need their own definition, which this book does not give.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 04-diagonalizing-symmetric-forms.md — Completing the square

- **Theorem** `thm-symmetric-form-diagonalizable` (**major**): over any field of characteristic \( \ne 2 \), every symmetric form has an orthogonal basis; every symmetric matrix is congruent to a diagonal one. Proof by induction, completing the square, with the degenerate case (\( q \equiv 0 \) on a subspace) handled honestly — that case is where a careless proof fails, so give it its own step.
- **Worked:** diagonalize a 3×3 symmetric integer matrix by symmetric row-and-column operations, showing the operations in pairs. **This is the algorithm a reader can run**, so make it a worked example and not a remark.
- **Over ℂ** `cor-complex-symmetric-classification`: every symmetric matrix is congruent to \( \I_r \oplus \0 \); rank is the complete invariant.
- **Over a general field:** the diagonal entries may be scaled by squares, so the classification depends on \( F^{\times}/(F^{\times})^2 \); state this and do \( \nQ \) or \( \nF_p \) in an example or exercise.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 05-sylvesters-law-of-inertia.md — Inertia

- **Theorem** `thm-sylvester-inertia` (**major**): over ℝ, the numbers \( (n_+, n_-, n_0) \) are the same for every diagonalization. **Definition** `def-signature`. Proof: the subspace-dimension argument (a positive subspace and a non-positive subspace must intersect trivially) — the "contradiction plus a dimension count" move.
- **Corollary** `cor-real-symmetric-classification`: complete classification of real symmetric matrices up to congruence by \( (n_+, n_-) \); the canonical form \( \I_{n_+} \oplus -\I_{n_-} \oplus \0 \).
- **Reconciling with the spectral theorem:** the signs of the eigenvalues give the inertia, because an orthogonal change of basis is simultaneously a similarity and a congruence — **this pays off Chapter 11 §05's promise**, and Chapter 12 §04's. Read both and answer in their words.
- **Warning:** congruent matrices have the same inertia but unrelated eigenvalues; the eigenvalues move, only the signs stay. Give numbers.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C, including computing signatures by symmetric elimination.

## 06-definiteness-and-second-derivatives.md — Definiteness revisited, and a use

Short: ≈2,500–3,000 words.

- **Recall from Chapter 12** (do not reprove): `thm-pd-characterizations`, Sylvester's criterion. Restate them in the language of forms, and add the indefinite and semidefinite cases as the signature reading — `thm-definiteness-by-signature`.
- **The second-derivative test** `thm-second-derivative-test`: for a twice continuously differentiable \( f \colon \nR^n \to \nR \) with a critical point, the Hessian's signature decides a local minimum, a local maximum or a saddle, with the degenerate case undecided. **State the analysis input explicitly** (Taylor's theorem with a second-order remainder) and cite nothing the book has not proved; the linear algebra is the signature, and that is what to prove. Give the classic \( f(x,y) = x^2 - y^2 \) saddle and a degenerate example where the test fails.
- **Warning:** the test reads the signature, not the determinant alone; \( \det > 0 \) is compatible with a maximum.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 07-isotropic-vectors-and-hyperbolic-planes.md — Where the form vanishes

- **Definitions** `def-isotropic-vector`, `def-totally-isotropic-subspace`, `def-hyperbolic-plane`.
- **Lemma** `lem-isotropic-extends-to-hyperbolic`: in a non-degenerate form, every non-zero isotropic vector sits in a hyperbolic plane — the structural workhorse. Prove it.
- **Theorem** `thm-witt-decomposition`: a non-degenerate space splits as an orthogonal sum of hyperbolic planes and an anisotropic part; the number of planes (the **Witt index**) is an invariant — `def-witt-index`.
- **Over ℝ:** the Witt index is \( \min(n_+, n_-) \); over ℂ, everything of dimension ≥ 2 is isotropic. Work both out.
- **Warning:** an isotropic vector is not a zero vector; a form can vanish on a whole subspace without being degenerate. This is the intuition an inner-product-trained reader must give up, so spend a paragraph on it.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 08-witts-theorems.md — Witt extension and cancellation

- **Theorem** `thm-witt-extension` (**major**): an isometry between subspaces of a non-degenerate space extends to an isometry of the whole space. Proof by induction on dimension, splitting the anisotropic and isotropic cases; characteristic \( \ne 2 \) throughout. This is the chapter's long theorem — give it a Step roadmap and Claims, per STYLE.
- **Corollary** `cor-witt-cancellation`: \( U \perp W \cong U \perp W' \) with \( W \cong W' \) implies… state it correctly (cancellation of the *complement*, not of an arbitrary summand) and prove it from extension.
- **Consequence:** the Witt index and the anisotropic part are well defined up to isometry, finishing §07.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 09-symplectic-forms.md — Alternating forms and Darboux bases

- **Theorem** `thm-symplectic-standard-form` (**major**): a non-degenerate alternating form exists only in even dimension, and has a basis in which its matrix is \( \begin{psmallmatrix} \0 & \I_m \\ -\I_m & \0\end{psmallmatrix} \) — a **Darboux** or symplectic basis. Proof: repeatedly split off hyperbolic pairs. **No characteristic hypothesis is needed here** — say so, it is the one place in the chapter where char 2 causes no trouble.
- **Corollary** `cor-alternating-even-rank`: every alternating matrix has even rank, and `cor-alternating-determinant-square` the determinant is a square (the Pfaffian, mentioned by name only, with the 2×2 and 4×4 cases computed).
- **Contrast** with the symmetric case, in a table: symmetric forms have many congruence classes, alternating forms have exactly one per rank.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 10-the-classical-groups.md — Groups defined by forms

- **Definition** `def-isometry-group-of-form`: the matrices preserving a form, \( \P\tp\A\P = \A \). Specialize to \( \Orth(n) \), \( \Orth(p, q) \), \( \Unit(n) \), \( \Symp(2m) \) — recall Ch 10 §07's definitions, do not restate them.
- **Theorem** `thm-classical-groups-are-groups`, and the determinant constraints: \( \pm1 \) for orthogonal, modulus 1 for unitary, and **exactly** \( +1 \) for symplectic (prove the symplectic case for 2×2 and state the general one, or prove it via the Pfaffian if §09 set that up).
- **Dimension count** (informal but honest): how many independent conditions \( \P\tp\A\P = \A \) imposes, for each family. Flag that "dimension" here means the count of free parameters and that the topology belongs elsewhere.
- **Warning:** \( \Orth(p, q) \) is not compact for \( p, q \ge 1 \), unlike \( \Orth(n) \) — recorded, not proved, and flagged as such.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 11-minkowski-space.md — The form physics uses

Short: ≈2,500–3,000 words.

- **Setting:** \( \nR^{1,3} \) with signature \( (1, 3) \) (state the convention and note the other one exists). Light cone, timelike, spacelike, null vectors — as isotropy from §07, not as new notions.
- **Theorem** `thm-lorentz-boost-form`: the 2-dimensional Lorentz group is the hyperbolic rotations \( \begin{psmallmatrix}\cosh t & \sinh t\\ \sinh t & \cosh t\end{psmallmatrix} \), the exact analogue of Chapter 11's \( \Orth(2) \) classification. Prove it, and put the two canonical forms side by side.
- **The reversed triangle inequality** `thm-reversed-triangle-inequality` for timelike vectors, with the twin-paradox reading in one honest sentence.
- **Warning:** every geometric intuition trained on positive definiteness fails here; the "unit sphere" is a hyperboloid. One TikZ picture of the light cone in \( \nR^{1,1} \).
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 12-clifford-algebras.md — A first look at Clifford algebras

Short: ≈2,500–3,000 words. Introduction only; the chapter ends, it does not develop a theory.

- **Hook:** the complex numbers and the quaternions both come from insisting that a quadratic form become a square.
- **Definition** `def-clifford-algebra` by generators and relations: \( \v^2 = q(\v)\cdot 1 \), with the universal property stated and the construction as a quotient of the tensor algebra **deferred to Chapter 14** in prose (that chapter builds tensor algebras).
- **Examples, in full:** \( \operatorname{Cl}(0) = F \), \( \operatorname{Cl}_{0,1}(\nR) = \nC \), \( \operatorname{Cl}_{0,2}(\nR) = \nH \), and the \( 2^n \) dimension count with its basis of products \( \e_{i_1}\cdots\e_{i_k} \).
- **Theorem** `thm-clifford-dimension`: \( \dim \operatorname{Cl}(q) = 2^n \), stated with the proof sketched honestly, or proved for \( n \le 2 \) with the general case attributed to Chapter 14. Decide and be explicit.
- **Closing:** what Clifford algebras are for (spinors, rotations as conjugation), in one paragraph, promising nothing the book will not deliver.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.
