Chapter numbers come from directory names, so Chapter 23 keeps its number.

**Purpose.** Every chapter so far has studied a vector space: a set with an origin. Geometry does not have an origin, and the two classical repairs are this chapter's subject.
- **Affine geometry** forgets the origin but keeps parallelism: points, differences of points, flats, and the maps that send lines to lines.
- **Projective geometry** adds the points at infinity, so that any two distinct lines in a plane meet, every quadric is one of a very short list, and duality becomes an exact symmetry of the axioms.

The chapter is the book's one sustained piece of synthetic geometry, and everything in it is proved with linear algebra: a projective space is the set of lines through the origin of a vector space, and a projective transformation is an invertible linear map read on those lines.

**The chapter owes almost nothing and is owed almost nothing.** The single debt is Ch 14 §08's hint at **Plücker coordinates** (recorded in STATUS as "hinted, chapter not named in the text"). **§10 pays it.** Note that Ch 14 §10's `exr-grassmann-and-clifford-c1` is the Plücker relation and is an **exercise**, so §10 must prove what it needs and may only credit it.

## 01-affine-spaces.md — Affine spaces and affine maps
- Hook: the set of solutions of \( \A\x = \b \) with \( \b \ne \0 \) is not a subspace but has all the geometry of one (Ch 2's `thm-general-solution-structure`). Name what it is.
- `def-affine-space`: a set \( \cA \) with a free transitive action of a vector space \( V \) — equivalently, a map \( \cA \times \cA \to V \), \( (P,Q) \mapsto \overrightarrow{PQ} \), with Chasles's relation \( \overrightarrow{PQ} + \overrightarrow{QR} = \overrightarrow{PR} \) and, for each \( P \), a bijection \( Q \mapsto \overrightarrow{PQ} \). Give both forms and prove they agree.
- Examples: \( V \) itself; a coset \( \x_0 + U \) (cite `prp-affine-hull-coset`); the solution set of a linear system; the affine plane over \( \nF_2 \) (four points) as the degenerate case worth seeing.
- `prp-choice-of-origin`: choosing \( O \in \cA \) identifies \( \cA \) with \( V \); the identification is canonical only after the choice. **This is the chapter's first named move.**
- `def-affine-combination` and `prp-affine-combination-well-defined`: \( \sum t_iP_i \) with \( \sum t_i = 1 \) is independent of the origin, and no other coefficient sum is. Prove both halves. Connect to Ch 17 §01's `def-affine-hull`.
- `def-affine-map`, `thm-affine-map-is-linear-plus-translation`: \( f \) is affine iff \( f(P) = T(\overrightarrow{OP}) + f(O) \) for a unique linear \( T \), the **linear part**. Composition, invertibility, the affine group; matrix form as an \( (n+1) \times (n+1) \) block \( \begin{psmallmatrix} \A & \b \\ \0 & 1\end{psmallmatrix} \), which the rest of the chapter uses.
- Warning: an affine map is linear exactly when it fixes the origin; the non-example.

## 02-affine-subspaces.md — Flats, independence, and coordinates
- `def-flat` (affine subspace), dimension, `prp-flat-is-coset`, parallelism as an equivalence relation on flats of equal dimension.
- `thm-flat-intersection-and-join`: two flats either meet or are contained in parallel hyperplanes; the dimension formula \( \dim(A \vee B) = \dim A + \dim B - \dim(A \cap B) \) when they meet, and \( \dim A + \dim B - \dim(\vec A \cap \vec B) + 1 \) when they do not. **Prove both cases**; the second is where affine geometry differs from linear.
- `def-affine-frame`, `thm-barycentric-coordinates`: \( n+1 \) affinely independent points give every point unique coefficients summing to 1. Build on Ch 17 §01's `def-affine-independence` and `lem-affine-dependence`; do not redefine them.
- `thm-affine-map-determined-by-frame`: an affine map is determined by its values on an affine frame, and any assignment extends. The negative result: \( n \) points are not enough.
- A flat as a solution set: `thm-flat-is-solution-set` (every flat is \( \{\x : \A\x = \b\} \)), via Ch 2 and Ch 4's annihilator.
- Worked examples: the line through two points in barycentric form; the medians of a triangle meet at the centroid \( \tfrac13(P_1+P_2+P_3) \) — prove it, it is one line in barycentric coordinates and it is the best advertisement in the chapter.

## 03-polyhedra-and-linear-programming.md — Convex polyhedra, geometrically
- **A reading of Chapter 17, not a reproof.** Open by saying so.
- `def-polyhedron-face` of a polyhedron (note: `def-face` is taken — Ch 17 §08 uses it) (via a supporting hyperplane, Ch 17 §03), `prp-face-is-polyhedron`, vertices = extreme points = basic feasible solutions (cite `thm-vertices-basic-feasible`, `thm-minkowski-extreme`), edges, and the face lattice of a simple example (a cube, a simplex) computed in full.
- `thm-polytope-is-hull-of-vertices` as the bounded case of Minkowski–Weyl (cite; prove only the bounded specialization if it is short).
- The geometry of linear programming: the objective's level sets are parallel hyperplanes, the optimum is at a vertex (`cor-linear-max-at-extreme`), and moving from vertex to vertex along edges is what an algorithm would do — **no algorithm here**, and say that Chapter 23 is where algorithms live.
- Duality read geometrically: cite `thm-weak-duality` and `thm-strong-duality` and interpret the dual variables as the supporting hyperplane's coefficients.
- Negative: an unbounded polyhedron with no vertex (a slab), and one with vertices but no optimum.

## 04-euclidean-motions.md — Euclidean motions
- `def-euclidean-motion` (distance-preserving bijection of \( \nR^n \)).
- `thm-motion-is-affine` (**major**): every distance-preserving map of \( \nR^n \) is affine, with orthogonal linear part. Route: translate so \( f(\0) = \0 \), then show \( f \) preserves inner products by polarization (Ch 10 `lem-polarization-identities`), then that a map preserving inner products is linear (compute \( \norm{f(\x+\y) - f(\x) - f(\y)}^2 \) and get zero). **Surjectivity is not needed** — say so, since it is usually assumed.
- `def-direct-and-opposite`, by \( \det \) of the linear part.
- `thm-plane-motions` (**major**): a motion of \( \nR^2 \) is a translation, a rotation about a point, a reflection in a line, or a glide reflection — and no two of these coincide except in the stated degenerate cases. Prove by cases on the linear part (Ch 11 §06) and whether a fixed point exists.
- `thm-space-motions`: in \( \nR^3 \), a direct motion is a rotation about an axis, a translation, or a **screw motion** (Chasles); an opposite motion is a reflection, a glide, or a rotatory reflection. Prove what closes; if the full classification is long, prove the direct case and state the opposite case with credit, **flagged**.
- The group: motions form a group, translations are normal, and the quotient is the orthogonal group — one short proposition using Ch 3 §09's quotient machinery.
- Worked example: compose two reflections in intersecting lines and get a rotation through twice the angle; compose two in parallel lines and get a translation.

## 05-quadrics.md — Affine and Euclidean quadrics
- `def-quadric`: the zero set of \( q(\x) + \l(\x) + c \) with \( q \) a non-zero quadratic form (Ch 13), \( \l \) linear. **char \( F \ne 2 \) throughout; say so.**
- The \( (n+1) \times (n+1) \) symmetric matrix of a quadric, and how an affine change of coordinates acts on it (congruence by the affine block matrix of §01).
- `thm-affine-classification-of-quadrics` (**major**): over \( \nR \), completing the square reduces every quadric to one of a short normal-form list, determined by the rank and inertia of \( q \) (Ch 13's `thm-sylvester-inertia`) together with whether a centre exists and whether the constant can be cleared. **Give the complete list for \( n = 2 \)** — ellipse, hyperbola, parabola, the pairs of lines, the point, the empty set — and say what changes for \( n = 3 \).
- `thm-euclidean-classification`: over \( \nR \) with distances, the spectral theorem (Ch 11 §05) rotates the axes and the normal form keeps the eigenvalues, which are the reciprocal squared semi-axes. The principal axes are eigenvectors — connect to Ch 16's Rayleigh quotient, and to Chapter 23 §09's vibrating chain if that reads naturally.
- `def-centre` and `prp-centre-exists-iff`: a centre exists iff \( \l \) lies in the image of the matrix of \( q \); the parabola is exactly the centreless case.
- Worked example, exact: classify \( 5x^2 + 4xy + 5y^2 + \dots \) by both routes, giving the same curve.
- Warning: the rank of the big matrix alone does not classify; inertia does. Give two quadrics with the same rank and different shape.

## 06-projective-space.md — Projective space
- Hook: two lines in the affine plane that do not meet (write them down). Every repair that keeps linearity adds points.
- `def-projective-space` \( \nP(V) = (V \setminus \{\0\})/\sim \) with \( \x \sim \lambda\x \); dimension \( \dim V - 1 \); `def-homogeneous-coordinates`; `prp-projective-points-are-lines`.
- `thm-affine-chart`: \( \nP^n(F) = \{x_0 \ne 0\} \sqcup \nP^{n-1}(F) \), the first piece in bijection with \( \nA^n \) and the second the **hyperplane at infinity**. Prove it, and do the \( n = 2 \) case in full: the projective plane is the affine plane plus a line of directions.
- `def-projective-subspace`, and `thm-projective-dimension-formula`: \( \dim(S \vee T) + \dim(S \cap T) = \dim S + \dim T \), **with no exceptional case** — the point of the whole construction. Prove it from Ch 1's dimension formula, and contrast it explicitly with §02's two-case affine version.
- `cor-two-lines-meet`: distinct lines in a projective plane meet in exactly one point. Then return to the hook and resolve it: the two parallel lines meet at infinity, with coordinates computed.
- Counting: \( \lvert\nP^n(\nF_q)\rvert = (q^{n+1}-1)/(q-1) \); the Fano plane as \( \nP^2(\nF_2) \), drawn and verified.
- Warning: \( [\x] \) is a point, \( \x \) is a vector, and a "coordinate" of a projective point is meaningless alone — only ratios are.

## 07-projective-transformations.md — Projective transformations
- `def-projective-transformation`: the map \( [\x] \mapsto [\T\x] \) induced by \( \T \in \GL(V) \); well defined; `thm-pgl`: two maps induce the same projectivity iff they differ by a non-zero scalar, so the group is \( \PGL(V) = \GL(V)/F^{\times} \) (Ch 3 §09's quotient machinery, or a direct argument).
- `def-general-position` (\( n+2 \) points in \( \nP^n \), every \( n+1 \) of them independent).
- `thm-fundamental-theorem-of-projective-geometry` (**major**, the linear-algebra form): given two lists of \( n+2 \) points in general position there is exactly one projectivity carrying the first to the second. Prove existence and uniqueness. **Negative result: \( n+1 \) points do not suffice** — exhibit two different projectivities agreeing on them.
- `def-cross-ratio` for four collinear points, `thm-cross-ratio-invariant` (**major**): projectivities preserve it, and it is a complete invariant of four points on a line up to projectivity. Prove both. Give the formula in an affine chart and compute an example exactly.
- Perspectivities: projection from a point maps one line to another and preserves the cross-ratio; the classical "why railway sleepers look evenly spaced and are not" reading, in one paragraph.
- Relation to §01: affine maps are exactly the projectivities fixing the hyperplane at infinity **as a set**. Prove it; it is three lines in block form and it ties the chapter together.

## 08-projective-duality.md — Duality
- `def-dual-projective-space` \( \nP(V^{*}) \); a point of it is a hyperplane of \( \nP(V) \) (Ch 4's annihilator).
- `thm-duality-correspondence`: \( S \mapsto S^{\circ} \) is an inclusion-reversing bijection between projective subspaces of \( \nP(V) \) and of \( \nP(V^{*}) \), with \( \dim S^{\circ} = n - 1 - \dim S \). Prove from `thm-annihilator-properties` and `thm-dimension-annihilator`, and say clearly how the projective dimension shift works.
- `thm-duality-principle`: every theorem of projective geometry has a dual, obtained by exchanging "point" with "hyperplane" and "join" with "meet". State it as a working principle about statements in the incidence language, prove the translation for the statements this book makes, and **be honest that a general metatheorem needs a formal language the book has not set up**.
- Worked dualization: the dual of "two distinct points lie on a unique line" is "two distinct lines meet in a unique point", in \( \nP^2 \).
- `thm-double-dual-projective`: \( \nP(V^{**}) = \nP(V) \) canonically (Ch 4's `thm-double-dual-isomorphism`), so dualizing twice returns the original statement.
- Pole and polar with respect to a conic — **state here and prove in §10**, or defer entirely to §10; the drafter chooses and says which.

## 09-desargues-and-pappus.md — Desargues and Pappus
- `thm-desargues` (**major**): two triangles in a projective plane are perspective from a point iff they are perspective from a line. Route: the clean proof lifts the plane into \( \nP^3 \) and uses that two planes meet in a line; the purely \( 2 \)-dimensional proof chooses representative vectors and compares two expressions for the same point. **Give one complete proof and say which route it is**; verify every representative-scaling step, as that is where such proofs go wrong.
- `thm-pappus` (**major**): six points alternating on two lines give three collinear intersection points. Prove it in coordinates, choosing a frame by §07's fundamental theorem so that the computation is short, and **check the degenerate configurations are excluded by hypothesis**.
- The converse direction of Desargues by duality (§08) — one line, if it really is one line.
- **Negative result, stated with credit and not proved:** Pappus's theorem is equivalent to commutativity of the underlying division ring, and Desargues holds over every division ring but fails in some non-Desarguesian planes. The book has not built division rings or synthetic planes, so this is a pointer, flagged, with nothing depending on it.
- A worked instance of each theorem with exact coordinates.

## 10-conics-and-plucker.md — Projective quadrics, conics, and lines in space
- `def-projective-quadric`: the zero set of a non-zero quadratic form on \( V \), well defined on \( \nP(V) \) because \( q(\lambda\x) = \lambda^2q(\x) \). **char ≠ 2.**
- `thm-projective-classification`: over \( \nC \) (or any algebraically closed field of char ≠ 2) a quadric is classified by the **rank** alone; over \( \nR \) by rank and inertia up to sign (Ch 13's Sylvester). Give the \( \nP^2(\nR) \) list: empty, a point, a line, two lines, a smooth conic.
- `thm-five-points-determine-a-conic`: five points in general position lie on exactly one conic. Prove it by counting: the quadratic forms in three variables are a 6-dimensional space, each point imposes one linear condition, and general position makes the conditions independent — **prove the independence, do not assume it**.
- `thm-conic-parametrization`: a smooth conic over any field with a rational point is in bijection with \( \nP^1 \), by projection from a point of the conic. Prove it and compute the rational parametrization of \( x^2 + y^2 = z^2 \), recovering the Pythagorean triples — the chapter's best payoff.
- Pole and polar, tangency, and the dual conic, with §08's duality.
- **Plücker coordinates — pays Ch 14 §08's hint.** A line in \( \nP^3 \) is a 2-dimensional subspace of \( F^4 \), hence a point of \( \nP(\Lambda^2 F^4) = \nP^5 \) by Ch 14's `thm-wedge-coordinates-are-minors`; the six coordinates satisfy the **Plücker relation** \( p_{01}p_{23} - p_{02}p_{13} + p_{03}p_{12} = 0 \), and conversely every point of that quadric is a line. **Prove both directions** — Ch 14 §10's `exr-grassmann-and-clifford-c1` is an exercise, so credit it and prove what you use. The Klein quadric is a quadric in \( \nP^5 \), which ties the section's two halves together.
- **Closing "Summary and transfer"** for the chapter.
