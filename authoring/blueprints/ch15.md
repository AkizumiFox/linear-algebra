# Blueprint: Chapter 15 — Norms and Matrix Analysis

**Directory:** `src/ch15-norms/` (new). **Chapter title:** "Norms and Matrix Analysis". **Part:** opens "Part V · Matrix Analysis" (set the `part` key on this chapter in `config/config.json`).

**Purpose.** The book has been promising this chapter since Chapter 9. Convergence of matrix powers, the matrix exponential, the stability of a least-squares solve, "nearly defective" — every one of these was stated qualitatively, with the quantitative version deferred. The tool that was missing is a norm. This chapter builds

- vector norms, and the fact that in finite dimension they are all equivalent;
- dual norms;
- matrix norms, and the operator norm induced by a vector norm;
- the spectral radius, `ρ(A) ≤ ‖A‖`, and Gelfand's formula;
- Neumann series and perturbation of the identity;
- the matrix exponential with bounds instead of exact solutions, and the derivatives of `det` and of the inverse;
- continuity of the eigenvalues;
- condition numbers, and what they predict about solving `Ax = b`.

**Allowed prerequisites.** Chapters 0–14. **The field is ℝ or ℂ** throughout (norms need absolute values); say so once. This chapter imports more analysis than any before it, and the book's practice is now established: name each imported fact where it is used, in the manner of Chapter 13 §06's Taylor assumption and Chapter 10's Abel–Ruffini. **Keep the imports to a short, explicit list** — compactness of the closed unit ball in a finite-dimensional space, continuity of a continuous function on a compact set attaining its bounds, completeness of ℝ and ℂ, and the convergence of a monotone bounded sequence. Anything beyond that list should be flagged as recorded, not proved.

**Signature moves:**
- **Compactness turns "for all unit vectors" into a maximum.** The equivalence of norms and the existence of the operator norm are both this.
- **Submultiplicativity is the whole point of a matrix norm.** Ask of every candidate whether `‖AB‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖B‖`, and show a natural norm that fails it.
- **Bound the tail of a series.** Neumann, the exponential, Gelfand.
- **Perturb and compare.** The condition number is the constant in the comparison.

## 01-vector-norms.md — Norms

- **Definition** `def-norm` (**major**): positivity, homogeneity, triangle inequality on a real or complex space. Note it drops the inner product's extra structure and keeps what measurement needs.
- Examples: `p`-norms for `p = 1, 2, ∞` with the unit balls drawn (one TikZ figure, the three balls in ℝ²); the induced norm of Chapter 10; a weighted norm; a norm on a function space; a non-example failing homogeneity.
- **Which norms come from an inner product** `thm-parallelogram-characterization`: exactly those satisfying the parallelogram law. **This pays off Chapter 10 §01's promise** — read it, and cite `exr-inner-products-c1`, which already proves the ℝ² case, rather than redoing it. Prove the general statement or state precisely what Chapter 10 left and what you add.
- **Warning:** `‖·‖₁` and `‖·‖_∞` are not inner-product norms; check the parallelogram law on two explicit vectors.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 02-equivalence-of-norms.md — All norms agree

- **Theorem** `thm-norm-equivalence` (**major**): on a finite-dimensional real or complex space any two norms are equivalent, with constants. Proof: compare an arbitrary norm with `‖·‖₂` on the unit sphere, using compactness — name compactness and the extreme value theorem as the imported facts, at the point of use.
- **Corollaries** `cor-convergence-norm-independent`, `cor-closed-bounded-compact`: convergence, boundedness and continuity do not depend on which norm is used, so Chapter 9 §10's entrywise convergence is *the* convergence. Connect to `def-entrywise-convergence` explicitly.
- **Warning:** the constants depend on the dimension, and the theorem is false in infinite dimensions. Give the sequence-space example, flagged as outside the book's scope.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 03-matrix-and-operator-norms.md — Measuring a matrix

- **Definition** `def-matrix-norm` (submultiplicative), `def-operator-norm` (**major**): `‖A‖ = max{‖Ax‖ : ‖x‖ = 1}`, existence by compactness.
- **Theorem** `thm-operator-norm-properties`: it is a norm, it is submultiplicative, `‖Ax‖ ≤ ‖A‖‖x‖`, and `‖I‖ = 1`.
- **Computations** `thm-operator-norm-formulas`: `‖A‖₁` is the largest column sum, `‖A‖_∞` the largest row sum, and **`‖A‖₂ = σ₁(A)`** — the last one **pays off the promises from Chapter 12 §08 and §12 and Chapter 11 §11**; read them and answer in their words, citing `def-singular-values`.
- **Warning:** the Frobenius norm is submultiplicative but is *not* an operator norm (`‖I‖_F = √n`); and the entrywise max is not submultiplicative at all. Give both witnesses. This is where a reader's intuition usually breaks.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 04-spectral-radius.md — The spectral radius

- **Definition** `def-spectral-radius`, stated as the one Chapter 9 §10 used in prose.
- **Theorem** `thm-spectral-radius-le-norm`: `ρ(A) ≤ ‖A‖` for every matrix norm. **This is the promise Chapter 9 §10 made** — discharge it in its words.
- **Theorem** `thm-norm-close-to-spectral-radius`: for every `ε > 0` there is a norm with `‖A‖ ≤ ρ(A) + ε` (via a Schur triangularization and a diagonal rescaling — cite Ch 11 §01). The two theorems together say `ρ` is the infimum of the norms.
- **Gelfand's formula** `thm-gelfand`: `ρ(A) = lim ‖A^k‖^{1/k}`. Prove it; both inequalities follow from the two theorems above plus Ch 9 §10's convergence criterion.
- **Corollary** `cor-powers-converge-iff-rho-lt-one`, recovering `thm-matrix-powers-converge-to-zero` with a quantitative rate — say what is new: not the criterion, but the bound.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 05-neumann-series.md — Inverting a perturbation of the identity

Short: ≈2,500–3,000 words.

- **Theorem** `thm-neumann-series`: `‖A‖ < 1` implies `I − A` invertible with `(I − A)^{-1} = ∑ A^k` and `‖(I − A)^{-1}‖ ≤ 1/(1 − ‖A‖)`. Compare with Chapter 9 §10's `thm-neumann-series-spectral`, which has the sharp spectral hypothesis but no bound; say which is stronger in which direction.
- **Corollary** `cor-invertible-matrices-open`: the invertible matrices form an open set, and inversion is continuous — a fact several earlier chapters used informally.
- **Perturbation of a solve** `thm-perturbed-inverse-bound`, the estimate §08 turns into the condition number.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 06-matrix-exponential-and-calculus.md — The exponential, with bounds; and derivatives

- **Bounds** `thm-exponential-norm-bound`: `‖e^A‖ ≤ e^{‖A‖}`, and `‖e^A − I − A‖ ≤ ‖A‖²e^{‖A‖}/2`. **This discharges Chapter 9 §09's promise** to return with quantitative bounds; read it and answer in its words. Cite `def-matrix-exponential` and do not redevelop the series.
- **Stability** `thm-exponential-decay`: if every eigenvalue has negative real part then `e^{tA} → 0`, with an explicit rate from §04's ε-norm. This is what Chapter 11 §10's Lyapunov remark pointed at — connect them.
- **Derivatives** `thm-derivative-of-det` (Jacobi's formula, `d/dt det(A(t)) = tr(adj(A) A'(t))`, and `(det)'(I) = tr`) and `thm-derivative-of-inverse` (`d/dt A^{-1} = −A^{-1}A'A^{-1}`). Both need the differentiability of the entries as the imported fact; state it.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 07-continuity-of-eigenvalues.md — Eigenvalues move continuously

- **Theorem** `thm-roots-depend-continuously`, stated carefully: the *multiset* of roots of a monic polynomial depends continuously on the coefficients; there is no continuous choice of individual eigenvalues. Give the standard `√t` example showing why the multiset formulation is forced. Proving this properly needs complex analysis, so **state it as an imported fact with the counterexample proved**, in the book's established manner.
- **Corollary** `cor-eigenvalues-continuous`, and `cor-diagonalizable-dense-again`: Chapter 11 §01's `cor-schur-normal-matrix-nearby` becomes the statement that diagonalizable matrices are dense, now with a metric. **Chapter 11 said Chapter 15 would make that argument routine** — deliver it.
- **Warning:** continuity is not Lipschitz; the eigenvalues of a Jordan block move like `ε^{1/k}`. Compute the 2×2 and `k×k` cases; this is the quantitative form of Chapter 11 §11's defect discussion, so cite `def-departure-from-normality`.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.

## 08-condition-numbers.md — How wrong can the answer be?

Closes the chapter.

- **Definition** `def-condition-number`: `κ(A) = ‖A‖‖A^{-1}‖`, and `κ₂(A) = σ₁/σ_n` — cite Chapter 12 §08.
- **Theorem** `thm-relative-error-bound`: the relative error in the solution of `Ax = b` is at most `κ(A)` times the relative perturbation, for perturbations of `b` and (to first order) of `A`. Prove both, using §05.
- **Worked:** a small ill-conditioned system where the bound is nearly attained, computed exactly.
- **Least squares and QR** `thm-normal-equations-squares-conditioning`: `κ₂(A*A) = κ₂(A)²`. **This is the reason Chapter 10 §08 gave for preferring QR and deferred** — read that passage and discharge it precisely, saying what is proved here and what belongs to Chapter 23's error analysis.
- **Warning:** a large condition number does not mean the computed answer is wrong, and a small one does not make an algorithm stable; conditioning is a property of the problem, stability of the algorithm. Chapter 23 makes the distinction; state it cleanly here.
- **Closing: what the chapter has paid off**, listing the notes from Chapters 9, 10, 11 and 12 that are now settled.
- **Check. Exercises** A/B/C.
