1.1  Vector Spaces

Why do we bother with a list of eight axioms? To a student first encountering Linear Algebra, this can feel like a bureaucratic exercise in “checking boxes.” However, there is a profound beauty hidden in this formality.

In our preliminary chapter, we saw three very different worlds: the world of geometric arrows (Rn), the world of functions (F[x]), and the world of matrices (Mm×n). On the surface, an arrow is not a polynomial, and a polynomial is not a matrix. But if you squint, they all behave the same: you can add them, and you can scale them.

By defining a Vector Space through these axioms, we are choosing to ignore what the objects are and focus entirely on how they act. If we prove a theorem using only these eight axioms, that theorem becomes a “universal law” that applies to arrows, functions, and matrices all at once. This is the power of abstraction: solve the problem once, and you solve it for every universe that obeys these rules.

1.1.1. Vector Spaces and Vectors

Definition 1.1.1 (Vector Space)

A set V over a field F with two operations: addition + and scalar multiplication ⋅ +:V×V→V,⋅:F×V→V such that satisfy the following axioms for all v1,v2,v3∈V and α,β∈F:

  • (VS1) v1+v2=v2+v1
  • (VS2) (v1+v2)+v3=v1+(v2+v3)
  • (VS3) There exists 0∈V such that v1+0=v1
  • (VS4) There exists v1′∈V such that v1+v1′=0
  • (VS5) α(v1+v2)=αv1+αv2
  • (VS6) (α+β)v1=αv1+βv1
  • (VS7) α(βv1)=(αβ)v1
  • (VS8) 1v1=v1

Remark. Instead of writing (V,+,⋅) is a vector space over F, we usually simplify it to V is a vector space over F, or even just V is a vector space if the context is clear.

Remark. We sometimes will abuse the name and refer to 0=0V∈V as the “zero vector” of the vector space V. Readers should not confuse 0V with the zero scalar 0=0F∈F from the field F.

Example (Examples of Vector Spaces)

The following are classic examples of vector spaces:

  1. The standard example: Fn with the usual operations of + and ⋅ is a vector space over F.

  2. Working with polynomials is common too: F[x] with the usual operations of + and ⋅ is a vector space over F.

  3. Let D be any open interval. Let C(D):={f:D→R:f is continuous}. Then C(D) is a vector space over R. The zero vector of this vector space is given by the zero function (x↦0).

  4. Let F be any field and fix n∈N. Then Mn(F) is a vector space over F.

  5. The set V=R>0 of positive real numbers forms a vector space over F=R under the following operations: for x,y∈V and α∈F, define x⊕y:=xy,α⊙x:=xα=eαlog⁡x. Under these operations, the zero vector is 0=1, and the additive inverse of x is x−1.

You can check if addition and scalar multiplication make sense and follow all axioms of vector spaces.

Example (Q Adjoin 2)

Show that Q(2):={a+b2:a,b∈Q} is a vector space over Q by checking all eight axioms.

Solution. Let v1=a1+b12, v2=a2+b22, v3=a3+b32∈Q(2) and α,β∈Q.

  • (VS1) v1+v2=(a1+a2)+(b1+b2)2=(a2+a1)+(b2+b1)2=v2+v1

  • (VS2) (v1+v2)+v3=(a1+a2+a3)+(b1+b2+b3)2=v1+(v2+v3)

  • (VS3) The element 0=0+02=0∈Q(2) satisfies v1+0=(a1+0)+(b1+0)2=v1

  • (VS4) For v1=a1+b12, define −v1:=(−a1)+(−b1)2∈Q(2). Then v1+(−v1)=(a1−a1)+(b1−b1)2=0

  • (VS5) α(v1+v2)=α(a1+a2)+α(b1+b2)2=(αa1+αa2)+(αb1+αb2)2=αv1+αv2

  • (VS6) (α+β)v1=(α+β)a1+(α+β)b12=(αa1+βa1)+(αb1+βb1)2=αv1+βv1

  • (VS7) α(βv1)=α(βa1+βb12)=αβa1+αβb12=(αβ)v1

  • (VS8) 1⋅v1=1⋅a1+1⋅b12=a1+b12=v1

Thus Q(2) is a vector space over Q.

Definition 1.1.2 (Vectors and Scalars)

Let V be a vector space over F. Then the elements of V are called vectors, and the elements of F are called scalars.

Additionally, 0∈V is called the zero vector, and (v1′) in (VS4) is called the inverse element of v1.

1.1.2. Vector Space Theorems

Theorem 1.1.1 (Left Cancellation Law)

Let V be a vector space over F, let u,v1,v2∈V. If u+v1=u+v2, then v1=v2.

Theorem 1.1.2 (Right Cancellation Law)

Let V be a vector space over F, let u,v1,v2∈V. If v1+u=v2+u, then v1=v2.

Proof. Since we have u+v1=u+v2⟹v1=v2, applying (VS1) to it gives v1+u=v2+u⟹v1=v2.

Let V be a vector space over F. The zero vector 0∈V is unique.
