1.7  Sums of Subspaces

In the previous sections, we studied intersections and unions of subspaces. While the intersection of two subspaces is always a subspace, the union typically is not. This raises a natural question: what is the “right” way to combine two subspaces into a larger one?

The answer is the sum of subspaces. Given two subspaces W1 and W2, their sum W1+W2 consists of all vectors that can be written as w1+w2 for some w1∈W1 and w2∈W2. This construction always yields a subspace—in fact, it is the smallest subspace containing both W1 and W2.

A particularly nice situation occurs when W1 and W2 have only the zero vector in common. In this case, their sum is called a direct sum, and every vector in the sum can be decomposed uniquely into a piece from W1 and a piece from W2. Direct sums are fundamental in decomposing vector spaces into simpler, independent components.

1.7.1. Definition and Properties

Definition 1.7.1 (Sum of Subspaces)

Let W1, W2 be subspaces of a vector space V. Then we define W1+W2:={w1+w2:w1∈W1 and w2∈W2}.

Theorem 1.7.1 (Sum of Subspaces Theorem)

W1+W2 is a subspace of V. Furthermore, this is the smallest subspace containing both W1 and W2.

Proof. To prove that W1+W2 is a subspace of V, we verify the three conditions of the Subspace Test (Theorem 1.2.1):

  • Non-emptiness: As W1, W2 are subspaces, they both contains 0. And since 0=0W1+0W2, therefore W1+W2 is non-empty.

  • Closed under addition: Let a,b∈W1+W2. Then we have {a=w1+w2, for some w1∈W1,w2∈W2b=w1′+w2′, for some w1′∈W1,w2′∈W2 Then a+b=(w1+w1′)⏟∈W1+(w2+w2′)⏟W2∈W1+W2. So W1+W2 is closed under addition.

  • Closed under scalar multiplication: Let a∈W1+W2, and λ∈F. Then we have a=w1+w2 for some w1∈W1,w2∈W2. Then λa=λw1⏟∈W1+λw2⏟∈W2∈W1+W2. So W1+W2 is closed under scalar multiplication.

This concludes that W1+W2 is a subspace.

To prove that W1+W2 is the smallest subspace containing both W1 and W2, we first prove that it actually contains W1 and W2, then we prove that it is the smallest subspace of V containing so.

  • Containment: Since W1 and W2 are subspaces, they both contain the zero vector 0. Since for any w1∈W1, we can write w1=w1+0, by 0∈W2, we have w1∈W1+W2. Thus W1⊆W1+W2. The same logic applies to W2 therefore W2⊆W1+W2.

  • Minimality: Let U be any subspace of V such that W1⊆U and W2⊆U. We want to show that W1+W2⊆U. Take any element W1+W2. By definition v=w1+w2 for some w1∈W1 and w2∈W2. Because W1⊆U, then w1∈U. Similarly, because W2⊆U, then w2∈U. At last, since U is a subspace, it is closed under addition. Therefore w1+w2∈U⟹v∈U. This concludes that W1+W2 is the smallest subspace of V containing W1 and W2.

1.7.2. Dimension of W1+W2

Theorem 1.7.2 (Dimension Formula of Subspace Sum)

Let V be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let W1, W2 subspaces of V. Then dim⁡(W1+W2)=dim⁡(W1)+dim⁡(W2)−dim⁡(W1∩W2).

Proof.

We use the above diagram to build intuition for the proof.

Let n=dim⁡(W1∩W2). Let B={b1,…,bn} be a basis of the intersection W1∩W2.

Since B is a linearly independent subset of both W1 and W2, by the Basis Extension Theorem (Theorem 1.6.5), we can extend it to form bases for both subspaces:

  1. For W1: Extend B with {s1,…,sp} such that B1={b1,…,bn,s1,…,sp} is a basis of W1. Note that dim⁡(W1)=n+p.

  2. For W2: Extend B with {t1,…,tq} such that B2={b1,…,bn,t1,…,tq} is a basis of W2. Note that dim⁡(W2)=n+q.

Claim. The set S={b1,…,bn,s1,…,sp,t1,…,tq} is a basis of W1+W2.

Proof.

  • S spans W1+W2

    Let w∈W1+W2. By definition, w=w1+w2 for some w1∈W1,w2∈W2. Since B1 spans W1 and B2 spans W2, both w1 and w2 can be written as linear combinations of vectors in S. Thus, their sum w is also in span(S).

  • S is Linearly Independent

    ∑i=1nαibi+∑j=1pβjsj+∑k=1qγktk=0
    (1.7.1)

    Rearrange the equation to isolate the terms involving tk: v:=∑i=1nαibi+∑j=1pβjsj=−∑k=1qγktk

    Observe the vector v. From the LHS, we have v∈W1. From the RHS, we have v∈W2. Therefore, v∈W1∩W2.

    Since v∈W1∩W2, it can be written uniquely as a linear combination of the basis B of the intersection: v=∑i=1nδibi

    Equating this with the RHS expression of v: ∑i=1nδibi=−∑k=1qγktk⟹∑i=1nδibi+∑k=1qγktk=0.

    The set B2={b1,…,bn,t1,…,tq} is a basis of W2 and is therefore linearly independent. This forces all coefficients to be zero. Specifically, all γk=0.

    Substitute γk=0 back into Eq. 1.7.1: ∑i=1nαibi+∑j=1pβjsj=0

    Since B1={b1,…,bn,s1,…,sp} is a basis of W1, it is linearly independent. This forces all αi=0 and all βj=0.

Since all α,β,γ are zero, S is linearly independent.

Use dimension counting, since S is a basis for W1+W2: dim⁡(W1+W2)=|S|=n+p+q=(n+p)+(n+q)−n=dim⁡(W1)+dim⁡(W2)−dim⁡(W1∩W2).

Remark. Counter-example for 3 Subspaces

It is a coincidence that this formula resembles the “Inclusion-Exclusion Principle” from set theory. This intuition fails for three or more subspaces. The formula: dim⁡(W1+W2+W3)≠∑dim⁡(Wi)−∑dim⁡(Wi∩Wj)+dim⁡(W1∩W2∩W3)

Counter-example: Consider three distinct lines through the origin in R2 (e.g., x-axis, y-axis, and y=x).

  • LHS: The sum of three lines fills the plane, so dimension is 2.
  • RHS: 1+1+1−(0+0+0)+0=3.

Since 2≠3, the formula is false.

1.7.3. Direct Sum of Two Subspaces

Definition 1.7.2 (Direct Sum of Two Subspaces)

Let W1, W2 be subspaces of a vector space V. If W1∩W2={0}, then we write W1+W2 as W1⊕W2 and call it the direct sum of W1 and W2.

Example

Consider the vector space V=Mn(C), and two of its subspaces:

  • W1={A∈Mn(C):A⊤=A} (the subspace of symmetric matrices);

  • W2={A∈Mn(C):A⊤=−A} (the subspace of skew-symmetric matrices).

We can derive that V=W1⊕W2.

Proof. We first show that V=W1+W2. That is, any square matrix is a sum of a symmetric matrix and a skew-symmetric matrix. Let A∈Mn(C), notice that we have the identity: A=12(A+A⊤)+12(A−A⊤) where the first term is symmetric and the second term is skew-symmetric. Therefore, any square matrix is a sum of a symmetric matrix and a skew-symmetric matrix.

To establish that W1∩W2={O}, let A∈W1∩W2. By definition, this implies A∈W1 and A∈W2. Consequently, the identities A=A⊤ and A=−A⊤ must hold simultaneously. Substituting the former into the latter yields A=−A. This implies 2A=O, and thus A=O.
