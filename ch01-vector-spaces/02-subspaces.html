1.2  Subspaces

Some vector spaces might be “too big” and be unwieldy to deal with. Therefore, it is often convenient to look at “smaller vector spaces” inside a given vector space. These are called subspaces.

1.2.1. Definition and the Subspace Test

Definition 1.2.1 (Subspace)

Let V be a vector space over F. A subset W of V is called a subspace of V, if W is also a vector space over F with addition and scalar multiplication inherited from V.

Remark. Let (V,+,⋅) be a vector space over F, and W⊆V. If (W,+,⋅) is also a vector space over F then W is a subspace of V.

Example (Trivial Subspaces)

Given a vector space V, then

  • V is a subspace of V itself;
  • {0} is a subspace of V (also known as the “zero subspace”);
  • ∅ is not a subspace of V.
Theorem 1.2.1 (Subspace Test)

Let V be a vector space over F and W⊆V. Then W is a subspace of V if and only if the following are satisfied:

  • W is non-empty;
  • W is closed under addition: w1+w2∈W for all w1,w2∈W;
  • W is closed under scalar multiplication: αw∈W for all α∈F and w∈W.

Proof. (⇒) If W is a subspace, then W is a vector space, so it contains the zero vector (hence non-empty) and is closed under addition and scalar multiplication by definition.

(⇐) Assume the three conditions hold. Since W⊆V, the operations on W are inherited from V, so associativity, commutativity, and distributivity are automatically satisfied. It remains to verify the existence of identity elements and inverses.

  • Zero vector: Since W≠∅, there exists w∈W. By closure under scalar multiplication, 0⋅w=0∈W.
  • Additive inverse: For any w∈W, closure under scalar multiplication gives (−1)⋅w=−w∈W.

Thus W is a vector space under the inherited operations, i.e., a subspace of V.

Remark. Note that from the proof, the first condition of the Subspace Test can be replaced by the requirement that W contains the zero vector of V.

Remark. In practice, to test whether a given subset of a vector space is a subspace, we use Theorem 1.2.1 instead of checking all eight vector space axioms from scratch, which is much less tedious. Moreover, some introductory textbooks use Theorem 1.2.1 as the definition of a subspace. While this is logically equivalent, it can be somewhat less intuitive than defining a subspace as a subset that is itself a vector space.

1.2.2. Examples of Subspaces

Example (Line as Subspace)

W={(x,y)∈R2:x+2y=0} is a subspace of R2. Indeed:

  • Zero vector: (0,0)∈W since 0+2(0)=0.
  • Addition: If (x1,y1),(x2,y2)∈W, then (x1+x2)+2(y1+y2)=(x1+2y1)+(x2+2y2)=0+0=0, so their sum is in W.
  • Scalar multiplication: If (x,y)∈W and c∈R, then (cx)+2(cy)=c(x+2y)=c(0)=0, so c(x,y)∈W.
Example (Translated Line is Not a Subspace)

W={(x,y)∈R2:x+2y=3} is not a subspace of R2. This is because it does not contain the zero vector: 0+2(0)=0≠3, so (0,0)∉W.

Example (Polynomials of Bounded Degree)

Let F[x] be the vector space of all polynomials over a field F. For a fixed n∈N, the set F[x]≤n of polynomials of degree at most n is a subspace of F[x].

  • Zero vector: The zero polynomial has degree −∞, so 0∈F[x]≤n.
  • Addition: If deg⁡p≤n and deg⁡q≤n, then deg⁡(p+q)≤max(deg⁡p,deg⁡q)≤n.
  • Scalar multiplication: If deg⁡p≤n and c∈F, then deg⁡(cp)≤deg⁡p≤n.
Example (Polynomials of Exact Degree)

The set F[x]=n of polynomials of degree exactly n is not a subspace of F[x].

  • It does not contain the zero vector (since deg⁡0=−∞≠n).
  • It is not closed under addition. For example, if p(x)=xn+x and q(x)=−xn, both have degree n, but p(x)+q(x)=x, which has degree 1≠n (assuming n>1).
Example (Differentiable Functions)

Let C(R)={f:R→R∣f is continuous} be the set of all continuous functions on R. We consider the set of differentiable functions: D(R)={f:R→R∣f is differentiable} To verify that D(R) is a subspace of C(R):

  • Subset: Since differentiability implies continuity, we have D(R)⊆C(R).
  • Zero vector: The zero function f(x)=0 is differentiable, so 0∈D(R).
  • Closure under addition: If f,g∈D(R), then f+g is differentiable with (f+g)′=f′+g′, so f+g∈D(R).
  • Closure under scalar multiplication: If f∈D(R) and c∈R, then cf is differentiable with (cf)′=cf′, so cf∈D(R).

Thus, D(R) is a subspace of C(R).

Example (Symmetric Matrices)

The set of all symmetric matrices in Mn(F), denoted by Sn(F)={A∈Mn(F):A=A⊤}, is a subspace of Mn(F).

  • Zero vector: The zero matrix O is symmetric since O⊤=O, so O∈Sn(F).
  • Addition: If A,B∈Sn(F), then (A+B)⊤=A⊤+B⊤=A+B, so A+B∈Sn(F).
  • Scalar multiplication: If A∈Sn(F) and c∈F, then (cA)⊤=cA⊤=cA, so cA∈Sn(F).
Example (Trace-free Matrices)

The set of all trace-free matrices in Mn(F), denoted by W={A∈Mn(F):tr(A)=0}, is a subspace of Mn(F).

  • Zero vector: tr(O)=0+0+⋯+0=0, so O∈W.
  • Addition: If A,B∈W, then tr(A+B)=tr(A)+tr(B)=0+0=0, so A+B∈W.
  • Scalar multiplication: If A∈W and c∈F, then tr(cA)=ctr(A)=c(0)=0, so cA∈W.

1.2.3. Intersections and Unions

Theorem 1.2.2 (Intersection of Subspaces)

Let W1 and W2 be subspaces of a vector space V. Then the intersection W1∩W2 is also a subspace of V.

Proof. We use the Subspace Test (Theorem 1.2.1) on W=W1∩W2:

  • Zero vector: Since W1 and W2 are subspaces, 0∈W1 and 0∈W2. Thus 0∈W1∩W2.
  • Closure under addition: Let w,z∈W1∩W2. Then w,z∈W1 and w,z∈W2. Since W1 and W2 are subspaces, they are closed under addition, so w+z∈W1 and w+z∈W2. Hence w+z∈W1∩W2.
  • Closure under scalar multiplication: Let w∈W1∩W2 and c∈F. Then w∈W1 and w∈W2. Since W1 and W2 are closed under scalar multiplication, cw∈W1 and cw∈W2. Hence cw∈W1∩W2.

Since all three conditions of the Subspace Test are satisfied, W1∩W2 is a subspace of V.

Remark. Unlike the intersection, the union of two subspaces is not necessarily a subspace. For W1∪W2 to be a subspace, one subspace must be contained within the other (i.e., W1⊆W2 or W2⊆W1).

Example (Union of Axes)

Consider the vector space V=R2. Let W1 be the x-axis and W2 be the y-axis: W1={(x,0):x∈R},W2={(0,y):y∈R}. Both W1 and W2 are subspaces of R2. However, their union W1∪W2 is not a subspace because it is not closed under addition.

Indeed, (1,0)∈W1⊆W1∪W2 and (0,1)∈W2⊆W1∪W2, but their sum: (1,0)+(0,1)=(1,1) is not in W1∪W2 since (1,1) is neither on the x-axis nor the y-axis.

Proposition 1.2.3 (Union of Subspaces)

Let W1,W2 be subspaces of V. Then W1∪W2 is a subspace of V if and only if W1⊆W2 or W2⊆W1.

Proof. (⇐) If W1⊆W2, then W1∪W2=W2, which is a subspace. Similarly, if W2⊆W1, the union is W1, which is also a subspace.

(⇒) We prove the contrapositive: if neither is contained in the other, then the union is not a subspace. Assume W1⊈W2 and W2⊈W1.

  • Since W1⊈W2, there exists a vector u∈W1 such that u∉W2.
  • Since W2⊈W1, there exists a vector v∈W2 such that v∉W1.

Consider the sum w=u+v. We show that w∉W1∪W2:

  • If w∈W1, then v=w−u. Since w∈W1 and u∈W1, their difference v must be in W1 (by closure). But we chose v∉W1, which is a contradiction.
  • If w∈W2, then u=w−v. Since w∈W2 and v∈W2, their difference u must be in W2. But we chose u∉W2, which is a contradiction.

Therefore, u+v is in neither W1 nor W2, so it is not in W1∪W2. Thus, the union is not closed under addition and is therefore not a subspace.
